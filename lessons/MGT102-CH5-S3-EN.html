Chapter 5 — Perception and Unfair Treatment

MGT 102 · Chapter 5 · Section 3 of 4: Perception and Unfair Treatment
Objective LO5-4: Discuss how perception and the use of schemas can result in unfair treatment.

This section is “why does unfairness happen even when everyone wants fairness?”

In the previous lesson you learned two ethical principles that guide a manager in diversity: distributive justice = sharing out pay, promotions, and resources by contribution, not by personal traits a person cannot control, and procedural justice = fair procedures that decide how outcomes are shared out. Almost everyone agrees with them. So why are diverse employees sometimes treated unfairly? The book's answer in this section (LO5-4) is two words: inaccurate perceptions.

The whole section is one chain — memorize it in order from now: perception → schema → gender schemas → stereotype → bias → implicit bias (and two of its sources, social status and salience) → overt discrimination. Everything before the last one happens without intent, even in managers with good intentions; only the last one is on purpose. The exam gives you a scenario and asks which term fits it, so take the English definitions word for word from the EXAM BOX and memorize the key word in each one. And there is no Figure or Table from the book in this section — it is all definitions and examples.

Perception
A manager does not see reality itself — he sees his interpretation of it
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Perception = the process in which people select, organize, and interpret what their senses pick up, to give the world around them meaning and order. It is inherently subjective: if it is accurate the decision is good, and if it is wrong the decision is wrong.
🧠
Three managers look at the same employee through three colored glasses; each one swears he saw “the truth”.
EXPLANATION — a manager's decision is an “interpretation”, not objective truth

Most people think that a manager's decisions and actions are the result of an objective determination of the issue and the situation. The book says no: a manager's interpretation of any situation — or even of another person — is only an interpretation (precisely that—an interpretation).

And where does interpretation differ the most? Diversity. Because each person's interpretation of a situation, and their response to it, is affected by their own background. The book counts seven:

EXAM BOX

Most people tend to think that the decisions managers make in organizations and the actions they take are the result of objective determination of the issues involved and the surrounding situation. However, each manager’s interpretation of a situation or even of another person is precisely that—an interpretation.

Perception is the process through which people select, organize, and interpret sensory input—what they see, hear, touch, smell, and taste—to give meaning and order to the world around them. All decisions and actions of managers are based on their subjective perceptions. When these perceptions are relatively accurate—close to the true nature of what is actually being perceived—good decisions are likely to be made and appropriate actions taken.

When managers’ perceptions are relatively inaccurate, managers are likely to make bad decisions and take inappropriate actions that hurt organizational effectiveness. Bad decisions concerning diversity for reasons of age, ethnicity, or sexual orientation include (1) not hiring qualified people, (2) failing to promote top-performing employees, who subsequently may take their skills to competing organizations, and (3) promoting poorly performing managers because they have the same “diversity profile” as the manager or managers making the decision.

TRAP
  • The three verbs: select, organize, and interpret. A choice with collect, store, and recall or observe, judge, and decide = wrong. And the senses are all five, not only sight.
  • Perception is subjective. A choice that says a manager's decisions are “an objective determination of the situation” = this is what “most people tend to think”, and the book rejects it.
  • The bad decisions are three. The trickiest is the third: promoting a weak manager because he looks like the decision maker — so unfairness is not only denying someone something, it can be rewarding someone who does not deserve it. And note: the second one includes the employee going to competitors.
  • Do not swap the two lists: McDonald's, Pizza Hut, Wendy's = noticed accurately; Kentucky Fried Chicken, Jack-in-the-Box, Burger King = were late. And corn pies = Thailand, seafood burgers = Hong Kong.

EXPLANATION — group two: past experiences and schemas

The perceptions that managers — and all organizational members — have of one another are also affected by their past experiences and acquired knowledge about people, events, and situations, and this information is organized into preexisting schemas.

TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Schema
المخطّط الذهني		 An abstract knowledge structure that is stored in memory and makes possible the interpretation and organization of information about a person, an event, or a situation. an abstract knowledge structure stored in memory that lets you interpret and organize information about a person, event, or situation.

How a schema works — the chain in order:

  1. You build a schema for a certain kind of person or event.
  2. Any new person or situation that is related to it activates it, and information is processed in a way that is consistent with what is stored in it.
  3. So people perceive others through the expectations or preconceived notions in their schemas — and these expectations come from past experience and knowledge.
  4. People pay attention to information that fits the schema, and ignore or discount information that does not fit.
  5. So schemas are reinforced and strengthened over time, because what you paid attention to seems to confirm them.
  6. The result: schemas are resistant to change.

But be careful: this does not mean they never change — if that were the case, people could never adapt to new conditions or learn from their mistakes. The truth is that they are slow to change, and to change them a person must face a considerable amount of contradictory information.

And this difference is exactly discussion question 4 at the end of the chapter: Discuss the ways in which schemas can be functional and dysfunctional.

EXAM BOX

Several managers’ perceptions of the same person, event, or situation are likely to differ because managers differ in personality, values, attitudes, and moods (see Chapter 3).

Schemas are abstract knowledge structures stored in memory that allow people to organize and interpret information about a person, an event, or a situation. Once a person develops a schema for a kind of person or event, any newly encountered person or situation that is related to the schema activates it, and information is processed in ways consistent with the information stored in the schema.

People tend to pay attention to information that is consistent with their schemas and to ignore or discount inconsistent information. Thus, schemas tend to be reinforced and strengthened over time because the information attended to is seen as confirming the schemas. This also results in schemas being resistant to change. This does not mean schemas never change; if that were the case, people could never adapt to changing conditions and learn from their mistakes. Rather, it suggests that schemas are slow to change and that for people to change their schemas, they need to encounter a considerable amount of contradictory information.

Schemas that accurately depict the true nature of a person or situation are functional because they help people make sense of the world around them. People typically confront so much information that it is not possible to make sense of it without relying on schemas. Schemas are dysfunctional when they are inaccurate because they cause managers and all members of an organization to perceive people and situations inaccurately and assume certain things that are not necessarily true.

TRAP
  • The factors are four: personality, values, attitudes, and moods. A choice with intelligence, education, or income as one of the four = not one of them.
  • Do not flip the examples: high openness = learning opportunity, low = threat. Satisfied and committed = the transfer is an opportunity; dissatisfied and uncommitted = the transfer is a demotion.
  • Schemas are slow to change, not impossible to change. Any choice with schemas never change = wrong.
  • Not all schemas are bad: accurate ones are functional, and inaccurate ones are dysfunctional. A choice that says “a manager must get rid of all schemas” = the opposite of the book.
  • People pay attention to what fits and ignore what does not fit — not the other way round. That is the reason schemas get stronger over time.

EXPLANATION — perception as a cause of unfair treatment: stereotypes

The book starts with a question: almost everyone agrees that distributive justice and procedural justice are desirable goals, so why are diverse employees sometimes treated unfairly? One important overarching reason: inaccurate perceptions. To the extent that managers rely on wrong information — such as gender schemas — to guide their perceptions of each other, unfair treatment is likely to happen.

TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Stereotype
الصورة النمطية		 Simplistic and often inaccurate belief about the typical characteristics of particular groups of people. a simplistic and often inaccurate belief about the typical characteristics of particular groups of people.
  • The relationship: “Gender schemas are a kind of stereotype” — stereotype is the wider term, and gender schemas are one kind of it.
  • What are they based on? Usually a visible characteristic: age, gender, or race.
  • What do they do to a manager? He assumes erroneously that a person has a whole host of characteristics just because the person happens to be an Asian woman, a white man, or a lesbian, for example.
  • The book's examples: African American men are often stereotyped as good athletes, and Hispanic women (women of Latin American origin) as subservient. Obviously there is no reason to assume that every one of them is like that — but the stereotype makes people assume it.
  • A wrong management decision (it comes as a scenario): a manager who accepts stereotypes might not promote a highly capable Hispanic woman into a management position, because he thinks she will not be assertive enough to supervise others.
  • Mothers: a recent study says stereotypes might hamper the progress of mothers when they seek to advance in positions that men have traditionally held, especially as the pandemic recedes; because people — based on gender stereotypes — see mothers as less competent in the skills needed for these positions.
  • People with disabilities: they too might be treated unfairly because of stereotypes. Even though the ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act, lesson 1) requires organizations to give them accommodations, their employment rate is low: around 19% of them are employed compared to 64%. (The book writes “compared to 64% of people with disabilities” — clearly a printing mistake, and it means people without disabilities. Memorize the two numbers as they are.)

Biases, Implicit Bias, Social Status, and Salience
Perception errors that work behind your back
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Bias = a systematic tendency to use information about others in a way that produces wrong perceptions — and usually you do not know it. Implicit bias = bias that works unconsciously and involuntarily, and two of its sources are social status (you judge by rank) and salience (you pay too much attention to the one who is different).

In a meeting the general manager speaks and you nod before listening; the only different person at the table has every eye on him.

EXPLANATION — bias and implicit bias
  • The wrong perceptions that lead to unfair treatment can also come from biases. And because of the way they work, people often are unaware that their perceptions of others are wrong.
  • Implicit bias works by itself: activated involuntarily and without awareness — without will and without awareness.
  • Research says it is pervasive in daily life, such as employment, education, and criminal justice, among others.
  • Why does it matter to a manager? Because it can lead to unfair treatment of diverse employees simply because they are different from the managers who perceive them, evaluate them, and make decisions about their future in the organization.
  • A practical fix from the book: some companies use a cross-functional interview team as part of hiring. The team meets after the interview to discuss the candidate, so that any member's biases can be explored and vetted.
  • The book admits: managers — especially top managers — are likely to be white men. Even if they endorse distributive and procedural justice, they may unintentionally fall into the trap of seeing other white men more positively than women and others in underrepresented groups. The fix: (1) being aware of this bias, and (2) using objective information about the employee's capabilities and performance as much as possible in decisions about job assignments, pay raises, promotions, and other outcomes.
EXPLANATION — social status and salience: two sources of implicit bias

Social status: the tendency to see a high-status person more positively than a low-status person. The high-status person is seen as smarter, more believable, capable, knowledgeable, and responsible — even in the absence of objective information about either person.

The book's example (it comes as a scenario): at a company holiday party you meet two white men in their late thirties; one is on the top management team and the other is a supervisor in the mailroom. From this information alone, you might assume that the top manager is smarter, more capable, more responsible, and even more interesting. And because women and others in underrepresented groups have traditionally had lower social status than white men, the social status effect may lead some people to see them less positively.

Salience: the book asks you: have you ever stood out in a crowd? Maybe you were the only man in a group of women, or you came dressed formally while everyone else was in jeans. The salience effect puts extra attention on the person who stands out from the group mold. And the salient person feels that all eyes are watching him — and the book says this feeling is not far from the mark.

The book's example: a manager with six white employees and one Hispanic employee may — inadvertently — pay more attention to the Hispanic employee in meetings. And salient people get two things:

  1. They are seen as primarily responsible for outcomes and operations.
  2. They are evaluated more extremely in either direction: if the employee does a good job, he gets excessive praise, and if he misses a deadline, he is excessively chastised.

And part of being a good manager is being aware of these tendencies and actively working against them.

EXAM BOX

Inaccurate perceptions leading to unfair treatment of diverse members of an organization also can be due to biases. Biases are systematic tendencies to use information about others in ways that result in inaccurate perceptions. Because of the way biases operate, people often are unaware that their perceptions of others are inaccurate.

Implicit bias is defined as the attitudes or stereotypes that affect our understanding, actions, and decisions in an unconscious manner. These tendencies are activated involuntarily and without awareness.

Social status—a person’s real or perceived position in a society or an organization—can also be a source of implicit bias. This is the tendency to perceive individuals with high social status more positively than we perceive those with low social status.

Salience (that is, conspicuousness) is another source of implicit bias. The salience effect is the tendency to focus attention on those who are conspicuously different from others in a group; the salience effect results in extra attention being focused on a person who stands out from the group mold. Individuals who are salient are often perceived to be primarily responsible for outcomes and operations and are evaluated more extremely in either a positive or a negative direction.

TRAP
  • Stereotype = a belief about a group. Bias = a systematic tendency in using information. Implicit bias = bias that is unconscious and involuntary. A scenario with “he did not know” or “without awareness” → implicit bias.
  • Status → social status; being different and standing out → salience. Do not mix them up, and both are sources of implicit bias — not types of overt discrimination.
  • Salience gives an extreme evaluation in both directions (too much praise and too much blame) — not only negative. This is the most famous trap in the section.
  • Social status = a real or perceived position, and the positive impression happens without any objective information.
  • The interview team meets after the interview (not before) so that biases are explored and vetted. And the second fix: awareness + objective information about capabilities and performance.
  • The areas where implicit bias is pervasive that the book names: employment, education, and criminal justice.

When the unfairness is on purpose, not by mistake
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Everything before was without intent. Overt discrimination = knowingly and willingly denying diverse individuals opportunities and outcomes in the organization — and it is both unethical and illegal.

  • The number 11% comes twice in the chapter: 11% of the top earners in the S&P 500 are women (lesson 1), and 11% of managerial positions in Japan are held by women (here). Watch which one they ask about. And Japan's law is from 1985.
  • The numbers: Michigan = $11 million; T.J. Simers = $15 million (age + health, 2013, 22 years); Ford = four employees and retirement benefits.
  • Lawsuits now increasingly come from older workers — not only from women and minorities as in the past.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 2, go back to the schema chain in the second concept; if you miss question 4, go back to the social status against salience table in the fourth concept.

1. According to the text, which of the following is NOT one of the bad decisions concerning diversity that can result from managers' inaccurate perceptions?
  1. Not hiring qualified people
  2. Failing to promote top-performing employees, who may then take their skills to competing organizations
  3. Using a cross-functional interview team that meets after the interview to discuss the candidate
  4. Promoting poorly performing managers because they have the same "diversity profile" as the managers making the decision
Answer

C. A cross-functional interview team is a fix that some companies use to reduce implicit bias, not a bad decision. The book's bad decisions are three, and they are exactly A, B, and D. The trap in D: some students think it is “not unfair” because it is a promotion, not a denial, but the book lists it as the third decision — promoting a weak manager because he looks like the decision maker. And the question uses NOT, so do not rush and pick the first decision you know.

2. Omar believes that young employees are lazy. He quickly notices every time a young employee arrives late, but he barely notices when young employees stay late to finish a project. Over time, his belief becomes even stronger. This best illustrates that:
  1. Schemas never change once they are formed
  2. People pay attention to information consistent with their schemas and ignore or discount inconsistent information
  3. Schemas are functional whenever they are strongly held
  4. Omar is engaging in overt discrimination
Answer

B. Omar pays attention to the information that fits his schema (arriving late) and ignores the information that does not fit (staying late on the project), so the schema is reinforced and strengthened over time — the book's chain exactly. The trap in A: the book says schemas are slow to change and need a considerable amount of contradictory information, not never change. C is a trap: a schema is functional when it is accurate, not when it is strong — and this one is wrong, so it is dysfunctional. And D is a trap: there is no conscious intent to deny anyone an opportunity.

3. A manager decides not to promote a highly capable Hispanic woman into a management position because he thinks she will not be assertive enough to supervise others. The manager's decision is most likely based on:
  1. A stereotype
  2. The salience effect
  3. Procedural justice
  4. A functional schema
Answer

A. This is exactly the book's example: the manager judged a whole person from a visible characteristic (a Hispanic woman) and assumed she is not assertive — a simplistic and often inaccurate belief about a group = stereotype. The trap in B: salience is about extra attention to one person who stands out in a group, not about assuming the traits of a group. C is the opposite of what happened — the manager violated justice. And D is a trap: the schema here is inaccurate, so it is dysfunctional.

4. A manager has six white employees and one Hispanic employee. In group meetings, the manager pays more attention to the Hispanic employee, gives the employee excessive praise for a good project, and excessively criticizes the employee for missing a deadline. This is best described as:
  1. The social status effect
  2. The salience effect
  3. Overt discrimination
  4. Distributive justice
Answer

B. The employee is different and stands out from the rest of the group (6 + 1), so he gets extra attention and is evaluated extremely in both directions — too much praise and too much blame. This is the book's example of the salience effect. The trap in A: social status is about rank (top management against the mailroom), and there is no rank difference here. C is a trap: the book says the manager does it inadvertently (without intent), and overt discrimination must be on purpose. And D is an ethical principle, not a description of a perception error.

5. Unlike inaccurate schemas and perceptual biases, overt discrimination is:
  1. Unintentional and usually done by well-meaning managers
  2. Intentional and deliberate, and it is both unethical and illegal
  3. Intentional, but it is unethical rather than illegal
  4. Activated involuntarily and without awareness
Answer

B. Overt discrimination = knowingly and willingly, which means intentional and deliberate, and the book says both unethical and illegal. The trap in A: this describes schemas and biases, which “lead well-meaning managers to unintentionally discriminate”. C is a trap: the book says both, not only unethical. And D is the description of implicit bias.
