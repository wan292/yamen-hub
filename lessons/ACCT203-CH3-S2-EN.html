Chapter 3 — Deferral of Expense and Revenue

ACCT 203 · Principles of Financial Accounting · Chapter 3: Adjusting Accounts for Financial Statements · Section 2 of 4: Deferral of Expense and Revenue
Objective P1: Prepare adjusting entries for deferral of expenses.
Objective P2: Prepare adjusting entries for deferral of revenues.

This section is “money that moved early, and the adjustment puts it back in its right month”

In the previous lesson you learned that there are four types of adjustments, and that every adjustment follows three steps (Exhibit 3.4): 1) What is the balance now? 2) What should it be? 3) An adjusting entry takes you from the first to the second. Here we take the first two types — deferrals, which means the money moved before the benefit or the service arrived:

  • Deferral of Expense (objective P1): you paid before you benefit → you have an asset, and as you use it, it turns into an expense. The book's examples: insurance, supplies, depreciation.
  • Deferral of Revenue (objective P2): you received cash before you provided the service → you have a liability, and as you provide the service, it turns into revenue.

The book continues with the company FastForward and gives each adjustment a letter: (a) insurance, (b) supplies, (c) depreciation, (d) unearned revenue. Remember the letters — they come back in the adjusted trial balance in the next lesson. And the rule that holds everything together: an adjusting entry affects one or more income statement accounts and one or more balance sheet accounts, and it never touches Cash. The questions here are all numbers: how much is the expense? What is the entry? What is the balance after the adjustment? So focus on the tables and the entries.

Prepaid Expenses · Prepaid Insurance
You pay today for coming months — and the adjustment cuts off each month's share
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Prepaid expenses = an asset you paid for before you get its benefit. Every time you use some of it, that part becomes an expense. The adjusting entry: debit the expense, credit the asset (for depreciation, credit Accumulated Depreciation).
🧠
Apartment rent paid a year ahead: on payment day it is an asset you own; every month you live there, one month becomes expense.
EXPLANATION — WHAT ARE PREPAID EXPENSES?

Prepaid expenses, also called deferred expenses, are assets paid for before you receive their benefits — the book says paid for in advance. When you use these assets, these advance payments become expenses.

The framework: adjusting entries for prepaid expenses increase expenses and decrease assets — the adjustment shows the using up of prepaid expenses. To demonstrate it, the book uses three examples:

  1. Prepaid insurance (this concept)
  2. Supplies (the second concept)
  3. Depreciation (the third concept)

And all three make the same move: we decrease an asset account (balance sheet) and increase an expense account (income statement).

Exhibit 3.5 — Adjusting for Prepaid Expenses (decrease asset and increase expense)

This is Exhibit 3.5. It has two T-accounts: on the left Asset, with the label Decrease above it, and on its debit side the words Unadjusted balance overstated (the balance before the adjustment is bigger than the correct one). On the right Expense, with the label Increase. Under them is the entry Dr. Expense… # / Cr. Asset… #: a green arrow goes from the debit up to the debit side of Expense, and a red arrow goes from the credit up to the credit side of Asset. How to read it: the same amount decreases the asset and increases the expense. What they ask: the direction of the entry, and that the asset before the adjustment is overstated.

Adjustment (a) — journal entry and T-account postings

On top is the entry Adjustment (a), and under it the posting to two T-accounts: Prepaid Insurance (No. 128): debit Dec. 1 2,400, credit Dec. 31 100 in red, and the balance 2,300. Insurance Expense (No. 637): debit Dec. 31 100. Check: 2,400 − 100 = 2,300 ✓. The red numbers = what comes from the adjusting entry.

After adjusting and posting, the Insurance Expense balance = $100 and the Prepaid Insurance balance = $2,300 — ready for the financial statements:

TRAP
  • The direction: debit the expense, credit the asset. A choice with “Debit Prepaid Insurance; credit Insurance Expense” is reversed.
  • Prepaid Insurance is an asset — not an expense and not a liability. It becomes an expense only when it expires.
  • The adjusting entry has no Cash. The cash left on December 1 and that is finished; any choice that debits or credits Cash in the adjustment is wrong.
  • The expense = the expired part, not what remains. In NTK 3-1 the remaining part is 1,000 and the expense is 4,000.
  • Divide by the number of months in the policy (24), not 12: 2,400 ÷ 24 = 100.

Supplies · Other Prepaid Expenses
Count at the end of the period: what is missing from the recorded amount is your expense
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Supplies do not expire with time — you count them. The expense = the balance before adjustment − what is left by the count. The entry: debit Supplies Expense, credit Supplies.
🧠
A grocery counts its shelves at month-end: books say 9,720, shelves hold 8,670 — the 1,050 difference was used, so it is the month's expense.
EXPLANATION — EXAMPLE (b): FASTFORWARD'S SUPPLIES

We count supplies at the end of the period to find the amount used, and then we make the adjusting entry.

Supplies — the FastForward timeline

The picture: Dec. 2, 6, 26 Purchase supplies and record asset — three purchases, each one recorded as an asset. Then Dec. 31 Physical count (counting what is really on the shelf), and Dec. 31 Record expense. So: count first, then the entry.

Step 1FastForward bought $9,720 of supplies in December and used some of them in the same month. When it prepares the statements at December 31, the cost of the supplies used in December is expensed.
Step 2It physically counts the remaining unused supplies at December 31 and finds $8,670 left out of the $9,720. The difference 9,720 − 8,670 = 1,050 = December's supplies expense.
Step 3The adjusting entry is below ↓

Adjustment (b)

Supplies Expense went up → an expense increases with a debit; Supplies went down → an asset decreases with a credit. The effect on the equation: Assets −1,050 = Liabilities + Equity −1,050.

Adjustment (b) — journal entry and T-account postings

The Supplies account (No. 126): its debit side has the three purchases — Dec. 2 2,500, Dec. 6 7,100, Dec. 26 120 — the credit Dec. 31 1,050, and the balance 8,670. Supplies Expense (No. 652): debit Dec. 31 1,050. Check: 2,500 + 7,100 + 120 = 9,720 and 9,720 − 1,050 = 8,670 ✓.

The Supplies balance after posting = $8,670 — equal to the cost of the remaining supplies.

EXPLANATION — OTHER PREPAID EXPENSES
  • Expenses such as Prepaid Rent and Prepaid Advertising are accounted for exactly as insurance and supplies are.
  • The exception: some prepaid expenses are both paid for and fully used up within a single period. The book's example: a company pays its monthly rent on the first day of each month. Here we record the cash paid with a debit to Rent Expense right away, instead of an asset account — and no adjustment is needed.

Need-To-Know 3-1 (rent): on October 1, the company paid $12,000 for one year of rent in advance for facilities it occupies from that day forward, and it debited Prepaid Rent and credited Cash for 12,000. The statements are prepared at December 31.

  • Step 1: Prepaid Rent = $12,000 (before adjustment)
  • Step 2: it should be $9,000 (the unexpired part): 12,000 − 3,000 = 9,000, and the 3,000 comes from (12,000 ÷ 12 months) × 3 months — October, November, and December
DateAccount titles and explanationDebitCredit
Dec. 31Rent Expense3,000
    Prepaid Rent3,000
Record expired prepaid rent.

Rent Expense went up with a debit (an expense), and Prepaid Rent went down with a credit (an asset) — exactly the same shape as insurance.

From the Decision Maker box (Investor): a publisher signs an Olympic athlete to write a book. The company pays the athlete $500,000 to sign plus future book royalties. A note to its statements says that prepaid expenses include $500,000 in author signing fees “to be matched against future expected sales”. The book's answer: prepaid expenses are assets that are expensed as they are used up. As an investor, you are concerned about the risk of future book sales. The riskier the likelihood of future book sales is, the more likely your analysis is to treat the $500,000, or a portion of it, as an expense, not an asset.

EXAM BOX

We count supplies at period-end to find the amount used and then make an adjusting entry. The balance of the Supplies account is $8,670 after posting—equaling the cost of the remaining supplies.

Other prepaid expenses, such as Prepaid Rent and Prepaid Advertising, are accounted for exactly as insurance and supplies are.

Some prepaid expenses are both paid for and fully used up within a single period. One example is when a company pays monthly rent on the first day of each month. In this case, we record the cash paid with a debit to Rent Expense instead of an asset account.

TRAP
  • Supplies expense = what was used (the difference), not what is left by the count. A choice that puts 8,670 or 500 as the expense is wrong.
  • Beginning balance + purchases − what is left. In NTK 3-1: 1,000 + 2,000 − 500 = 2,500, and the trap is 2,000 − 500 = 1,500 (it forgot the beginning balance).
  • Rent from October 1: only 3 months have expired (October–December) = 3,000, and Prepaid Rent keeps 9,000.
  • Rent that is paid and used up in the same month → debit Rent Expense from the start; there is no asset and no adjusting entry.

Plant Assets · Depreciation
The machine's cost is spread over its life — not charged all on the day you buy it
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Depreciation = allocating the cost of a plant asset over its useful life — not measuring the fall in its market price. The entry: debit Depreciation Expense, credit Accumulated Depreciation.
🧠
A delivery van works five years: you don't charge its whole price to the purchase month — you spread it over the months it works.
EXPLANATION — PLANT ASSETS AND DEPRECIATION

A special category of prepaid expenses is plant assets, also called Property, Plant, & Equipment (PP&E):

  • long-term tangible assets used to produce and sell products and services.
  • The book's examples (four): buildings, machines, vehicles, and equipment.
  • They provide benefits for more than one period.
  • All of them — excluding land — eventually wear out or become less useful.

The costs of these assets are gradually reported as expenses in the income statement over the useful life (or benefit period). Depreciation = the allocation of the costs of these assets over their expected useful lives. But depreciation does not necessarily measure decline in market value. Depreciation expense is recorded with an adjusting entry similar to the entries for other prepaid expenses.

The book's Point: an asset's expected value at the end of its useful life is called salvage value.

Straight-line depreciation = allocates equal amounts of the asset's net cost to depreciation during its useful life.

(Cost − Salvage value) ÷ Useful life
($26,000 − $8,000) ÷ 60 months = $18,000 / 60 = $300 per month

  • Depreciation = cost allocation. A choice that says depreciation measures the decline in market value is wrong — the book: does not necessarily measure decline in market value.
  • Land is not depreciated (excluding land). Any choice that computes depreciation for land is wrong.
  • Subtract the salvage value before you divide: (26,000 − 8,000) ÷ 60 = 300, not 26,000 ÷ 60.
  • 5 years = 60 months. If the question wants the monthly depreciation, divide by months, not years.
  • The credit is Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment — not Equipment directly and not Cash.

Accumulated Depreciation · Contra Account · Book Value
The cost stays written; depreciation piles up next to it and is subtracted from it
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Accumulated Depreciation = a contra account with a normal credit balance. Depreciation collects in it month after month, and it is subtracted from Equipment. The result = book value.
🧠
Your car: the books keep its price and never change it. Next to it, total depreciation grows every year. Price − that total = book value.
EXPLANATION — THE CONTRA ACCOUNT

Accumulated Depreciation is a separate contra account, and its normal balance is a credit.

A contra account has three features:

  1. It is linked with another account.
  2. Its normal balance is the opposite of that account's balance.
  3. It is reported as a subtraction from that account's balance.

At FastForward: Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment is subtracted from Equipment in the balance sheet.

And the accumulated account holds the total depreciation expense for all prior periods in which the asset was used. The book's example: on February 28, 2022, after three months of adjusting entries, the two accounts look like this:

Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment · 168
DebitCredit

Equipment has one debit (Dec. 3 26,000), and Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment has three credits of 300 (Dec. 31, Jan. 31, Feb. 28) and a balance of 900. Check: 300 + 300 + 300 = 900 ✓. Notice that the accumulated account is on the credit side — the opposite of the asset, which is on the debit side.

EXPLANATION — BOOK VALUE

The balance of the accumulated account ($900) is subtracted from the cost of its related asset ($26,000). The difference ($25,100) is called book value, or net amount = the asset's cost minus its accumulated depreciation.

Book value = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation
$26,000 − $900 = $25,100

These balances are reported in the assets section of the February 28 balance sheet. The book shows two ways to report plant assets:

Left: Equipment $26,000, under it Less accumulated depreciation 900, and the result 25,100. Right: one line, Equipment, net $25,100. Between the two is the word OR and a box Book Value with arrows to both sides. How to read it: both ways give the same number (25,100) — the first shows the details, and the second shows only the net. What they ask: compute the book value, or what Equipment, net means.

EXAM BOX

Accumulated Depreciation is a separate contra account and has a normal credit balance. A contra account is an account linked with another account, it has an opposite normal balance, and it is reported as a subtraction from that other account's balance.

The Accumulated Depreciation contra account includes total depreciation expense for all prior periods for which the asset was used.

The $900 balance in the Accumulated Depreciation account is subtracted from its related $26,000 asset cost. The difference ($25,100) between these two balances is called book value, or net amount, which is the asset's costs minus its accumulated depreciation.

TRAP
  • Accumulated Depreciation is not a liability and not an expense — it is a contra asset with a credit balance, subtracted from the asset in the balance sheet.
  • Depreciation Expense = the depreciation of this period only (300 for December) and it goes to the income statement. Accumulated Depreciation = the total from the first day (900 after 3 months) and it goes to the balance sheet.
  • Book value = cost − accumulated depreciation. Not cost − salvage value, and not the market value.
  • The Equipment account itself stays at its cost of 26,000 — depreciation never goes into it.

Unearned Revenue
You got paid before you worked — so you owe the service until you deliver it
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Unearned revenue = cash you received before you provide the service → a liability for you. As you provide the service, it turns into revenue: debit Unearned Revenue, credit Revenue.
🧠
A yearly gym membership: the gym takes your money on day one but owes you a year of training; each month you attend becomes revenue.
EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITION AND THE FRAMEWORK

Unearned revenue = cash received before providing products and services. Its other name is deferred revenues, and they are liabilities. When you accept the cash, you accept an obligation to provide the product or service. So we defer, or postpone, reporting the amounts received as revenues until the product or service is provided.

The framework: as the products or services are provided, the liability decreases and the unearned revenues become revenues. So the adjusting entries decrease the unearned revenue account (balance sheet) and increase the revenue account (income statement).

Exhibit 3.8 — Adjusting for Unearned Revenues (decrease liability and increase revenue)

This is Exhibit 3.8 — the same shape as 3.5, but the two accounts changed: on the left Liability with Decrease above it, and on the right Revenue with Increase above it. The entry is Dr. Liability… # / Cr. Revenue… #: a red arrow goes to the debit side of the liability, and a green arrow goes to the credit side of the revenue. What they ask: the debit is the liability and the credit is the revenue — and the reversed choice is the most famous trap.

The book's sports example: when the Boston Celtics receive cash from advance ticket sales, they record it in an unearned revenue account called Deferred Game Revenues, and they record revenue as games are played. Unearned revenues are common in sporting and concert events.

EXPLANATION — EXAMPLE (d): FASTFORWARD'S UNEARNED CONSULTING REVENUE

On December 26, FastForward agreed to provide consulting services to a client for 60 days for a fixed fee of $3,000.

Step 1: on December 26, the client paid the 60-day fee in advance, covering the period from December 27 to February 24. The entry to record the cash received in advance:

DateAccount titles and explanationDebitCredit
Dec. 26Cash3,000
    Unearned Consulting Revenue3,000
Received advance payment for services over the next 60 days.

This is not an adjusting entry — Cash (an asset) went up with a debit, and Unearned Consulting Revenue (a liability) went up with a credit. The effect: Assets +3,000 = Liabilities +3,000 + Equity. The payment increased cash and created a liability to do consulting work for 60 days: 5 days this year and 55 days next year.

Unearned Revenues — the FastForward timeline

The picture: Dec. 26 Cash received in advance and record liability — the client hands over the cash and the consultant says Thanks for cash in advance. I'll work now through Feb. 24. At the end, Dec. 31 Provided 5 days of services and record revenue = the adjusting entry.

Step 2: as time passes, FastForward earns the payment by consulting. By December 31 it has provided 5 days of service, so it has earned 5/60 of the 3,000: $3,000 × 5/60 = $250. The revenue recognition principle requires that the $250 be reported as revenue on the December income statement.

Step 3: the adjusting entry that reduces the liability and recognizes the revenue:

DateAccount titles and explanationDebitCredit
Adjustment (d)
Dec. 31Unearned Consulting Revenue250
    Consulting Revenue250
Record earned revenue that was received in advance ($3,000 × 5/60).

Unearned Consulting Revenue went down → a liability decreases with a debit; Consulting Revenue went up → a revenue increases with a credit. The effect: Assets = Liabilities −250 + Equity +250 — assets did not move.

Adjustment (d) — journal entry and T-account postings

Unearned Consulting Revenue (236): debit Dec. 31 250, credit Dec. 26 3,000, and a 2,750 credit balance. Consulting Revenue (403): credits Dec. 5 4,200, Dec. 12 1,600, Dec. 31 250, and the balance 6,050. Check: 3,000 − 250 = 2,750 and 4,200 + 1,600 + 250 = 6,050 ✓.

The adjusting entry transfers $250 from unearned revenue (a liability account) to a revenue account:

Before adjustmentAdjustmentAfter adjustment
Unearned Consulting Revenue = $3,000 Deduct 250 from Unearned Consulting Revenue and add 250 to Consulting Revenue Unearned Consulting Revenue = $2,750
Reports 3,000 in unearned revenue for consulting services promised for 60 days ($50 per day) Records 5 days of earned consulting revenue = 5/60 of the unearned amount Reports 2,750 in unearned revenue for consulting services owed over the next 55 days (55 days × $50 = $2,750)

If you do not make the adjustment: the 250 stays in Unearned Consulting Revenue → liabilities are overstated by 250, and Consulting Revenue is 250 too small → net income and equity are understated by 250. This is the opposite direction of a prepaid expense.

From the Ethical Risk box (Clawbacks from Accounting Fraud): former executives at Saba Software, a cloud-based talent management system, were charged with accounting fraud by the SEC. They were charged for falsifying revenue. The goal was to boost income. This alleged overstatement of income led to a payback of millions of dollars to the company by the former CEO and former CFO. So recording revenue at the right time is not a small detail — it is honesty.

EXAM BOX

Unearned revenue is cash received in advance of providing products and services. Unearned revenues, or deferred revenues, are liabilities. When cash is accepted, an obligation to provide products or services is accepted. We defer, or postpone, reporting amounts received as revenues until the product or service is provided.

As products or services are provided, the liability decreases and the unearned revenues become revenues. Adjusting entries for unearned revenue decrease the unearned revenue (balance sheet) account and increase the revenue (income statement) account, as shown in Exhibit 3.8.

The adjusting entry transfers $250 from unearned revenue (a liability account) to a revenue account.

TRAP
  • Unearned revenue = a liability — not a revenue and not an asset. The word revenue in the name tricks you.
  • The direction of the adjustment: debit Unearned Revenue (the liability decreases) / credit Revenue. The reversed entry is wrong.
  • The December 26 entry (debit Cash) is not an adjusting entry — it records the cash received. The December 31 adjustment has no Cash.
  • The revenue = the earned part only (5 days = 250), and the remaining 2,750 stays a liability. A choice that puts 2,750 as revenue is the opposite.
  • If you forget the adjustment: liabilities are bigger and net income is smaller — the opposite of a prepaid expense. Do not carry the direction from one type to the other.
  • Unearned (you received cash before you worked) ≠ accrued revenue (you worked and did not receive cash yet) — the second one is in the next lesson.

1. The Supplies account had an $800 debit balance at the start of the year. Supplies of $2,600 were purchased during the year and debited to Supplies. A December 31 physical count shows $650 of supplies remaining. The adjusting entry is:
  1. Debit Supplies Expense $650; credit Supplies $650
  2. Debit Supplies Expense $1,950; credit Supplies $1,950
  3. Debit Supplies Expense $2,750; credit Supplies $2,750
  4. Debit Supplies $2,750; credit Supplies Expense $2,750
Answer

C. The amount used = the balance before adjustment − what is left by the count: 800 + 2,600 − 650 = 2,750. The entry debits Supplies Expense (an expense went up) and credits Supplies (an asset went down). The trap in A: it puts what is left (650) instead of what was used. B forgot the beginning balance (2,600 − 650 = 1,950) — the same trap as NTK 3-1. And in D the amount is right, but the entry is reversed.

2. On October 1, a company paid $6,000 for a 24-month insurance policy and debited Prepaid Insurance. What amount of insurance expense is reported for the year ended December 31?
  1. $750
  2. $1,500
  3. $5,250
  4. $6,000
Answer

A. 6,000 ÷ 24 = 250 per month, and 3 months have expired (October, November, and December): 250 × 3 = 750. The trap in B: it divided by 12 instead of 24. C (5,250) is the balance left in Prepaid Insurance after the adjustment, not the expense. And D (6,000) is the cash basis — the whole amount as an expense on the payment day, which is exactly what the accrual basis rejects.

3. Equipment costing $50,000 has an expected salvage value of $5,000 and a useful life of 5 years. Using straight-line depreciation, what is the equipment's book value after 2 years?
  1. $27,000
  2. $32,000
  3. $30,000
  4. $41,000
Answer

B. (50,000 − 5,000) ÷ 5 = 9,000 per year; after two years Accumulated Depreciation = 18,000; and Book value = 50,000 − 18,000 = 32,000. The trap in A: it subtracted the accumulated depreciation from 45,000 — the salvage value is used only to compute depreciation, and book value = cost − accumulated depreciation. C forgot to subtract the salvage value (50,000 ÷ 5 = 10,000 per year). And D counted only one year.

4. On November 1, a company received $4,800 in advance for services to be provided evenly over 6 months (November 1 to April 30) and credited Unearned Revenue. The December 31 adjusting entry is:
  1. Debit Service Revenue $1,600; credit Unearned Revenue $1,600
  2. Debit Unearned Revenue $3,200; credit Service Revenue $3,200
  3. Debit Cash $1,600; credit Service Revenue $1,600
  4. Debit Unearned Revenue $1,600; credit Service Revenue $1,600
Answer

D. 4,800 ÷ 6 = 800 per month, and two months are earned (November and December) = 1,600. The liability decreases with a debit and the revenue increases with a credit. The trap in A: the entry is reversed. B (3,200) is the part that is still unearned (4 months). And C: an adjusting entry never touches Cash — the cash was received on November 1 and recorded then.

5. At year-end, a company forgets to record the adjusting entry for $900 of unearned revenue that has now been earned. What is the effect on the financial statements?
  1. Assets are overstated by $900 and net income is overstated by $900
  2. No effect, because the cash was already received and recorded
  3. Liabilities are overstated by $900 and net income is understated by $900
  4. Liabilities are understated by $900 and net income is overstated by $900
Answer

C. Without the adjustment, the 900 stays in Unearned Revenue (a liability) → liabilities are overstated by 900; and revenue is missing 900 → net income is understated by 900. The trap in A: this is the effect of forgetting a prepaid expense adjustment (assets and income too big) — a different type. D reversed the direction. And B: it is true that the cash was recorded, but the revenue was not recorded in its period — and that is the whole idea of the accrual basis.
