Chapter 6 — The Global Environment and the Task Environment

MGT 102 · Chapter 6 · Section 1 of 4: The Global Environment and the Task Environment
Objective LO6-1: Explain why the ability to perceive, interpret, and respond appropriately to the global environment is crucial for managerial success.
Objective LO6-2: Differentiate between the global task and global general environments.
Objective LO6-3: Identify the main forces in the global task and general environments, and describe the challenges that each force presents to managers.

This section is “what is outside your company and affects it? The global environment, and its close circle: the task environment”

This is the first lesson in Chapter 6, and the whole chapter is built on one idea: outside the boundaries of any organization there are forces that change all the time and bring opportunities and threats, and the successful manager is the one who sees them, understands them, and responds to them well (LO6-1). The book divides this global environment into two circles: a close circle (the task environment) with only 4 forces, and a wider circle (the general environment) with 5 forces (LO6-2). Then it explains the whole close circle in detail: suppliers, distributors, customers, and competitors, with barriers to entry (LO6-3). The five forces of the general environment come in detail in the next lesson.

From now on, watch for five things that trip up students here: (1) global against general — the first is the whole and the second is a part of it, (2) which force is in which circle (suppliers = task, demographic = general) — this is Figure 6.1, (3) the two conditions of a supplier's power (only source + vital input) and the meaning of global outsourcing, (4) competitors against potential competitors, (5) the three sources of barriers to entry — this is Figure 6.2. And take the English definitions word for word from the tables and the EXAM BOX.

The Global Environment
Everything outside your company that affects its resources — it brings opportunities and it brings threats
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The global environment = forces and conditions outside the organization's boundaries that affect how it works. These forces change over time, so they give the manager opportunities and threats — and the manager who understands them and responds well makes his organization succeed.
🧠
A sailor cannot control the wind, but the one who reads the weather well arrives — and the one who ignores it sinks.
EXPLANATION — WHY MUST MOST ORGANIZATIONS BECOME GLOBAL?
  • Managers of a global company like BMW work in an environment where they compete with other companies for scarce and valuable resources.
  • Managers of companies large and small found that, for an organization to survive and prosper in the 21st century, most organizations must become global organizations — they work and compete not only domestically (at home) but also globally, in countries around the world.
  • Working in the global environment is uncertain and unpredictable — the reason: it is complex and changes constantly.
  • For the organization to adapt successfully to this environment, its managers must learn to understand the forces that work in it, and how these forces give rise to opportunities and threats.

What the whole chapter covers (from the book's introduction):

  1. Why the environment — at home and globally — has become more open, vibrant, and competitive.
  2. How the forces of the task environment and the general environment affect global organizations and their managers.
  3. The four approaches organizations use to expand internationally — they come in lesson 3.
  4. Why it is important for the manager to develop a global perspective as he tries to increase the organization's efficiency and effectiveness.

EXPLANATION — THE BOX “A MANAGER'S CHALLENGE: BMW'S GLOBAL EXPANSION FOCUSES ON CHINA”
  • The largest U.S. car exporter by value is BMW — a German company! It exported more than 250,000 U.S.-made vehicles worth $10.1 billion in 2021, and it was number one for the eighth year in a row.
  • The top destination for its U.S. exports: China (almost 25%), then Germany, South Korea, Canada, Great Britain, and more than 100 other countries.
  • Most of them were made at the plant in Spartanburg, South Carolina (11,000 people), and the headquarters in Munich calls it the “center of competence” for its X line of vehicles. In 2021 it set a record: almost 434,000 vehicles, despite disruptions in the market for car parts.
  • China became BMW's largest market in the world (almost 40% of its sales), and Chinese interest in BMW's electric vehicles grew almost 70% in 2021. Its Chinese factories made 700,000 vehicles in 2021 and will begin to make the X5 — with no cuts in Spartanburg, because the extra production is to meet the growth in demand.
  • BMW was already making X5s for China in Thailand, since the time China put tariffs (import taxes) on U.S. imports.
  • In 2018 the Chinese government announced that it would phase out the rules that forced foreign companies to partner with Chinese owners and own no more than half. BMW announced that it would take majority control of its Chinese joint venture in 2022 — this needs renegotiating with the partners + a license from the government.
  • Its success is built on superior engineering and premium product quality, so it applies standard processes and structures across a production network of 31 facilities in 15 countries (with artificial intelligence, 3D printing, virtual reality, and the Internet of Things), and it has a sales network in more than 140 countries.

Why does the box matter? It shows you the forces of the global environment in real life: new customers in China (an opportunity), tariffs and ownership rules (political and legal forces — lesson 2), and a joint venture (a way to expand — lesson 3).

EXAM BOX

Managers of companies large and small have found that to survive and prosper in the 21st century, most organizations must become global organizations that operate and compete not only domestically, at home, but also globally, in countries around the world. Operating in the global environment is uncertain and unpredictable because it is complex and changes constantly.

The global environment is a set of forces and conditions in the world outside an organization's boundary that affects how it operates and shapes its behavior. These forces change over time and thus present managers with opportunities and threats.

The ability of managers to understand forces in the global environment and to respond appropriately to those forces, such as BMW's ability to make and sell vehicles that customers around the world want to buy, are critical factors affecting organizational performance.

TRAP
  • Global environment ≠ general environment. The first is the whole (task + general), and the second is only a part of it. The two words start with the same letter — this is the easiest trap in the chapter.
  • Global organizations = work and compete in more than one country — they do not have to be huge; the book says companies large and small.
  • Why is the global environment uncertain and unpredictable? Because it is complex and changes constantly — not because it is “stable” or “simple”.
  • Do not flip the examples: new production technology, cheaper components, new markets = opportunities. New competitors, a global recession, an oil shortage = threats.
  • The ways to expand internationally = four (not three or five) — details in lesson 3.
  • BMW: the largest U.S. car exporter by value, and it is German; China ≈ 25% of its U.S. exports and ≈ 40% of its global sales — do not mix the two numbers.

Task Environment vs. General Environment
The close circle pushes on you every day; the far one moves the close one and is harder to notice
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The global environment has two components: the task environment (4 forces: suppliers, distributors, customers, competitors — its effect is immediate and direct, and it presses on you daily) and the general environment (5 wide forces — they affect the organization and its task environment, and they are harder to see and respond to).
🧠
Two circles around your shop: the close one knocks on your door daily; the far one is weather that changes everyone.

EXAM BOX

To identify opportunities and threats caused by forces in the environment, it is helpful for managers to distinguish between the task environment and the more encompassing general environment (see Figure 6.1).

The task environment contains the forces that have the most immediate and direct effect on managers because they pressure and influence managers daily.

The general environment includes the wide-ranging global, economic, technological, sociocultural, demographic, political, and legal forces that affect the organization and its task environment. For the individual manager, opportunities and threats resulting from changes in the general environment are often more difficult to identify and respond to than are events in the task environment.

Forces in the task environment result from the actions of suppliers, distributors, customers, and competitors both at home and abroad (see Figure 6.1). These four groups affect a manager's ability to obtain resources and dispose of outputs daily, weekly, and monthly and thus have a significant impact on short-term decision making.

The global environment has two components: the task environment and the general environment. The opportunities and threats associated with forces in the task environment become more complex as a company expands globally.

TRAP
  • Which force goes where? suppliers · distributors · customers · competitors = task. economic · technological · sociocultural · demographic · political and legal = general. A choice that puts demographic forces or economic forces in the task environment is wrong.
  • Words that go with task: most immediate and direct · daily · short-term decision making. Words that go with general: wide-ranging · more encompassing · more difficult to identify and respond to.
  • The general environment affects the organization and its task environment — a choice that says “it affects the organization only” or “it has nothing to do with the task environment” is incomplete or wrong.
  • The count: 4 in task and 5 in general, and 2 components. And political and legal = one force in the figure and in the headings.
  • The order of the layers: the organization in the center → task → general outside. So the general environment is the farthest and the widest.
  • A choice that says “opportunities and threats in the task environment become simpler as the company expands globally” is the opposite of the book; they become more complex.

Suppliers and Global Outsourcing
Who gives you your inputs, when he can twist your arm, and why companies buy and make things abroad
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Suppliers = those who give you the inputs (raw materials, parts, and even employees) in return for money. A supplier becomes a threat when it is the only source of a vital input. And globally, companies use global outsourcing: they buy or make their inputs with suppliers abroad to lower costs and improve quality.
🧠
The only gas station on a long road sets any price it wants — because you must fill up and have no other choice.
EXPLANATION — WHO ARE THE SUPPLIERS? (THE DELL EXAMPLE)

Input resources are things like raw materials, component parts, or even employees — and the supplier gets payment for them. One of the most important parts of the manager's job is to ensure a reliable supply of input resources. The book's example is Dell Technologies:

EXPLANATION — WHEN DOES A SUPPLIER BECOME A THREAT? (BARGAINING POSITION + THE NUTRASWEET STORY)

Changes in the nature, number, or type of suppliers bring opportunities and threats. The biggest threat: when the supplier's bargaining position is so strong that it can raise the prices of its inputs. It is strong especially when two conditions come together:

  1. the supplier is the sole source of an input, and
  2. the input is vital to the organization.
#The NutraSweet story in order
1G. D. Searle was the only supplier of NutraSweet (the artificial sweetener in most diet soft drinks) for 17 years.
2An important ingredient with no acceptable substitute — saccharin and other artificial sweeteners raised health concerns.
3The reason: Searle invented it and held the patent, and patents prohibit other organizations from introducing a competing product for 17 years.
4The result: a high price = twice the price of an equivalent amount of sugar → it raised the costs of Coca-Cola and PepsiCo.
5When the patent expired, many companies came in with similar products and prices fell.
6In the 2000s, Splenda (made by McNeil Nutritionals) replaced NutraSweet, NutraSweet's price fell further, and Splenda became the one that could demand a high price.
7Later a natural sweetener from the stevia plant (zero calories), introduced less than ten years ago, won market share, and its global market is expected to be more than $1.6 billion in 2030.

The opposite: when the organization has many suppliers for one input, it is the one in the strong position, and it can demand low-cost, high-quality inputs and push suppliers to lower their prices — as Dell does when it keeps searching for low-cost suppliers abroad to keep its prices competitive. And globally the organization can (1) buy from suppliers overseas, or (2) become its own supplier and make its products abroad.

EXPLANATION — THE BOX “ETHICS IN ACTION: LEVI STRAUSS INSISTS GLOBAL SUPPLIERS TREAT THEIR WORKERS WELL”
  • In the 1990s, when Levi Strauss & Company began to work with low-wage countries, it made a code of conduct called Terms of Engagement: it meets workers' basic needs and protects their rights and the environment, and any supplier who wants to sell to Levi's must follow it. Over time it became the norm in the clothing industry.
  • But violations continued, so CEO Chip Bergh decided that lasting change needs a shift in managers' attitudes.
  • The Worker Well-Being initiative: improve the experience of workers at suppliers → more efficiency and reliability → a stronger supply network = a win for all. Levi's gives money and guidance, but each supplier makes its own plan.
  • The method: survey the workers → learn what they need to be healthy, productive, and engaged at work → find the areas to improve → work with nonprofit partners. And it worked with Harvard's Center for Health and the Global Environment on success measures that connect worker well-being with business performance.
  • An example: the Apparel International (AI) factory in Nazareno, Mexico (1,200 workers): the survey did not show much, so they worked with a non-government organization (NGO), and the two key problems appeared: no access to health care and poor communication between managers and workers. They trained the supervisors to communicate respectfully, and when attitudes and productivity improved, they began spending on the health of the community around them.
  • The numbers: the first 5 years = 12 countries and 100,000 workers. In Egypt, health education reduced absenteeism and turnover among female employees, and in Turkey, changes to the work schedule did the same. The goal: factories producing 80% of its products by 2020, and all suppliers' workers by 2025.
EXPLANATION — THE GLOBAL SUPPLIER NETWORK AND GLOBAL OUTSOURCING
  • A common problem for managers of large global companies such as Ford, Caterpillar, and Dell: building a global supplier network that keeps costs down and quality up.
  • The Boeing 777 was built with parts from more than 500 global suppliers — chosen because they were the best in the world at their work. Pleased with the result, Boeing outsourced a bigger percentage of parts for the Boeing 787 Dreamliner — but many serious problems delayed the new aircraft for several years.
  • Global buying became more complicated because of (1) the development of many skills and competencies in different countries, and (2) the global impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Technology and the Internet made long-distance exchanges easier to coordinate, so many global companies consolidate the number of suppliers to reduce costs.
TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Global outsourcing
الإسناد الخارجي العالمي		 The purchase or production of inputs or final products from overseas suppliers to lower costs and improve product quality or design. buying or making inputs or the final product through suppliers in other countries to lower costs and improve product quality or design.

And in the main text (the booklet's wording): global outsourcing happens when a company contracts with suppliers in other countries to make the inputs or parts that go into its products, or to assemble the final product — to reduce costs.

  • The Apple example: it contracts with companies in Taiwan and China to make chips, batteries, and LCD displays → it contracts with Foxconn to assemble iPhones and iPads → and it even outsources distribution to FedEx or UPS. Because of COVID-19 disruptions in China, it announced that its chips will be made at a new plant in Arizona.
  • Why has it grown enormously? To take advantage of national differences in the cost and quality of resources such as labor and raw materials → much lower manufacturing costs or higher quality and reliability.
  • Intermediaries or brokers: the exchanges became so complex that some companies specialize in managing other companies' supply chains. The intermediary is located close to potential suppliers, finds the best ones, and designs the most efficient supply chain. Example: Li & Fung in Hong Kong has helped hundreds of major U.S. companies, especially with suppliers in mainland China.
  • The risks: in the pandemic, supply-chain disruptions delayed many products and parts, so some companies started to bring jobs back to the United States (reshore). A Deloitte report says: more than 60% of executives at U.S. transport and manufacturing companies expect to reshore some of their production in the next few years.
  • And companies that do not outsource manufacturing at all: they build their own factories in countries around the world to protect their proprietary technology — like most global automakers, which own their production in China and Mexico to keep control of their global decisions and keep their operations secret.
EXAM BOX

Suppliers are the individuals and companies that provide an organization with the input resources (such as raw materials, component parts, or employees) it needs to produce goods and services. In return, the suppliers receive payment for those goods and services. An important aspect of a manager's job is to ensure a reliable supply of input resources.

Another is trade unions, organizations that represent employee interests and can control the supply of labor by exercising the right of unionized workers to strike.

A supplier's bargaining position is especially strong when (1) the supplier is the sole source of an input and (2) the input is vital to the organization. In contrast, when an organization has many suppliers for a particular input, it is in a relatively strong bargaining position with those suppliers and can demand low-cost, high-quality inputs from them.

Gaining access to low-cost products made abroad represents an opportunity for U.S. companies to lower their input costs. Conversely, they have less control over production processes carried out by contractors located far away. This poses a threat to the brand if the suppliers deliver poor quality.

Global outsourcing occurs when a company contracts with suppliers in other countries to make the various inputs or components that go into its products or to assemble the final products to reduce costs.

Global outsourcing has grown enormously to take advantage of national differences in the cost and quality of resources such as labor or raw materials that can significantly reduce manufacturing costs or increase product quality or reliability.

TRAP
  • Inputs include employees — that is why educational institutions and unions are suppliers of labor, and banks are suppliers of capital. A choice that says “a supplier gives raw materials only” is incomplete.
  • A supplier's power = two conditions together: sole source + vital. And if the organization has many suppliers = the organization is the strong one, not the supplier — do not flip it.
  • NutraSweet: 17 years (the monopoly = the length of the patent), the price was twice sugar, and after it came Splenda, then stevia.
  • Global outsourcing = you bring in your inputs or your final product from suppliers abroad (buying or producing). Exporting = you make products in your country and sell them abroad (lesson 3) — the opposite direction. And its goal in the definition: lower cost + better quality or design.
  • Boeing 777 = more than 500 suppliers and it worked. Boeing 787 Dreamliner = more outsourcing and it was delayed for years. Do not reverse them.
  • The global supply chain: opportunity = cheaper inputs; two threats = the brand (poor quality) and social responsibility (reputation). Levi's answered with Terms of Engagement and then Worker Well-Being.
  • Reshoring (more than 60%, Deloitte) = bringing production back home. Companies that do not outsource do it because of proprietary technology. And Li & Fung = an intermediary in Hong Kong, not a supplier.

Distributors and Customers
The distributor delivers your goods and the customer buys them — and both can turn against you
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Distributors = organizations that help you sell to your customers; if they grow so big that they control the customer's access to your goods, they demand that you lower your prices. Customers = those who buy; changes in their number, types, and tastes bring opportunities and threats, and your success depends on your responsiveness to them.
🧠
The distributor is the door to your customer; if it becomes the only door, it sets the rules for entering.

EXPLANATION — DISTRIBUTORS: WHEN DO THEY BECOME A THREAT? (THE WALMART EXAMPLE)
  • The manager's decisions about how to distribute products to customers have important effects on organizational performance. Example: the package delivery companies FedEx, UPS, and the U.S. Postal Service have become vital distributors for the millions of items bought online and shipped by Internet retailers, at home and abroad.
  • The changing nature of distributors and distribution methods brings opportunities and threats. The threat: if they become so large and powerful that they control customers' access to a particular organization's goods, they can threaten it by demanding that it reduce its prices.
#The Walmart example in order
1The retail giant controls its suppliers' access to millions of customers → so it can demand that they lower their prices to keep its business.
2If Procter & Gamble refuses, Walmart might buy only from its competitors: Unilever and Colgate.
3Recently it pushed suppliers to deliver on time and in full to its warehouses: shipments 98% complete or a 3% fine on the cost of the late goods — to keep inventory low and shelves full while it competes head-on with Amazon.
EXPLANATION — CUSTOMERS: GROUPING THEM AND RESPONDING TO THEM

The book says Dell's customers can be segmented into several distinct groups:

  • Changes in the number and types of customers, or in their tastes and needs, bring opportunities and threats. The organization's success depends on its responsiveness to customers — can it satisfy their needs?
  • The PC industry: customers want smaller computers, longer battery life, new apps, and lower prices → so the companies introduced tablets and other mobile devices.
  • Even a school: if more students whose first language is not English enroll, it may need extra classes for the English language learner.

EXPLANATION — CUSTOMERS AROUND THE WORLD
  • The most obvious opportunity in expanding globally = the prospect of selling to millions or billions of new customers — as Jeff Bezos, Amazon's founder, discovered when he expanded into many countries. And Accenture and Capgemini (two large consulting companies) set up regional operating centers and hire and train thousands of consultants abroad to serve the customers of each region.
  • Many products have gained global acceptance: Coca-Cola, Apple iPads, McDonald's hamburgers, Samsung smartphones = a sign that the tastes of customers in different countries may not be so different after all.
  • And this consolidation is happening in consumer goods and in business products too: Cisco and Siemens (telecommunications equipment), Intel (microprocessors), Oracle and SAP (business systems management software) — they sell to customers all over the world.
EXAM BOX

Distributors are organizations that help other organizations sell their goods or services to customers. The decisions managers make about how to distribute products to customers can have important effects on organizational performance.

If distributors become so large and powerful that they can control customers' access to a particular organization's goods and services, they can threaten the organization by demanding that it reduce the prices of its goods and services.

Customers are the individuals and groups that buy the goods and services an organization produces. Changes in the number and types of customers or in customers' tastes and needs create opportunities and threats. An organization's success depends on its responsiveness to customers—whether it can satisfy their needs.

The most obvious opportunity associated with expanding into the global environment is the prospect of selling goods and services to millions or billions of new customers.

TRAP
  • Distributors = help others sell. Customers = buy. Suppliers = give inputs. A choice that describes a distributor as “provides the organization with raw materials” is the definition of a supplier.
  • The threat of a powerful distributor comes from controlling customers' access, and its weapon is demanding lower prices (Walmart against P&G). Walmart's numbers: 98% complete and a 3% fine — do not swap them.
  • FedEx, UPS, and the U.S. Postal Service = examples of distributors (and in the Apple example they appear as outsourced distribution).
  • Dell's customers = 4 groups, and the fourth is government + education together.
  • The most obvious global opportunity = millions or billions of new customers — not “cheaper labor” (that is on the suppliers' side).
  • Global acceptance says the tastes of countries may not be so different — and the consolidation is in consumer goods and business products, not only one of them.

Competitors and Potential Competitors
The one already here fights you with prices, and the one at the door can come in at any moment
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Competitors = organizations that are here now, produce similar goods and services, and try to attract your same customers. The rivalry between you is potentially the most threatening force because it brings a price war. Potential competitors = not here, but they have the resources to enter if they want.
🧠
The current competitor stands with you in the market now; the potential one stands at the door holding the key, waiting for one decision.

EXPLANATION — RIVALRY AND THE STORY OF THE PC INDUSTRY

Rivalry between competitors is potentially the most threatening force the manager must deal with. Why? A high level of rivalry usually brings price competition, and falling prices reduce revenues and profits.

WhenWhat happened
Early 2000sCompetition became intense because Dell was aggressively cutting costs and prices to grow its global market share. IBM had to exit the PC business after losing billions, HP suffered losses, and Dell's profits soared.
2006HP recovered (it lowered its costs and offered stylish new PCs), and Apple was growing fast → Dell's profit margins kept shrinking.
2009HP overtook Dell to become the largest PC maker in the world.
After thatAfter a long and harsh battle with investors, Michael Dell took the company private and focused on becoming more of an all-around technology company that depends less on PC sales.

EXPLANATION — THE THREAT OF NEW ENTRANTS (THE AMAZON EXAMPLE)
  • Extensive rivalry between existing competitors is a major threat to profitability, and so is the possibility of new competitors entering the task environment.
  • Ten years ago Amazon did not sell furniture or large appliances, but it could enter if its managers decided it could sell them online at a profit — so it was a potential competitor. Today it really sells them.
  • When new competitors enter an industry: competition increases, and prices and profits decrease — this is what furniture and electronics stores like Best Buy discovered while fighting Amazon.
  • What decides how likely they are to enter? Barriers to entry — the next concept.
EXAM BOX

One of the most important forces an organization confronts in its task environment is competitors. Competitors are organizations that produce goods and services similar and comparable to a particular organization's goods and services. In other words, competitors are organizations trying to attract the same customers.

Rivalry between competitors is potentially the most threatening force managers must deal with. A high level of rivalry typically results in price competition, and falling prices reduce customer revenues and profits.

Potential competitors are organizations that are not currently in a task environment but have the resources to enter if they so choose.

When new competitors enter an industry, competition increases and prices and profits decrease.

TRAP
  • Competitors = already here in the task environment. Potential competitors = not here but have the resources to enter. A scenario of “a company that does not sell this product but could enter” = potential.
  • Rivalry = potentially the most threatening force — not “the least dangerous force”. Its result: price competition → lower revenues and profits.
  • New competitors entering = more competition + lower prices and profits — do not flip the direction.
  • The PC sequence: IBM exited · 2009 HP overtook Dell · 2022 Lenovo first, then HP, Dell, Apple, Asus.
  • The margin definition says currently are not … but can enter, and the booklet says not presently … but could enter — the same meaning, do not worry about the different words.

Barriers to Entry
Three walls that make entering hard and costly — so they cut the number of competitors and protect the ones already inside
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Barriers to entry = factors that make entering a particular industry difficult and costly. The higher the barriers, the fewer the competitors and the lower the threat of competition. They come from three sources: economies of scale, brand loyalty, and government regulations.
🧠
A castle with three walls: a size that keeps your costs low, customers who never switch, and laws that lock the gate.

  2. Higher barriers → fewer competitors in the task environment → lower threat of competition.
  3. Fewer competitors → easier to get customers and keep prices high.
EXPLANATION — THE THREE SOURCES OF BARRIERS TO ENTRY

The book says barriers to entry come from three main sources: economies of scale, brand loyalty, and government regulations that impede entry.

SourceHow does it make a barrier?The book's example
1. Economies of scale
وفورات الحجم		 It comes from: (1) making products in very large quantities, (2) buying inputs in bulk, (3) using the organization's resources better than competitors by fully using employees' skills and knowledge. If the existing organizations are large and enjoy big economies of scale, their costs are lower than the costs of potential entrants → the newcomer finds entering expensive. Amazon: a highly efficient and expansive distribution system, and it bought huge parcels of land in Niagara, New York; Ontario, California; and Loveland, Colorado for distribution centers.
2. Brand loyalty
الولاء للعلامة		 If the existing organizations enjoy strong brand loyalty, a new entrant finds it difficult and costly to get a share of the market, and it must pay huge marketing costs so customers know about its goods. Apple, Google, Samsung, and Amazon: high loyalty + some of the highest website hit rates → they raise their marketing revenues.
3. Government regulations
اللوائح الحكومية اللي تعيق الدخول		 They work as a barrier at the industry level and the country level. The opposite is clear in deregulation: industries such as air transport, trucking, utilities, and telecommunications saw a high level of new entry after it, so the existing companies were forced to work more efficiently or go out of business. At the national and global levels there are administrative barriers: government policies that create barriers to entry and limit imports of goods by foreign companies. Japan is well known for many ways of restricting the entry of foreign competitors, and it came under pressure to relax and abolish regulations such as those on rice imports ↓
Figure 6.2 — Barriers to Entry and Competition

This is Figure 6.2 in the book (p. 160). Read it from left to right as one sentence: three boxes stacked on the left — Economies of scale (top), Brand loyalty (middle), Government regulations (bottom) — each one sends an arrow to the box in the middle: create barriers to entry, and from it one arrow goes to the last box on the right: that deter potential competitors. So the full sentence is: Economies of scale / Brand loyalty / Government regulations → create barriers to entry → that deter potential competitors. How to read it: the three on the left are the sources, the barriers are the result, and potential competitors are the ones who are deterred — notice that the figure says potential, not the competitors already in the market. What they ask: “what are the three sources?”, “which one is NOT a source?” (they use potential competitors or rivalry as a trap), or a scenario: a company whose huge distribution system lowers its costs → economies of scale; customers who do not want to change brands → brand loyalty; the government limits imports → government regulations.

EXPLANATION — THE STORY OF THE JAPANESE RICE MARKET (AN EXAMPLE OF ADMINISTRATIVE BARRIERS, IN ORDER)
  1. The Japanese rice market — like many Japanese markets — was closed to foreign competitors until 1993, to protect Japan's thousands of high-cost, low-output rice farmers.
  2. Growing rice is expensive there because of the country's mountainous terrain, and Japanese consumers have always paid high prices.
  3. Under foreign pressure the government opened the market — but it let foreign competitors export to Japan only 8% of its yearly rice consumption, to protect its farmers.
  4. In the 2000s an alliance between Lundberg Family Farms (an organic rice grower in California) and the Nippon Restaurant Enterprise Co. found a new way in: there is no tariff on rice used in processed foods, so they turned the U.S. organic rice into “O-bento” — an organic hot boxed lunch with rice, vegetables, chicken, beef, and salmon, all imported from the United States.
  5. The lunches became popular, and Japanese rice farmers protested to the government, because the entry of U.S. rice growers forced them to leave their fields idle or grow less profitable crops.
  6. Recently there was a severe shortage of reasonably priced rice for the food service sector (because of a strict policy on growing rice for animal feed) → higher demand for U.S. rice = roughly 58% of Japan's rice imports in 2021.
  7. But U.S.–Japan trade talks broke down, including the talks on rice imports, because of the U.S. withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership (the book writes it TTP). And countries in that agreement that export rice — including Australia — could give the United States strong competition in Japan's rice import business.

The book's summary of the competitors part: intense rivalry creates a task environment that is highly threatening and makes it harder for the manager to get resources. And low rivalry gives moderate competitive pressures and bigger opportunities for the manager to get the resources that make his organization effective.

EXAM BOX

Barriers to entry are factors that make it difficult and costly for a company to enter a particular task environment or industry. In other words, the more difficult and costly it is to enter the task environment, the higher are the barriers to entry. The higher the barriers to entry, the fewer the competitors in an organization's task environment and, thus, the lower the threat of competition. With fewer competitors, it is easier to obtain customers and keep prices high.

Barriers to entry result from three main sources: economies of scale, brand loyalty, and government regulations that impede entry (see Figure 6.2). Economies of scale are the cost advantages associated with large operations.

Brand loyalty is customers' preference for the products of organizations currently in the task environment. If established organizations enjoy significant brand loyalty, a new entrant will find it difficult and costly to obtain a share of the market.

At the national and global levels, administrative barriers are government policies that create barriers to entry and limit imports of goods by overseas companies.

In summary, intense rivalry among competitors creates a task environment that is highly threatening and makes it increasingly difficult for managers to gain access to the resources an organization needs to make goods and services. Conversely, low rivalry results in a task environment where competitive pressures are more moderate and managers have greater opportunities to acquire the resources they need to make their organizations effective.

TRAP
  • The relationship: higher barriers = fewer competitors = lower threat of competition = high prices for those already inside. A choice that says “high barriers increase the number of competitors” is upside down.
  • There are only three sources: economies of scale · brand loyalty · government regulations. Potential competitors are the ones who are stopped, and rivalry is not one of the sources.
  • Economies of scale = cost advantages (not quality). They come from huge quantities + buying in bulk + better use of resources and skills.
  • Brand loyalty = preference for the products of organizations already in the market (currently existing) — not the new ones; and the newcomer pays huge marketing costs.
  • Deregulation lowers the barrier: after it came a high level of new entry (air transport, trucking, utilities, telecommunications). And administrative barriers = government policies that limit imports (Japan and rice).
  • The rice numbers: closed until 1993 · only 8% allowed · O-bento because there is no tariff on processed foods · 58% U.S. share in 2021 · the reason the talks broke down = the U.S. withdrawal from the TPP.

1. According to Figure 6.1, which of the following is a force in the task environment rather than the general environment?
  1. Distributors
  2. Demographic forces
  3. Political and legal forces
  4. Technological forces
Answer

A. In Figure 6.1 the middle ring (TASK ENVIRONMENT) has only four forces: Competitors at the top, Distributors on the right, Customers at the bottom, Suppliers on the left. The trap in B, C, and D: they are all in the outer ring (GENERAL ENVIRONMENT) — Demographic at the lower right, Political and legal at the lower left, Technological at the top. A quick rule: if the force is people or companies you deal with (supplier, distributor, customer, competitor) = task; if it is the name of a wide area followed by the word forces = general.

2. For 17 years, G. D. Searle was the only company that could make NutraSweet, an ingredient that diet soft drink makers considered important and for which there was no acceptable substitute. As a result, Searle could charge twice the price of an equivalent amount of sugar. This situation is best explained by the fact that:
  1. soft drink makers had many suppliers for this input, so their bargaining position was strong
  2. Searle was the sole source of an input that was vital to soft drink makers, so its bargaining position was especially strong
  3. Searle enjoyed brand loyalty because it was a potential competitor
  4. soft drink makers used global outsourcing to lower their input costs
Answer

B. These are exactly the two conditions that make a supplier's bargaining position strong: sole source + vital, and the reason was a 17-year patent. The trap in A: it is the opposite situation — many suppliers make the buying organization strong, and here there was only one supplier. C mixes up terms: brand loyalty is a source of barriers to entry, and Searle was already in the market, not a potential competitor. And D: global outsourcing is used to lower costs, but here the soft drink makers' costs went up.

3. Apple contracts with companies in Taiwan and China to make chips, batteries, and LCD displays for its devices, and with Foxconn to assemble its iPhones and iPads, in order to reduce costs. This practice is best described as:
  1. exporting
  2. a joint venture
  3. global outsourcing
  4. economies of scale
Answer

C. Global outsourcing = buying or producing inputs or final products through suppliers abroad to lower costs and improve quality or design — and Apple is the book's own example. The trap in A: exporting = making products at home and selling them abroad (lesson 3) — the opposite direction; here the parts are made abroad and come to Apple. B: a joint venture = two companies own a new business together, and Apple did not share ownership of anything with Foxconn — it only signed a contract with it. D: economies of scale = cost advantages from large size, a source of barriers to entry, not a contract with suppliers.

4. According to Figure 6.2, which of the following is NOT shown as a source that creates barriers to entry?
  1. Economies of scale
  2. Brand loyalty
  3. Government regulations
  4. Potential competitors
Answer

D. Figure 6.2 has only three boxes on the left: Economies of scale, Brand loyalty, and Government regulations, and all of them point into create barriers to entry. Potential competitors are in the last box on the right: the barriers deter (stop) them — so they are the ones affected, not a source. The trap: the name is in the figure, so you think it is one of the sources. A, B, and C are all correct sources — and the question uses NOT, so do not pick the first name you know.

5. Ten years ago, Amazon was not in the furniture or large appliance business, but it had the resources to enter these businesses if its managers chose to. For furniture retailers at that time, Amazon was best described as a:
  1. supplier
  2. distributor
  3. current competitor
  4. potential competitor
Answer

D. Potential competitors = organizations that are not currently in a task environment but have the resources to enter if they so choose — this is the book's example word for word. The trap in C: competitors are already there, selling similar products to the same customers, and Amazon was not selling furniture at that time. A and B are other roles in the task environment: a supplier gives you inputs, and a distributor helps you sell. Today, after it really entered, Amazon became a competitor, so competition went up and prices and profits went down.
