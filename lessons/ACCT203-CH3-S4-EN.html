Chapter 3 — Closing, the Accounting Cycle, and the Classified Balance Sheet

ACCT 203 · Principles of Financial Accounting · Chapter 3: Adjusting Accounts for Financial Statements · Section 4 of 4: Closing, the Accounting Cycle, and the Classified Balance Sheet
Objective P6: Prepare closing entries and a post-closing trial balance.
Objective C2: Explain and prepare a classified balance sheet.
Objective A1: Compute and analyze profit margin and current ratio.

The last stop in the chapter: close the books, go around the cycle, and organize the balance sheet

In the previous lessons we made the adjusting entries and prepared the adjusted trial balance and the financial statements of FastForward. So what happens after the statements are done? Revenues, expenses, and dividends must go back to zero so that the next period starts clean — this is called closing (objective P6).

After that, the book puts together everything you studied in chapters 2 and 3 in 10 steps called the accounting cycle. It also teaches you to arrange the balance sheet in drawers: current and noncurrent (the classified balance sheet, objective C2). And last, two ratios to analyze a company: profit margin and current ratio (A1).

Focus on three things that come in the exam every time: which accounts are closed and which are not, the order of the four closing entries, and where each account goes in the classified balance sheet. All the numbers come from the book — add them up yourself while you read; do not just trust me.

The Closing Process: Temporary and Permanent Accounts
What goes back to zero at the end of the period, and what carries on into the next period
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Closing = zeroing out the period's accounts (revenues, expenses, dividends) and moving their effect to Retained Earnings. Temporary accounts are closed; permanent accounts — that is, all balance sheet accounts — keep their balance.
🧠
Your phone's step counter goes back to zero every night (temporary), but your weight does not — it continues from yesterday (permanent).
EXPLANATION — WHEN, WHAT STEPS, AND WHY

The closing process happens at the end of the accounting period, after the financial statements are finished. It has three steps:

  1. Identify the accounts for closing.
  2. Record and post the closing entries.
  3. Prepare a post-closing trial balance.

And it has two purposes:

  1. It resets the balances of revenues, expenses, and dividends to zero at the end of each period — so these accounts can measure income and dividends for the next period correctly.
  2. It updates the balance of Retained Earnings, so it matches the number in the balance sheet and in the statement of retained earnings.
EXPLANATION — TEMPORARY OR PERMANENT?
Temporary accountsPermanent accounts
The book's definition Relate to one accounting period Report on activities related to one or more future periods
What they include All income statement accounts (revenues and expenses) + the Dividends account + the Income Summary account Assets, liabilities, and equity = all balance sheet accounts (including Common Stock and Retained Earnings)
At period-end Closed — used for one period and then closed, so the balance goes back to zero Not closed — the ending balance carries into future periods

And the rule that sums up the topic: the closing process applies only to temporary accounts.

Temporary vs. Permanent Accounts

A signpost with two arrows: TEMPORARY and PERMANENT, and two boards under them. Left: Temporary Accounts (closed at period-end) = Revenues · Expenses · Dividends · Income Summary. Right: Permanent Accounts (not closed at period-end) = Assets · Liabilities · Common Stock · Retained Earnings. What they ask: “Which account is not closed?” — memorize the two lists; each one has four items.

EXAM BOX

The closing process occurs at the end of an accounting period after financial statements are completed. In the closing process we (1) identify accounts for closing, (2) record and post the closing entries, and (3) prepare a post-closing trial balance. The closing process has two purposes. First, it resets revenue, expense, and dividends account balances to zero at the end of each period. This is done so that these accounts can properly measure income and dividends for the next period. Second, it updates the balance of the Retained Earnings account, which matches that reported in the balance sheet and the statement of retained earnings.

Temporary accounts relate to one accounting period. They include all income statement accounts, the dividends account, and the Income Summary account. They are temporary because such accounts are used for a period and then closed at period-end. The closing process applies only to temporary accounts.

Permanent accounts report on activities related to one or more future accounting periods. They include asset, liability, and equity accounts (all balance sheet accounts). Permanent accounts are not closed each period and carry their ending balance into future periods.

TRAP
  • Dividends is not an income statement account, but it is temporary and it is closed. Thinking that everything connected to equity is permanent is wrong: Common Stock and Retained Earnings are permanent, Dividends is temporary.
  • Income Summary is temporary too — it is opened and closed inside the closing process itself.
  • Closing happens after the financial statements are prepared, not before. If you closed first, the income statement would show only zeros.
  • Do not follow the name: Unearned Consulting Revenue has the word revenue, but it is a liability → permanent. And Accumulated Depreciation (a contra account for an asset) is permanent, unlike Depreciation Expense, which is temporary.

Recording Closing Entries: The Four Steps
Everything flows into Income Summary, and then into Retained Earnings
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Closing entries transfer the end-of-period balances of revenues, expenses, and dividends to the permanent Retained Earnings account, in four steps, and the station in the middle is called Income Summary.
🧠
Revenues and expenses pour into the Income Summary bucket; the leftover (net income) goes to the Retained Earnings tank; dividends come straight from the tank.
EXPLANATION — WHY DO WE NEED CLOSING ENTRIES?

The book gives two reasons:

  1. Revenue, expense, and dividends accounts must begin each period with a zero balance.
  2. Retained Earnings must reflect prior periods' revenues, expenses, and dividends.

An income statement reports revenues and expenses for one period, and dividends are also reported for a period. Because these accounts record information separately for each period, each period must start from zero. The book's point: if Apple did not make closing entries, last year's iPhone revenue would be included with this year's revenue.

Income Summary = a temporary account used only in the closing process: it has a credit for total revenues (and gains) and a debit for total expenses (and losses). So its balance after the first two steps = net income or net loss.

EXPLANATION — THE FOUR STEPS
StepWhat you doThe shape of the entryWhy debit / credit
1Close the credit balances (revenue accounts) to Income Summary Debit each revenue · credit Income Summary for the total A revenue's normal balance is a credit, so you bring it to zero with a debit
2Close the debit balances (expense accounts) to Income Summary Debit Income Summary for the total · credit each expense An expense has a debit balance, so you bring it to zero with a credit
3Close Income Summary to Retained Earnings (if there is a profit) Debit Income Summary · credit Retained Earnings It moves net income into equity
4Close Dividends to Retained Earnings Debit Retained Earnings · credit Dividends Dividends has a debit balance, so a credit brings it to zero, and dividends decrease retained earnings

After the closing entries are posted, the revenue, expense, dividends, and Income Summary accounts all have a zero balance, and we say they are closed or cleared.

  1. The revenue (credit 79,000) is brought to zero with a debit, and it goes as a credit to Income Summary.
  2. The two expenses (debit) are brought to zero with a credit: 56,000 + 8,000 = 64,000 as a debit in Income Summary.

And the new balance of Retained Earnings: 45,000 + 15,000 − 20,000 = 40,000 — the same number you will find in Magic's classified balance sheet in concept 4.

EXAM BOX

Closing entries transfer the end-of-period balances in revenue, expense, and dividends accounts to the permanent Retained Earnings account. Closing entries are necessary at the end of each period after financial statements are prepared because

  • Revenue, expense, and dividends accounts must begin each period with zero balances.
  • Retained Earnings must reflect prior periods’ revenues, expenses, and dividends.

To close revenue and expense accounts, we transfer their balances to Income Summary. Income Summary is a temporary account only used for the closing process that contains a credit for total revenues (and gains) and a debit for total expenses (and losses). The Income Summary balance, which equals net income or net loss, is transferred to the Retained Earnings account. The Dividends account balance is transferred to the Retained Earnings account. After closing entries are posted, the revenue, expense, dividends, and Income Summary accounts have zero balances and are said to be closed or cleared.

Step 1: Close Credit Balances in Revenue Accounts to Income Summary
Step 2: Close Debit Balances in Expense Accounts to Income Summary
Step 3: Close Income Summary to Retained Earnings
Step 4: Close Dividends Account to Retained Earnings

TRAP
  • The order: revenues → expenses → Income Summary → Dividends. Dividends never pass through Income Summary — they are closed directly to Retained Earnings, because they are not an expense.
  • To bring an account to zero, do the opposite of its normal balance: revenue (credit) → debit · expense (debit) → credit · dividends (debit) → credit. The choice that says “debit Dividends / credit Retained Earnings” is reversed.
  • If there is a net loss, Income Summary has a debit balance, so step 3 is reversed: debit Retained Earnings / credit Income Summary.
  • The balance of Income Summary after step 2 = net income, not total revenues. For FastForward = 3,785, not 8,150.
  • The terminology trap: the book uses a corporation: Common Stock, Dividends, and Retained Earnings. Some class handouts use a sole proprietorship: Owner's Capital and Owner's Withdrawals (or Drawing). It is the same idea in a different business form, with the same debit and credit rules: capital is on the common stock side, and withdrawals are on the dividends side. So if you see “debit Income Summary / credit Owner's Capital” or “debit Owner's Capital / credit Withdrawals”, it is exactly step 3 and step 4.

Post-Closing Trial Balance and the Accounting Cycle
The last check, and then the ten steps all over again
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Post-closing trial balance = a list of the permanent accounts and their balances after all closing entries. And the accounting cycle = the steps in preparing financial statements — 10 steps that are repeated every period.
🧠
Like a washing machine: clothes in (transactions), wash and rinse (adjustments), take them out clean (statements), empty the tub (closing) — same cycle every week.
EXPLANATION — THE POST-CLOSING TRIAL BALANCE

It has only balance sheet accounts (the permanent ones). It verifies two things:

  1. Total debits = total credits for the permanent accounts.
  2. All temporary accounts have a zero balance.

And it is often the last step in the accounting process. This is FastForward's (Exhibit 3.18):

A situation from the book (Decision Maker — Staff Accountant): a friend shows you the post-closing trial balance she is working on, and it has a line for rent expense. How do you know there is an error? The book says the error is apparent, because Rent Expense is a temporary account, and a post-closing trial balance has only permanent accounts.

EXPLANATION — THE ACCOUNTING CYCLE: 10 STEPS

The accounting cycle = the steps in preparing financial statements. It is called a cycle because the steps are repeated each reporting period. And the timing:

  • Steps 1 to 3 happen regularly, every time the company enters into transactions.
  • Steps 4 to 9 are done at the end of the period.
  • Step 10 (reversing entries) is optional, and it is explained in Appendix 3C.

EXAM BOX

A post-closing trial balance is a list of permanent accounts and their balances after all closing entries. Only balance sheet (permanent) accounts are on a post-closing trial balance. A post-closing trial balance verifies that (1) total debits equal total credits for permanent accounts and (2) all temporary accounts have zero balances.

The accounting cycle is the steps in preparing financial statements. It is called a cycle because the steps are repeated each reporting period. Exhibit 3.19 shows the 10 steps in the cycle. Steps 1 through 3 occur regularly as a company enters into transactions. Steps 4 through 9 are done at the end of a period. Reversing entries in step 10 are optional and are explained in Appendix 3C.

TRAP
  • Any revenue, expense, Dividends, or Income Summary in a post-closing trial balance = an error (the book's example: rent expense).
  • Retained earnings in the post-closing trial balance = the ending balance (3,585), not the beginning balance (0) that is in the adjusted trial balance.
  • The order: unadjusted trial balance (4) → adjustments (5) → adjusted trial balance (6) → statements (7) → closing (8) → post-closing trial balance (9). The statements always come before closing.
  • There are 10 steps, and only the tenth is optional. A choice that says “closing is optional” or “9 steps” is wrong.
  • The work sheet is a helping tool — it does not replace journalizing and posting the adjustments.

Classified Balance Sheet
The same numbers, but arranged in drawers: current and noncurrent
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
An unclassified balance sheet broadly groups accounts into three: assets, liabilities, and equity. A classified balance sheet organizes assets and liabilities into subgroups — and the most important split is current (short-term) and noncurrent (long-term).
🧠
Your closet: the near shelf is for this week's clothes (current); the bag on top of the closet is for next year's clothes (noncurrent).
EXPLANATION — THE CLASSIFICATION STRUCTURE

An example of an unclassified balance sheet: FastForward's balance sheet in Exhibit 3.14 (the previous lesson). A classified one usually has the categories in Exhibit 3.20 — and there is no required layout.

The line between current and noncurrent: current items are expected to come due (be collected or be paid) within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer.

Operating cycle = the time span from when you use cash to acquire goods and services, until cash comes back to you from selling goods and services. Most operating cycles are less than one year, so most companies use one year to classify. The book says: assume the operating cycle is one year, unless we say otherwise.

EXAM BOX

An unclassified balance sheet broadly groups accounts into assets, liabilities, and equity. A classified balance sheet organizes assets and liabilities into subgroups.

An important classification is the separation between current (short-term) and noncurrent (long-term) for both assets and liabilities. Current items are expected to come due (either collected or owed) within one year or the company’s operating cycle, whichever is longer. The operating cycle is the time span from when cash is used to acquire goods and services until cash is received from the sale of goods and services.

A balance sheet lists current assets before noncurrent assets and current liabilities before noncurrent liabilities. Current assets and current liabilities are listed in order of how quickly they will be converted to, or paid in, cash.

Current assets are cash and other resources that are expected to be sold, collected, or used within one year or the company’s operating cycle, whichever is longer. Long-term (or noncurrent) investments include notes receivable and investments in stocks and bonds when they are expected to be held for more than the longer of one year or the operating cycle. Plant assets are tangible assets that are both long-lived and used to produce or sell products and services. Intangible assets are long-term assets that benefit business operations but lack physical form.

Current liabilities are liabilities due to be paid or settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Long-term liabilities are liabilities not due within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Equity is not separated into current and noncurrent categories.

TRAP
  • “Whichever is longer” — not shorter. And the choice that says “always exactly one year” is incomplete.
  • Unearned revenue = a current liability — not a revenue and not an asset.
  • A note receivable due in three years = a long-term investment, not a current asset. What decides it is the due date, not the account name.
  • The part of a long-term debt that is due within one year = a current liability.
  • Land used in operations = a plant asset, and accumulated depreciation is subtracted from the asset — it is not a liability.
  • Plant assets = PP&E = fixed assets — three names for one thing. And goodwill is an intangible asset.
  • Equity is not split into current and noncurrent. And the classified balance sheet has no required layout.

Profit Margin and Current Ratio
Decision Analysis — two ratios to judge profitability and the ability to pay
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Profit margin = how much profit you get from each dollar of sales. Current ratio = do the current assets cover the current liabilities?
🧠
Grocery shop: of 100 riyals of sales, how much is profit? That's profit margin. Can the till cover this month's bills? That's current ratio.
EXPLANATION — PROFIT MARGIN

A useful measure of a company's operating results: the ratio of net income to net sales. It is called profit margin or return on sales, and it shows the percent of profit in each dollar of sales.

Profit margin = Net income ÷ Net sales

VisaNet income$12,080$10,301$ 6,699

Visa's calculation for the current year: 12,080 ÷ 22,977 ≈ 53%, the year before 10,301 ÷ 20,609 ≈ 50%, and the year before that 6,699 ÷ 18,358 ≈ 36%.

Visa's margin is better than Mastercard's in all three years. For Mastercard to improve its margin, it must either reduce expenses or increase revenues at a relatively greater amount than expenses.

A situation from the book (Decision Maker — CFO): your health care equipment company consistently reports a 9% profit margin, similar to its competitors. The treasurer argues the margin can rise to 20% if the company cuts marketing expenses. Do you cut them? The book's answer: it raises the margin in the short run, but in the long run it can hurt current and future sales. Cut the “fat” (expenses that do not create sales), and be careful not to cut the ones that create sales.

EXPLANATION — CURRENT RATIO

Financial statements help assess a company's ability to pay its debts. This analysis affects the decisions of suppliers when they allow a company to buy on credit, and the decisions of creditors when they lend it money — even the loan terms, like the interest rate and the due date. The current ratio is one measure of a company's ability to pay its short-term obligations:

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Exhibit 3.24

A ratio over 1.0 = the current obligations can be covered with current assets.

Costco's ratio in the last three years is slightly above or slightly below 1.0 — so it can cover its current liabilities, but with no extra buffer. And it is better than Walmart in all three years.

A situation from the book (Decision Maker — Analyst): you analyze a dirt bike company: its current ratio is 1.2. Then you find that a big part of its accounts receivable is from one client who has not paid anything in 12 months. When you remove this receivable from current assets, the ratio drops to 0.7. The book's answer: 1.2 suggests that current assets are sufficient to cover current liabilities, but after removing the past-due receivable it is 0.7 — so you conclude that the company will have difficulty meeting its loan payments.

EXAM BOX

A useful measure of a company's operating results is the ratio of its net income to net sales. This ratio is called profit margin, or return on sales, and is computed as in Exhibit 3.22. This ratio shows the percent of profit in each dollar of sales.

Profit margin = Net income / Net sales

The current ratio is one measure of a company's ability to pay its short-term obligations. It is defined in Exhibit 3.24.

Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities

A current ratio of over 1.0 means that current obligations can be covered with current assets.

TRAP
  • The top and the bottom of the fraction: Net income ÷ Net sales. If you flip it (sales ÷ income), you get a number bigger than 1, and it is wrong.
  • The current ratio uses current items only on both sides. Dividing by all liabilities (with long-term ones) is wrong.
  • The line is 1.0: above it = covers, below it = does not cover. Costco is close to 1.0 = it covers with no buffer.
  • Past-due receivables that will not be collected inflate the current ratio — remove them and see the real number (the example 1.2 → 0.7).
  • Raising profit margin by cutting expenses that create sales (like marketing) hurts in the long run — the book says cut the “fat” only.

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer before you open the solution. If you miss question 1, go back to the four-steps table; if you miss question 4, go back to the seven-categories table.

1. At year-end, before closing, a company's accounts show: Services Revenue $40,000; Salaries Expense $22,000; Rent Expense $6,000; Dividends $5,000; Retained Earnings $30,000. After revenues and expenses are closed, which entry closes Income Summary?
  1. Debit Retained Earnings $12,000; credit Income Summary $12,000
  2. Debit Income Summary $12,000; credit Retained Earnings $12,000
  3. Debit Income Summary $7,000; credit Retained Earnings $7,000
  4. Debit Income Summary $40,000; credit Retained Earnings $40,000
Answer

B. Income Summary got a credit of 40,000 (step 1) and a debit of 22,000 + 6,000 = 28,000 (step 2) → a credit balance of 40,000 − 28,000 = 12,000 = net income, so we bring it to zero with a debit and put it as a credit in Retained Earnings. The trap in A: this is the reversed entry (it happens only when there is a net loss). C is a trap: it subtracted the dividends (12,000 − 5,000) — but dividends never pass through Income Summary; they have their own fourth entry. And D forgot the expenses. After step 4, Retained Earnings = 30,000 + 12,000 − 5,000 = 37,000.

2. Which of the following accounts would appear on a post-closing trial balance?
  1. Rent Expense
  2. Income Summary
  3. Dividends
  4. Unearned Consulting Revenue
Answer

D. A post-closing trial balance has only permanent accounts (balance sheet accounts), and Unearned Consulting Revenue is a liability — permanent; in FastForward's post-closing trial balance its balance is 2,750. Do not let the word revenue push you away from D — it is a liability, not a revenue. A is an expense = temporary (the same as the book's Staff Accountant situation). B is temporary and is used only inside closing. And C is a trap: dividends reduce equity, but the account itself is temporary and is closed to Retained Earnings.

3. In the accounting cycle shown in Exhibit 3.19, which step comes immediately after "Prepare financial statements"?
  1. Prepare adjusted trial balance
  2. Prepare post-closing trial balance
  3. Close accounts
  4. Reverse and post (optional)
Answer

C. In Exhibit 3.19, step 7 is Prepare financial statements, and right after it comes step 8, Close accounts, then step 9, Prepare post-closing trial balance. The trap in A: the adjusted trial balance is step 6 — before the statements, not after. B skips a step (you cannot prepare a post-closing trial balance before you close). And D is the optional step 10 in the next period.

4. A company reports: Cash $8,000; Accounts receivable $12,000; Prepaid insurance $4,000; Land $50,000; Accounts payable $10,000; Unearned revenue $6,000; Notes payable (due in five years) $30,000. Its current ratio is:
  1. 0.52
  2. 1.25
  3. 1.50
  4. 2.40
Answer

C. Current assets 8,000 + 12,000 + 4,000 = 24,000 (Land is a plant asset, so it is out). Current liabilities 10,000 + 6,000 = 16,000 (the note payable due in five years is long-term, so it is out). 24,000 ÷ 16,000 = 1.50. The trap in A: it divided by all liabilities (24,000 ÷ 46,000). B forgot that prepaid insurance is a current asset (20,000 ÷ 16,000). And D forgot that unearned revenue is a current liability (24,000 ÷ 10,000).

5. Company X reports net income of $45,000 and net sales of $300,000. Its competitor, Company Y, has a profit margin of 12%. Which statement is correct?
  1. X's profit margin is 15%, so X generates more profit on each dollar of sales
  2. X's profit margin is 15%, so Y generates more profit on each dollar of sales
  3. X's profit margin is 6.67, so X generates more profit on each dollar of sales
  4. X's profit margin is 85%, so X generates more profit on each dollar of sales
Answer

A. Profit margin = Net income ÷ Net sales = 45,000 ÷ 300,000 = 15%, and 15% is bigger than 12% → X makes more profit from each dollar of sales. The trap in B: the math is right, but the comparison is reversed. C flipped the fraction (300,000 ÷ 45,000). And D subtracted the ratio from 100% — that is the part that went to expenses, not the profit.
