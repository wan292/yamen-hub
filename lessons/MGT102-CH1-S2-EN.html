Chapter 1 — The Four Functions of Management

MGT 102 · Chapter 1 · Section 2 of 4: The Four Functions of Management
Objective LO1-2: Distinguish among planning, organizing, leading, and controlling (the four principal managerial functions), and explain how managers' ability to handle each one affects organizational performance.

This section is the backbone of the whole course

The four functions — planning, organizing, leading, controlling — are not just one lesson in Chapter 1. The whole book is built on them: every later chapter explains one of them in detail. And if an exam question gives you a scenario and asks "the manager did this… which function is it?", this section is the answer.

Read the lesson twice. The second time, focus on the difference between each function and the others — that is what they test.

The Four Functions of Management
Memorize the four in order before you continue
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A manager's whole job comes down to four functions that run in a cycle: plan → organize → lead → control → and back to the start.
🧠
Picture a camping trip: make the plan, split people and bags between the cars, hype them up on the road, then check everyone arrived.
EXPLANATION

The book says the job of management is to help the organization use its resources the best way to achieve its goals. How? Managers do this through four essential managerial functions:

#FunctionIn ArabicWhat the manager does, in short
1PlanningالتخطيطChooses the goals and decides how to reach them
2OrganizingالتنظيمArranges people, departments, and working relationships so they work together
3LeadingالقيادةMakes the vision clear and fires up the employees
4ControllingالرقابةMeasures performance and corrects mistakes

The first person to write down these functions was the French manager Henri Fayol, around the year 1900, in his book General and Industrial Management. The textbook still calls it the classic statement of what managers must do to build a high-performing organization.

Figure 1.2 — Four Functions of Management

This is Figure 1.2 in the book. The arrows show the sequence managers usually follow: planning → organizing → leading → controlling, and controlling sends you back to planning. Memorize the order and the number next to each one.

Three points the book insists on:

  • Managers at all levels and in all departments — small or large company, for-profit or not-for-profit, local or global — all of them are responsible for the four.
  • How well managers perform these functions determines how efficient and effective the organization is (go back to lesson 1 if you forgot the difference).
  • At all levels in the managerial hierarchy, effective management means performing these four activities successfully — in ways that increase efficiency and effectiveness.

Example from the book (the Manager as a Person box): Bernard Kim, CEO of Match Group (the owner of Tinder and Hinge): he resets the planning strategies to attract new subscribers; the organizing keeps changing (he announced Tinder's CEO was stepping down after weak results, and the team is reviewing future plans for Tinder); he treats innovation and global expansion, like launching Hinge in Germany, as a measure of performance; and he applies controlling to workforce and marketing costs. The book says his approach to all four functions guides the company through a hard time.

EXAM BOX

The four principal managerial functions are planning, organizing, leading, and controlling. Managers at all levels of the organization and in all departments perform these functions. Effective management means managing these activities successfully.

How well managers perform these functions determines how efficient and effective their organizations are. French manager Henri Fayol first outlined the nature of these managerial activities around the turn of the 20th century in General and Industrial Management.

TRAP
  • The number is four. Any choice that gives you five functions, or adds a word like negotiating, motivating, or staffing as a separate function, is wrong.
  • The order: planning → organizing → leading → controlling. If the question says "the function that comes after organizing", the answer is leading.
  • "Only top managers" = wrong. The book is clear: all levels and all departments.
  • Fayol = the four functions. Mintzberg = the ten roles (the last concept in this lesson). Do not mix the two names.

Planning and Organizing
The first decides "what we want"; the second arranges "who works with whom"
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Planning = you decide the goals and the road to them. Organizing = you build the structure that makes people work together to get there.
🧠
Picture a contractor: first he draws the blueprint and sets the budget (planning), then he assigns the workers and decides who reports to whom (organizing).
EXPLANATION — PLANNING

Planning: the manager identifies and selects appropriate goals and courses of action, and develops strategies to reach high performance. The book splits it into three steps — memorize them in order:

  1. Decide which goals the organization will pursue.
  2. Decide what strategies to adopt to attain those goals.
  3. Decide how to allocate resources to pursue those strategies.

The quality of planning determines the organization's performance level — its efficiency and effectiveness.

Example from the book: Airbnb, when it decided to let its employees work from anywhere. CEO Brian Chesky tested the idea himself, counted the costs (payroll administration and more complex taxes), weighed them against the benefits (attracting more employees, plus revenue from other companies whose workers would book long stays through Airbnb), and set objectives for the periods when employees must be in the office. This is planning step by step: goal, strategy, resources.

EXPLANATION — ORGANIZING

Organizing: structuring working relationships so the members of the organization interact and cooperate to achieve the goals. That means: putting people into departments according to the kind of work they do, and laying out the lines of authority and responsibility — who gives orders to whom, and who is responsible for what. The most important resource the manager arranges here is human resources.

The outcome of organizing is called the organizational structure: a formal system of task and reporting relationships that coordinates and motivates members so they work together. The structure determines how the organization can best use its resources to create goods and services.

Example from the book: during the pandemic, Airbnb's revenues collapsed because people stopped traveling. The CEO rethought the structure: he consolidated departments and reduced operations to the basic business functions, so the company stayed lean until the world returned to normal. That is organizing. (The book covers organizing in detail in Chapters 10–12.)

EXAM BOX

To perform the planning function, managers identify and select appropriate organizational goals and courses of action; they develop strategies for how to achieve high performance. The three steps involved in planning are deciding which goals the organization will pursue, deciding what strategies to adopt to attain those goals, and deciding how to allocate organizational resources to pursue the strategies that attain those goals.

Organizing is structuring working relationships so organizational members interact and cooperate to achieve organizational goals. The outcome of organizing is the creation of an organizational structure, a formal system of task and reporting relationships that coordinates and motivates members so they work together to achieve organizational goals.

TRAP
  • "Allocate resources" in this section is the third step of planning, not organizing. A choice that puts deciding how to allocate resources under organizing is wrong.
  • Organizational structure is the outcome of organizing — not of planning, and not of leading.
  • Planning vs. controlling: both contain the word goals. The difference is the verb: choose / select / decide = planning; evaluate / measure / monitor = controlling.
  • Planning has three steps, in order: goals → strategies → resources. A "how many steps" question does come.
🔥
Leading and Controlling
The first fires up the people; the second measures and corrects
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Leading = you make the vision clear and charge up the employees. Controlling = you measure whether the goals were reached, and correct if they were not.
🧠
Picture a team captain: before the match he fires up the players (leading); after it he checks the score and fixes the mistakes (controlling).
EXPLANATION — LEADING

Before leading, you need the word vision: a short, succinct, and inspiring statement of what the organization wants to become and the goals it seeks — its desired future state. Exactly like Vision 2030: one short sentence that pulls people in.

Leading: the manager articulates a clear vision for the members, and energizes and enables the employees so everyone understands their part in achieving the goals. The leader's tools, according to the book: power, personality, influence, persuasion, and communication skills — used to coordinate people and groups so their efforts are in harmony. Leadership is all about encouraging all employees to perform at a high level.

The outcome of leading: a highly motivated and committed workforce. Example from the book: Airbnb's employees appreciate the values of their leadership, especially Chesky's creative way of handling the work-from-anywhere policy. (Leading in detail: Chapters 13–16.)

EXPLANATION — CONTROLLING

Controlling: the manager evaluates how well the organization achieved its goals, and takes corrective actions to maintain or improve performance. How?

  • Monitors the performance of individuals, departments, and the whole organization: are they meeting the desired performance standards?
  • Decides which goals to measure — for example productivity, quality, or responsiveness to customers.
  • Then designs control systems that give the information needed to assess performance: to what degree were the goals met?

The outcome of controlling: the ability to measure performance accurately and regulate the organization's efficiency and effectiveness. And a point they love to ask about: controlling helps the manager evaluate his own performance in the other three functions (planning, organizing, leading) and correct it. That is why the arrow in the figure goes back from controlling to planning.

EXAM BOX

An organization's vision is a short, succinct, and inspiring statement of what the organization intends to become and the goals it is seeking to achieve—its desired future state. In leading, managers articulate a clear organizational vision for the organization's members to accomplish, and they energize and enable employees so everyone understands the part they play in achieving organizational goals. Another outcome of leadership is a highly motivated and committed workforce.

In controlling, the function of managers is to evaluate how well an organization has achieved its goals and to take any corrective actions needed to maintain or improve performance. The outcome of the control process is the ability to measure performance accurately and regulate organizational efficiency and effectiveness. The controlling function also helps managers evaluate how well they themselves are performing the other three functions of management—planning, organizing, and leading—and take corrective action.

TRAP
  • The word vision comes under leading in this book, not under planning. A choice that says "articulating the vision = planning" is wrong.
  • Leading vs. organizing: both are about people. But organizing = a formal structure (departments, authority, reporting), and leading = motivation and influence. If the question has energize, motivate, or inspire → leading.
  • "Corrective action" = the fingerprint of controlling. If you see it in a scenario, the answer is controlling.
  • Controlling is not separate from the rest: it evaluates the other three functions. A choice that says controlling is independent of the other functions is wrong.
  • The two outcomes: leading → motivated and committed workforce. controlling → measure performance accurately. Do not swap them.

Mintzberg's Managerial Roles
Ten roles in three categories — the count itself is a question
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Mintzberg watched managers hour by hour and came up with ten roles grouped into three categories: decisional, interpersonal, informational.
🧠
Picture one manager's day: cut a ribbon, put out a crisis, split a budget, answer the press — ten hats in one day.
EXPLANATION

Until now the book showed management as an orderly process: you gather information, think, and decide. Henry Mintzberg (a professor at McGill University) was one of the first to show that reality is different. Management is often chaotic, marked by quick decisions in a tense and sometimes emotional environment. The manager is overloaded with responsibilities and has no time to analyze every detail, so he decides under uncertain conditions and relies on intuition and experience to make snap decisions. And a decision that is right today may prove wrong tomorrow.

Mintzberg followed managers and observed what they actually do, hour by hour and day by day, and came up with 10 roles (a role = a set of job responsibilities). He grouped them by the nature of the responsibility into three categories: decisional, interpersonal, and informational. This is Table 1.1 in the book:

That is why experienced managers accept an employee's failure as a normal part of learning and a rite of passage to becoming an effective manager — everyone learns from both successes and failures.

EXAM BOX

Henry Mintzberg was one of the first to show that management is often chaotic, marked by quick decisions in a tense and sometimes emotional environment. Managers often must make snap decisions using the intuition and experience gained through their careers. By following managers and observing what they actually do hour by hour and day by day, Mintzberg identified 10 kinds of specific roles, or sets of job responsibilities, that capture the dynamic nature of managerial work. He grouped these roles according to whether the responsibility is primarily decisional, interpersonal, or informational.

TRAP
  • The count: 10 roles in 3 categories. Decisional 4 (entrepreneur · disturbance handler · resource allocator · negotiator), interpersonal 3 (figurehead · leader · liaison), informational 3 (monitor · disseminator · spokesperson). A "how many roles / how many categories" question comes word for word.
  • The Leader role in Mintzberg (interpersonal category) is not the same as the Leading function in Fayol. If the question says role, it means Mintzberg; if it says function, it means the four.
  • The Monitor role is in the informational category — do not mix it with the controlling function.
  • Three of them talk, but to whom? The Disseminator informs employees inside the company. The Spokesperson talks to the outside (advertising, the community). The Figurehead outlines future goals at meetings, opens a new headquarters, and states the ethical guidelines — and its category is interpersonal, not informational.
  • Mintzberg says management is chaotic and its decisions are quick and intuitive. A choice that says Mintzberg showed that management is an orderly, deliberate process is wrong.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 1 or 4, go back to the trap "planning vs. controlling".

1. A manager spends the morning deciding which goals the company will pursue next year, which strategies to adopt, and how to allocate resources among them. The manager is performing which function?
  1. Organizing
  2. Planning
  3. Leading
  4. Controlling
Answer

B. These are the three steps, word for word, from the definition of planning: goals → strategies → allocate resources. The trap is in A: the words allocate resources make you think of organizing, but the book puts it as the third step of planning. And D is the trap of the word goals — controlling evaluates the goals, it does not choose them.

2. Which of the following is NOT one of the four principal managerial functions?
  1. Planning
  2. Negotiating
  3. Leading
  4. Controlling
Answer

B. Negotiator is one of Mintzberg's roles (decisional category), not one of the four functions. The trap: the question uses NOT, and it gives you three correct functions so you rush and pick one of them. The four are planning, organizing, leading, controlling — nothing else.

3. A CEO gives a speech that lays out a clear picture of what the company intends to become, and energizes employees so each one understands his or her part in reaching that goal. The CEO is best described as performing the function of:
  1. Planning
  2. Organizing
  3. Leading
  4. Controlling
Answer

C. "A clear picture of what the company intends to become" = vision, and "energizes employees so each one understands his or her part" = energize and enable. Both come from the definition of leading. The trap is in A: a vision looks like planning, but the book puts it under leading. And B is a trap: organizing is a formal structure and departments, not a motivating speech.

4. A regional manager compares each store's monthly results with the performance standards set for it, and takes corrective action at the stores that fell short. This is best described as:
  1. Planning
  2. Organizing
  3. Leading
  4. Controlling
Answer

D. Performance standards + corrective action = exactly the fingerprint of controlling: evaluate how well the goals were achieved, and correct. The trap is in A: the question mentions standards and goals, but the manager here does not choose them — he measures whether they were met. Choosing = planning, measuring = controlling.

5. According to Mintzberg, a manager who moves quickly to deal with an unexpected crisis, such as an oil spill, is playing the ____ role, which belongs to the ____ category.
  1. disturbance handler; decisional
  2. monitor; informational
  3. liaison; interpersonal
  4. entrepreneur; decisional
Answer

A. "Moves quickly to deal with an unexpected problem like an oil spill" is the book's own example of the disturbance handler, a decisional role. The trap is in B: the word crisis pulls you to monitor, but the monitor evaluates managers' performance and watches the environment — it does not put out fires. And D is a trap: entrepreneur is in the same category (decisional) but it is about innovation and expansion. And remember the count: 10 roles, 3 categories.
