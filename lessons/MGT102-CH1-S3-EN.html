Chapter 1 — Levels and Skills of Managers

MGT 102 · Chapter 1 · Section 3 of 4: Levels and Skills of Managers
Objective LO1-3: Differentiate among three levels of management, and understand the tasks and responsibilities of managers at different levels in the organizational hierarchy.
Objective LO1-4: Distinguish among three kinds of managerial skill, and explain why managers are divided into different departments to perform their tasks more efficiently and effectively.

This lesson is all easy numbers: 3 levels, 3 skills, 4 functions

In the previous section you learned the four functions. Now the question: do all managers do the same thing? No. The book divides managers in two ways: by their level in the hierarchy, and by their type of skill and department.

The exam questions for this section are all "scenarios": they give you a person and ask which level he is, or which skill he is using. So read the book's examples carefully, because they appear in the answer choices with the same words.

You already met these ideas in Arabic. This page gives you the same ideas in English, because your textbook and your exam are in English.

Three Levels of Management
The pyramid — memorize who is above whom
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Managers are arranged in a pyramid of three layers: first-line at the bottom, middle in the middle, top at the top — and each layer is under the one above it.
🧠
Picture a restaurant: the chef watching the cooks daily = first-line, the branch manager arranging resources = middle, the owner deciding what to sell = top.
EXPLANATION

The book says organizations normally have three levels of management, arranged in a hierarchy of authority. The order from the bottom up: first-line managers report to middle managers, and middle managers report to top managers. Each level has different but related duties, and all of them use the organization's resources to increase efficiency and effectiveness.

LevelWho is under them? What is their job?Examples from the book
First-line managers
also called supervisors		 They supervise the nonmanagerial employees who actually produce the goods and services. Their job is daily supervision of these employees. They work in all departments. the supervisor of a work team in the manufacturing department of a car plant · the head nurse in the obstetrics department of a hospital · the chief mechanic overseeing a crew of mechanics in a car dealership
Middle managers They supervise the first-line managers. Their job: finding the best way to organize resources (human and other) to achieve the organization's goals. For efficiency: they help first-line managers and employees use resources better (reduce manufacturing costs, improve customer service). For effectiveness: they evaluate whether the goals are appropriate and suggest to top managers how goals should be changed. A big part of their job is developing skills and know-how, such as manufacturing or marketing expertise. Behind a top-notch sales force, there is a middle manager who trains, encourages, and rewards the salespeople · behind a committed staff of teachers, there is the principal who energizes them
Top managers They supervise the middle managers in all departments. They are responsible for the performance of all departments — they have cross-departmental responsibility. They establish the organization's goals (which goods and services to produce), decide how the departments should interact, and monitor how well middle managers use resources. They are ultimately responsible for the success or failure of the organization, and their performance is continually scrutinized by people inside and outside the organization — employees and investors. CEO · COO · vice presidents

Who are the CEO and the COO?

  • Chief executive officer (CEO) = the company's most senior and important manager, the one all the other top managers report to.
  • Chief operating officer (COO) = today the term usually means the top manager who is being groomed to take over as CEO when the current CEO retires or leaves.
  • Together, the CEO and the COO are responsible for building good working relationships among the top managers of the different departments (manufacturing and marketing, for example). These top managers usually have the title vice president.
  • Top management team = a group made up of the CEO, the COO, and the vice presidents most responsible for achieving the organization's goals. Creating a smoothly functioning top management team is a central concern of the CEO.
Figure 1.3 — Levels of Managers

The pyramid from top to bottom: CEO → Top managers → Middle managers → First-line managers, and under them the nonmanagerial employees (not a management level). The arrow means: each layer reports to the one above it.

EXAM BOX

Organizations typically have three levels of management. At the base of the managerial hierarchy are first-line managers, often called supervisors. They are responsible for daily supervision of the nonmanagerial employees who perform the specific activities necessary to produce goods and services. Middle managers are responsible for finding the best way to organize human and other resources to achieve organizational goals. Top managers are responsible for the performance of all departments. They have cross-departmental responsibility. Top managers establish organizational goals, decide how the different departments should interact, and monitor how well middle managers in each department use resources to achieve goals. The top management team is a group composed of the CEO, the COO, and the vice presidents most responsible for achieving organizational goals.

TRAP
  • The levels are three — do not count the CEO as a fourth level. The CEO is the highest person inside the top managers.
  • First-line supervises the nonmanagerial employees. Middle supervises the first-line managers. The question swaps them: if it says "daily supervision of employees", the answer is first-line, not middle.
  • Cross-departmental responsibility = top only. A middle manager is responsible for his own department.
  • "Suggest how goals should be changed" = middle. "Establish goals" = top. The difference: suggest ≠ set.
  • COO is not CEO. The COO is being groomed to become CEO. And the top management team has no middle managers in it.

Time Spent on the Four Functions
Higher up = more planning; lower down = more leading
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The higher you go in the hierarchy, the more time you spend on planning and organizing. The lower you go, the more time you spend on leading and controlling.
🧠
Picture a ladder: the top person looks far ahead and plans for years; the bottom person watches each worker's hands every day.
EXPLANATION

The relative importance of the four functions (planning, organizing, leading, controlling) to any manager depends on his position in the hierarchy. The book says: the time a manager spends planning and organizing increases as he ascends the hierarchy.

  • Top managers devote most of their time to planning and organizing — because these functions are crucial for determining the organization's long-term performance.
  • The lower a manager's position is in the hierarchy, the more time he spends leading and controlling first-line managers or nonmanagerial employees.

Careful: this does not mean first-line managers never plan. From the previous section you know: all levels perform the four functions — the difference here is only the amount of time.

Figure 1.4 — Relative Amount of Time Managers Spend on the Four Managerial Functions

Read the rows: for Top managers the longest bars are Planning and Organizing. For First-line managers the longest bars are Leading and Controlling. Middle is roughly in between. The lengths here are relative, like the book's drawing — not exact numbers.

EXAM BOX

The relative importance of planning, organizing, leading, and controlling to any manager depends on the manager's position in the managerial hierarchy. The amount of time managers spend planning and organizing resources to maintain and improve organizational performance increases as they ascend the hierarchy. Top managers devote most of their time to planning and organizing, the functions so crucial to determining an organization's long-term performance. The lower managers' positions are in the hierarchy, the more time they spend leading and controlling first-line managers or nonmanagerial employees.

TRAP
  • "The time spent on which two functions increases as a manager moves up?" → planning and organizing. The wrong choice will offer leading and controlling (these increase as you move down).
  • Do not choose "first-line managers do not plan" — all of them do the four functions. The difference is relative.
  • Link it: planning + organizing = long-term performance = top managers. You will need this again for conceptual skills.

Three Types of Managerial Skills
conceptual · human · technical — missing one = failure
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A good manager has three skills: he understands the big picture (conceptual), he knows how to deal with people (human), and he knows his own specialized job (technical).
🧠
Picture a restaurant manager: he works out why sales fell (conceptual), keeps the cooks happy (human), and cooks when the cook is absent (technical).
EXPLANATION

The book says education and experience help managers acquire and develop three types of skills: conceptual, human, and technical.

SkillDefinition (the book's words) — and who needs it most?Example
Conceptual skills
the big-picture skill		 The general ability to analyze and diagnose a situation and to distinguish between cause and effect. In other words: to see the big picture and think about the long-term goals. It develops through formal education (a bachelor's degree, an MBA) and the study of management.
Who needs it most: Top managers need the best conceptual skills because their primary responsibilities are planning and organizing.		 Brian Chesky (Airbnb) identifies new opportunities and mobilizes resources to take advantage of them. In the Managing Globally box: Salma Okonkwo, CEO of Blue Power in Ghana, saw foreign investors extracting resources without building infrastructure, so she decided to build it herself.
Human skills
the people skill		 The general ability to understand, alter, lead, and control the behavior of other individuals and groups. It includes communicating, coordinating, motivating, and molding individuals into a cohesive team. Like conceptual skills, it is learned through education and training and developed through experience. It needs empathy (understanding the other person's viewpoint) and feedback from your superiors, peers, and employees.
Who needs it most: everyone. The book says human skills distinguish effective from ineffective managers.		 The manager who can get his team to work as one team. Organizations use leadership programs to get the advantages of self-managed teams.
Technical skills
the job-specific skill		 The job-specific skills required to perform a particular type of work at a high level. Examples: manufacturing, accounting, marketing, and technological skills.
Who needs it most: all managers; the set of technical skills they need depends on their position.		 The restaurant manager: cooks when the cook is absent, keeps the accounts and the payroll (accounting and bookkeeping), and keeps the restaurant looking attractive for customers.

The rule that comes in the exam: effective managers need all three skills, and the absence of even one type can lead to failure. The book's examples:

  • Someone who starts a small business: he has technical skills but lacks conceptual and human skills — he knows the trade, but not how to manage the venture.
  • A scientist or engineer who switches from research to management: his biggest problem is a lack of human skills.

That is why companies invest in training their managers: Microsoft, IBM, and Oracle give every manager a budget for development programs, and Walgreens, Enterprise, and Goldman Sachs opened their own colleges to train their employees. The book says: a company's willingness to invest in a manager's skill development is a signal that he is performing well. And promotion is linked to the competencies the company thinks are important: at Apple and 3M, the ability to lead a new product development team; at Accenture and IBM, the ability to attract and retain clients.

EXAM BOX

Education and experience help managers acquire and develop three types of skills: conceptual, human, and technical. Conceptual skills are demonstrated in the general ability to analyze and diagnose a situation and to distinguish between cause and effect. Top managers require the best conceptual skills because their primary responsibilities are planning and organizing. Human skills include the general ability to understand, alter, lead, and control the behavior of other individuals and groups. Technical skills are the job-specific skills required to perform a particular type of work or occupation at a high level. Effective managers need all three kinds of skills; the absence of even one type of managerial skill can lead to failure.

TRAP
  • Conceptual ≠ technical. Analysis and big picture = conceptual. Specialized work (accounting, cooking, coding) = technical. The choices swap them.
  • The words job-specific in the question = technical, always.
  • "Top managers require the best ___ skills" → conceptual, not technical.
  • "Cause and effect" = conceptual. "Behavior of individuals and groups" = human.
  • The skills are three. If you see a choice like "decisional skills" or "interpersonal skills", those are names of roles from Mintzberg's table in Section 2, not skills.
  • Someone with technical skills only = no guarantee he can manage the business. The answer: he lacks conceptual and human skills.

Departments and Core Competency
Same skills → one department; a winning skill set → core competency
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
People with the same technical skill are grouped in one department, and inside every department there are all three levels. If a department's skills make the company better than its rivals, that is a core competency.
🧠
Picture a hospital: nurses in one department, accountants in another, each with its own top, middle, and first-line. Its famous heart unit = core competency.
EXPLANATION

The second way to divide managers (after level) is by skill: the organization groups managers into departments (or functions) according to their job-specific skills, expertise, and experience — engineering skills, marketing expertise, sales experience.

A department = a group of managers and employees who work together because they possess similar skills and experience, or use the same kind of knowledge, tools, or techniques to do their jobs. Examples: the manufacturing, accounting, engineering, or sales department. And inside each department are all three levels of management.

Why group them into departments?

  • People with the same technical skills become members of one department and are called, for example, marketing managers or manufacturing managers.
  • A major part of a manager's responsibility is to monitor, train, and supervise employees so their job-specific skills and expertise increase — and this is easier when employees with similar skills are in the same department, because they learn from one another and become more skilled and productive.
  • The book's example: when the head of manufacturing has no time to supervise computer assembly, he recruits experienced manufacturing middle managers from other companies to take on this responsibility.
Figure 1.5 — Types and Levels of Managers

The figure joins the two ideas: the horizontal layers = the three levels, and the vertical slices = the departments. Notice: every department contains all three levels from top to bottom.

Core competency = the specific set of a department's skills, knowledge, and experience that lets one organization outperform its competitors. In other words, departmental skills that create a core competency give the organization a competitive advantage.

CompanyCore competencyResult
Dellmaterials management — the first PC maker to develop it produces PCs at a much lower cost than its competitors
Googleresearch and development (R&D) innovates new products and services at a faster rate than its competitors — from AI to self-driving cars
EXAM BOX

A department, such as the manufacturing, accounting, engineering, or sales department, is a group of managers and employees who work together because they possess similar skills and experience or use the same kind of knowledge, tools, or techniques to perform their jobs. Within each department are all three levels of management. Managers are grouped into different departments because a major part of a manager's responsibility is to monitor, train, and supervise employees so their job-specific skills and expertise increase. The term core competency is often used to refer to the specific set of departmental skills, knowledge, and experience that allows one organization to outperform its competitors. Departmental skills that create a core competency give an organization a competitive advantage.

TRAP
  • A department is not a level. A department contains all three levels. Any choice that says "departments contain only first-line managers" is wrong.
  • Departments are built on technical (job-specific) skills — not on conceptual or human skills.
  • Core competency = the skills a department has. Competitive advantage = the result. The question gives you the Dell example and asks "this is best described as" → core competency.
  • "Why are managers grouped into departments?" → so they learn from one another and their job-specific skills increase. Not "to reduce the number of managers".

Test yourself

English, like the exam. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 1 or 4, go back to the tables above.

1. Fatimah is the head nurse in the obstetrics department of a hospital. She spends her day supervising the nurses who care for patients. Fatimah is best described as a:
  1. Top manager
  2. Middle manager
  3. First-line manager
  4. Member of the top management team
Answer

C. This is the book's own example, word for word: a head nurse = a first-line manager, because she supervises nonmanagerial employees daily. The trap is B: a middle manager supervises the first-line managers, not the nurses themselves. D is a second trap — the top management team is only the CEO, the COO, and the vice presidents.

2. Which of the following is a responsibility of top managers but NOT of middle managers?
  1. Daily supervision of nonmanagerial employees
  2. Cross-departmental responsibility for the performance of all departments
  3. Finding the best way to organize resources within a department
  4. Evaluating whether goals are appropriate and suggesting changes
Answer

B. Cross-departmental responsibility is the key phrase for top managers only. A is first-line work. C and D are middle-manager work — notice: the middle manager suggests changing the goals, and top management establishes them. The trap: the question has NOT in it, so do not pick the first thing that belongs to middle managers.

3. As managers move up the organizational hierarchy, the amount of time they spend on which two functions increases?
  1. Leading and controlling
  2. Planning and organizing
  3. Organizing and leading
  4. Planning and controlling
Answer

B. The higher a manager goes, the more time he spends on planning and organizing (the functions that determine long-term performance). The trap is A: leading and controlling increase as a manager moves down, not up. C and D mix one function from each pair.

4. Khalid can look at a complex business situation, see the big picture, and distinguish between cause and effect. Khalid is demonstrating:
  1. Technical skills
  2. Human skills
  3. Conceptual skills
  4. A core competency
Answer

C. "Analyze and diagnose", "cause and effect", and "big picture" are all conceptual-skill words. The trap is A: technical = a skill for one specific job (accounting, cooking). B = people's behavior. D is a different kind of trap: a core competency describes a department or an organization, not a person.

5. Dell was the first PC maker to develop a set of departmental skills in materials management that allowed it to produce PCs at a much lower cost than its competitors. This set of skills is best described as:
  1. A department
  2. Technical skills of the CEO
  3. A top management team
  4. A core competency
Answer

D. A department's skills that let the company outperform its competitors = a core competency, and this is the book's Dell example. The trap is A: the department is the group of people, not the skill. B turns a whole department's skill into one person's skill. C has nothing to do with it.
