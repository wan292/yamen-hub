Chapter 6 — The General Environment

MGT 102 · Chapter 6 · Section 2 of 4: The General Environment
Objective LO6-2: Differentiate between the global task and global general environments.
Objective LO6-3: Identify the main forces in the global task and general environments, and describe the challenges that each force presents to managers.

This section is “the big circle: five forces far away from you that still move everything around you”

In the previous lesson you saw the near circle — the task environment with its four forces: suppliers, distributors, customers, and competitors. Now we go out to the wider circle: the general environment with its five forces: economic, technological, sociocultural, demographic, and political and legal. These forces do not knock on your door every day, but they move the four near forces — and many times the manager does not even notice them. This lesson completes LO6-2 (the difference between the two environments) and covers the second half of LO6-3 (the forces of the general environment and the challenge each one brings).

From now on, watch for five things that trip students up here: (1) which force belongs to the task environment and which belongs to the general environment, (2) the difference between technology (the combination itself) and technological forces (the outcomes of changes in it), (3) the two sources of sociocultural forces: social structure and national culture, (4) demographic forces include changing attitudes, not only changing characteristics, (5) deregulation against privatization. And take the English definitions word for word from the EXAM BOX.

The General Environment
The wider circle: five forces that move the task environment itself, and they are harder to see
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The general environment = five wide forces — economic, technological, sociocultural, demographic, and political and legal — that affect the organization and its task environment. Their effect usually reaches you indirectly, and the manager may not notice it.
🧠
The weather does not knock on your shop's door, but it changes vegetable prices at your supplier and the number of customers at your door.

EXPLANATION — THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE: THE 2007–2008 CRISIS (MEMORIZE THE CHAIN)

The book opens the section with a point: the general forces often have important effects on the task environment forces that decide an organization's ability to obtain resources — effects that managers may not be aware of. The example:

StageWhat happened
The causes (3 together) 1. complex new financial lending instruments called derivatives
2. a speculative boom in commodities and housing prices
3. lax government regulation that let unethical bankers and financial managers exploit the derivatives to make immense short-term profits
Start and peak the sudden, dramatic upheavals in the mortgage and banking industry started in 2007, and the economic crisis peaked in 2008 but continued to ripple through the world economy for years
The results stock markets around the world plummeted · the retirement savings of hundreds of millions of ordinary people were devastated · millions of employees were laid off because companies slashed their workforces after customers reduced their spending
After that sound economic policies during the recession helped the economy rebound strongly, although the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic continue to take their toll on the global economy

Notice how general forces (lax government regulation + a price boom) went down until they reached the near circle: customers spent less, and companies laid off their employees. This is exactly the meaning of “affect an organization and its task environment”.

The lesson the book states clearly (The implication is clear): the manager must continuously analyze the forces of the general environment, because they affect ongoing decision making and planning, and how well he does this task decides how quickly the organization can respond to the changes taking place.

EXAM BOX

The general environment comprises wide-ranging global economic, technological, sociocultural, demographic, political, and legal forces that affect an organization and its task environment.

For the individual manager, opportunities and threats resulting from changes in the general environment are often more difficult to identify and respond to than are events in the task environment.

Economic, technological, sociocultural, demographic, political, and legal forces in the general environment often have important effects on forces in the task environment that determine an organization's ability to obtain resources—effects that managers may not be aware of.

The implication is clear: Managers must continuously analyze forces in the general environment because these forces affect ongoing decision making and planning. How well managers can perform this task determines how quickly an organization can respond to the changes taking place.

TRAP
  • Task = 4 (suppliers, distributors, customers, competitors) and General = 5. Any choice that puts customers, competitors, or distributors with the general forces is wrong, and any choice that puts interest rates or demographic forces in the task environment is wrong.
  • Global environment ≠ general environment. The first is everything (task + general), and the second is only a part of it. The two words look alike — read the question slowly.
  • Political and legal = one force with one heading in the book. If you are asked “how many forces are in the general environment?”, the answer is 5, even though the definition writes political and legal as two words.
  • The general environment affects the organization and its task environment — a choice that says “it affects only the organization and has nothing to do with suppliers and customers” is wrong.
  • What affects you daily, directly, and in short-term decisions = task. What is harder to identify and affects ongoing decision making and planning = general.
  • The causes of the 2007–2008 crisis are three together: derivatives + speculative boom + lax government regulation. It started in 2007 and peaked in 2008 — do not swap them.

Economic Forces
The health of the economy: a good economy opens opportunities, a bad economy makes resources hard to get
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Economic forces = the health of the economy: interest rates, inflation, unemployment, and economic growth. A good economy = opportunities (people spend more and resources are cheaper), and a bad economy = a threat (resources get harder to get and customers get fewer).
🧠
When salaries are good and loans are cheap, restaurants are full; when a recession comes, dinner out is the first thing people cut.

EXPLANATION — the Management Insight box: Nollywood's a Hit in a Developing Economy
  • The question the book opens with: which filmmaking center produces 1,500 movies per year, is worth over $3 billion, and employs a million people? The answer: Nollywood — the nickname of the Nigerian film industry.
  • The start (1992): an electronics salesman named Kenneth Nnebue bought a large quantity of blank videocassettes, and he knew they would sit on store shelves because his customers had no way to use a blank cassette. So he worked with the seller to record something on them: they wrote a script, hired a producer, director, and actors, and made a movie that sold hundreds of thousands of copies in Nigeria and other African countries.
  • The smart part: Nnebue saw that the local market had no movie theaters but plenty of people who enjoyed a good story on film. His early profitability encouraged banks to lend and created a market for this kind of entertainment, and the demand for videos led to demand for actors and the establishment of film schools.
  • The growth: it became the second-largest center of film production in terms of output, after Bollywood in India. It expanded its audience from Nigeria to the rest of Africa, and kept up with economic conditions and advances in technology.
  • The problem: piracy of intellectual property — a popular movie can actually lose money because people buy illegally made DVDs.
  • The solution: the studio EbonyLife Films made Nollywood's first deal with Netflix to stream its hit Chief Daddy to a global market of 149 million customers in 190 countries. Netflix also bought its first original Nollywood movie: Lionheart.
  • The lesson for a manager evaluating the economic environment: a developing nation may lack many things people think a business needs — a transportation network, a trustworthy court system, supportive financial institutions. But Nnebue saw an opportunity that could bring the prosperity needed to change the environment itself. Instead of entering an existing market, he helped create one by focusing on the needs of his local community and offering an affordable product.
EXAM BOX

Economic forces affect the general health and well-being of a country or world region. They include interest rates, inflation, unemployment, and economic growth. Economic forces produce many opportunities and threats for managers. Low levels of unemployment and falling interest rates give people more money to spend, and as a result organizations can sell more goods and services.

In contrast, worsening macroeconomic conditions, like those in 2020–2021 during the global pandemic, pose a major threat because they reduce managers' ability to gain access to the resources their organizations need to survive and prosper.

Poor economic conditions make the environment more complex and managers' jobs more difficult and demanding.

TRAP
  • Economic forces give opportunities and threats — not only threats.
  • The four parts by name: interest rates · inflation · unemployment · economic growth. If you see aging population, it is demographic; if you see deregulation or a tariff, it is political and legal.
  • The opportunity comes from low unemployment + falling interest rates. A choice that says “rising interest rates give people more money” is upside down.
  • The 2020 numbers: hotels more than 70%, donations double digits — and even nonprofits were hit, not only profit-seeking companies.
  • Nollywood: 1992, Kenneth Nnebue, blank cassettes; the second-largest after Bollywood (not the first); Chief Daddy = the first Netflix deal (149 million · 190 countries), and Lionheart = the first original movie. The lesson: create a market, not enter an existing one.

Technological Forces
Technology = the combination itself; technological forces = the outcomes of changes in it, a threat and an opportunity together
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Technology = the combination itself (tools, machines, skills, knowledge…) that you use to design, produce, and distribute, and technological forces = the outcomes of changes in this combination. This change is a threat (products become old) and an opportunity (new products) together.
🧠
The video-rental shop: the same technology that closed it opened the streaming apps.

EXAM BOX

Technology is the combination of tools, machines, computers, skills, information, and knowledge that managers use to design, produce, and distribute goods and services; technological forces are outcomes of changes in that technology.

Technological change can make established products obsolete—for example, cathode-ray tube (CRT) computer monitors and televisions, bound sets of encyclopedias, and newspapers and magazines—forcing managers to find new ways to satisfy customer needs. Although technological change can threaten an organization, it also can create a host of new opportunities for designing, making, or distributing new and better kinds of goods and services.

Changes in technology are altering the nature of work itself within organizations, including that of the manager's job.

TRAP
  • Technology ≠ technological forces. The first = the combination (combination of skills and equipment), and the second = the outcomes of changes (outcomes of changes). The trap puts one definition on the other one's name.
  • The definition of technology has two correct forms: the margin (skills and equipment) and the main text (tools, machines, computers, skills, information, and knowledge). Do not be surprised by either one.
  • Technological forces are a threat and an opportunity together. A choice that says “they only threaten organizations” or “they only help” is wrong.
  • The examples of obsolete products are three: CRT · bound encyclopedias · newspapers and magazines. And the example of an opportunity: AMD Ryzen in 2022.
  • Telecommuting, videoconferencing, and cloud computing = a technological effect on the nature of work — not a sociocultural or a demographic force.

Sociocultural Forces
Pressures from two sources: social structure and national culture, and both change over time
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Sociocultural forces = pressures that come from two sources: social structure (the traditional system of relationships between people and groups) and national culture (values and norms of behavior). These pressures can constrain or facilitate the way the organization works and the way the manager behaves.
🧠
A pork-sausage advert flopped in Riyadh, and a family-meal ad that works in Seoul falls flat in Brooklyn: same product, different values.

EXPLANATION — SOURCE 1: SOCIAL STRUCTURE AND SOCIAL STRATIFICATION

Societies differ a lot in their social structure. The measure the book uses is social stratification — the number of distinctions between individuals and groups:

High stratificationLow stratification
What it meansmany distinctions between individuals and groups → a multilayered social structure the social structure reveals few distinctions among people
The book's examplescaste systems in India and Tibet, and the recognition of numerous social classes in Great Britain and France New Zealand (relatively egalitarian) and the United States

Societies also differ in how much they put the individual over the group, and this difference may decide how the manager needs to motivate and lead his employees.

EXPLANATION — SOURCE 2: NATIONAL CULTURE, AND CHANGE INSIDE A SOCIETY
  • Societies differ in the values and norms they emphasize. The book's example: in the United States individualism is highly valued, but in Korea and Japan a person is expected to conform to group expectations.
  • National culture affects how the manager motivates and coordinates employees, and how organizations do business. Ethics is an important aspect of it, and you studied it in Chapter 4. The details of national culture (Hofstede and others) come in lesson 4 of this chapter.
  • The important point: social structure and national culture do not only differ across societies — they also change inside the same society over time. The book's examples (3):
    1. In the United States: attitudes toward the roles of women, sex, marriage, and the LGBTQ community changed in each past decade.
    2. In Asian countries such as Hong Kong, Singapore, Korea, and even China: many people think the younger generation is far more individualistic than the ones before it.
    3. In much of Eastern Europe: new values that emphasize individualism and entrepreneurship are replacing communist values based on collectivism and obedience to the state.
    And the pace of change continues to speed up.
  • What the manager must do: be responsive to the changes in social structures and national cultures — and to the differences between them — in every country where he works. Today the manager often deals with people from several countries, and many managers live and work abroad. The effective manager is sensitive to differences between societies and adjusts his behavior to fit them.
EXPLANATION — THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE: THE HEALTH AND FITNESS WAVE IN THE UNITED STATES
  • In the last decades Americans have become more and more interested in their health and fitness. The managers who saw this trend early and used its opportunities won big gains for their organizations — such as organic food and meal-kit delivery services.
  • The organic produce industry has been growing for ten years because of people's interest in healthy food. Some companies deliver organic fruits, vegetables, and meats to homes every week, and others sell complete meal kits that take away the stress and time of planning the menu and shopping. The book's photo names Blue Apron as one of these online meal-kit companies.
  • But: getting portioned ingredients delivered to your front door is not cheap — a recent study found that buying meal kits is five times more expensive than shopping at a local store.
EXAM BOX

Sociocultural forces are pressures emanating from the social structure of a country or society or from the national culture, such as the concern for diversity, discussed in the previous chapter. Pressures from both sources can either constrain or facilitate the way organizations operate and managers behave. Social structure is the traditional system of relationships established between people and groups in a society.

National culture is the set of values that a society considers important and the norms of behavior that are approved or sanctioned in that society.

Social structure and national culture not only differ across societies but also change within societies over time.

Effective managers are sensitive to differences between societies and adjust their behavior accordingly.

TRAP
  • The sources are exactly two: social structure + national culture. A choice that says “from national culture only” is incomplete; and national culture is a part of sociocultural forces, not another name for them.
  • Social structure = a system of relationships (classes, stratification). National culture = values and norms. Classes and caste → social structure; individualism and conforming to the group → national culture.
  • High stratification: India, Tibet, Great Britain, France. Low: New Zealand and the United States. The trap flips them.
  • The pressures constrain or facilitate — not “only constrain”.
  • “They differ across societies only and do not change inside them” is wrong; the book says also change within societies over time, and the pace keeps speeding up.
  • The health and fitness wave = a social change inside a society (sociocultural) — not demographic. And meal kits are five times more expensive.

The characteristics of the population and the attitudes toward them — and the biggest trend today is the aging of the population

Most significant are a relative decline in the number of young people joining the workforce and an increase in the number of active employees who are postponing retirement beyond the traditional age of 65.

TRAP
  • The definition has “changes in, or changing attitudes toward” — so even a change in how people see the characteristics counts as demographic. A choice that says “only changes in the characteristics themselves” is incomplete.
  • The definition lists six examples (“such as”): age · gender · ethnic origin · race · sexual orientation · social class. Don't expect other items, like economic force names (interest rates, inflation), in this list.
  • The causes of aging are three, and notice that births and deaths both fall (falling birth and death rates) — not “births rise”.
  • The effect on the workplace: relatively fewer young people + older people postponing retirement past 65. A choice that says “employees retiring early” is the opposite of the book.
  • An overlap to watch: the book mentions changing attitudes toward women's roles and the LGBTQ community under sociocultural, and the demographic definition has changing attitudes toward … gender … sexual orientation. Follow the words of the question: characteristics of a population, age, births, or aging → demographic; values, norms, social structure, or individualism → sociocultural.

Political and Legal Forces
Changing laws and regulations: they limit you, open doors for you, and bring new competitors in
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Political and legal forces = the outcomes of changes in laws and regulations — inside one nation, inside one world region, or across the whole world. Laws limit the organization, and in this way they create opportunities and threats together.
🧠
The government opens the telecom market: an opportunity for the new company, and a threat for the old one that was alone.

EXPLANATION — POLITICAL INTEGRATION: THE EUROPEAN UNION AND BREXIT

Another important political and legal force: the political integration of countries. Nations are forming political unions that allow the free exchange of resources and capital.

  • The European Union (EU): common laws govern trade between the member countries, and the European Commission has the right to examine the business of any global organization and to approve any proposed mergers between foreign companies that operate inside the EU.
  • The Apple and Shazam example: Apple's proposed acquisition of Shazam (a UK-based app) was reviewed by the Commission because it might threaten competition among music-sharing platforms in Europe. The app uses the phone's or computer's microphone to identify almost any song playing nearby, and then sends you to places where you can listen to it, such as Apple Music or Spotify — so the acquisition might push more people to sign on with Apple Music. The review happened at the request of several member countries, including Austria, Italy, and Sweden. After a detailed review, the Commission gave Apple the green light and said the acquisition did not violate the EU's antitrust laws.

EXPLANATION — FALLING TRADE BARRIERS, THE TPP STORY, AND OTHER CHALLENGES

International agreements that abolish the laws that restrict trade between countries have deep effects on global organizations — and they have two sides:

OpportunityThreat
falling legal trade barriers create enormous opportunities for companies to sell goods and services internationally but because they let foreign companies compete in the domestic market, they pose a serious threat because they increase competition in the task environment
  1. The Obama administration negotiated for several years for the United States to join the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP) — a trade agreement between the United States and 11 other countries.
  2. In 2017, in the early days of his presidency, Donald Trump issued an executive order pulling the country out of the TPP, as a stand against foreign competitors in his “America First” strategy.
  3. The agreement was renamed the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) to reflect the 11 countries' commitment to join their economies more and to fair trade in the region.
  4. At the time the book was written, the Biden administration had not decided whether the United States would rejoin; but China, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom have submitted requests to join.

And the book closes the section: deregulation, privatization, and the removal of legal barriers to trade are just a few of many ways that political and legal forces challenge organizations. Other ways (3):

  1. more emphasis on environmental protection and the preservation of endangered species;
  2. more emphasis on workplace safety;
  3. legal constraints against discrimination on the basis of race, gender, or age.

And managers face major challenges when they try to use the opportunities created by changing political, legal, and economic forces.

EXAM BOX

Political and legal forces are outcomes of changes in laws and regulations. They result from political and legal developments that take place within a nation, within a world region, or across the world and significantly affect managers and organizations everywhere.

Laws constrain the operations of organizations and managers and thus create both opportunities and threats. For example, throughout much of the industrialized world there has been a strong trend toward deregulation of industries previously controlled by the state and privatization of organizations once owned by the state, such as airlines, railways, and utility companies.

The falling legal trade barriers create enormous opportunities for companies to sell goods and services internationally. But by allowing overseas companies to compete in a nation's domestic market for customers, falling trade barriers also pose a serious threat because they increase competition in the task environment.

Deregulation, privatization, and the removal of legal barriers to trade are just a few of the many ways in which changing political and legal forces can challenge organizations and managers. Others include increased emphasis on environmental protection and the preservation of endangered species, increased emphasis on workplace safety, and legal constraints against discrimination on the basis of race, gender, or age.

TRAP
  • Deregulation = the state removes its control from an industry (previously controlled). Privatization = the state sells an organization it used to own (once owned). “The government sold the national airline” = privatization; “the government allowed new companies into the airline market” = deregulation.
  • The examples in the definition are three: deregulation · privatization · environmental protection. And the other challenges: the environment and endangered species, workplace safety, legal constraints against discrimination (race, gender, age).
  • Falling trade barriers = an opportunity and a threat; the threat is because they increase competition in the task environment — in other words, a general force that moves a force in the task environment (competitors).
  • Brexit: the exit in January 2020, and the vote nearly five years before it — do not mix them. And the numbers 16% (UK → EU) and 20% (EU → UK).
  • Apple–Shazam: the Commission approved it (the green light; it did not violate antitrust laws) — it did not reject it. And the request came from Austria, Italy, and Sweden.
  • TPP: the United States + 11; Obama negotiated, Trump pulled out in 2017 (America First), the new name is CPTPP, and requests to join came from China, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom.

1. A sharp rise in interest rates and inflation in the countries where a company sells its products is best described as a change in the company's:
  1. task environment, because it affects the company's customers
  2. general environment, through demographic forces
  3. general environment, through economic forces
  4. task environment, through its suppliers
Answer

C. Interest rates and inflation are two of the four parts in the definition of economic forces, and economic forces are one of the five forces of the general environment. The trap in A and D: it is true that the change will reach customers and suppliers — because the general environment affects the organization and its task environment — but the force itself is a general economic force; the task environment = only the four groups (suppliers, distributors, customers, competitors). B is a trap: demographic = the characteristics of the population (age, gender, race…), not interest rates and inflation.

2. CRT computer monitors, bound sets of encyclopedias, and newspapers and magazines have been made obsolete by technological change. According to the chapter, this illustrates:
  1. technology, the combination of skills and equipment that managers use
  2. technological forces creating a threat for some organizations
  3. sociocultural forces created by a changing national culture
  4. technological forces, which create only threats and never opportunities
Answer

B. Technological forces = the outcomes of changes in technology, and these are exactly the book's three examples of the threat: products that became obsolete. The trap in A: technology is the combination itself (skills and equipment), not the outcome of a change in it. C: the question is about a technological change, not a change in values or in social structure. D is the trap of the word only: the book says technological change also creates a host of new opportunities (the AMD Ryzen example in 2022).

3. Which of the following statements about sociocultural forces is accurate according to the chapter?
  1. They come only from a country's national culture.
  2. Social stratification is higher in New Zealand and the United States than in India and Tibet.
  3. Social structure and national culture differ across societies but do not change within a society over time.
  4. Pressures from a country's social structure and national culture can either constrain or facilitate the way organizations operate and managers behave.
Answer

D. The book says: Pressures from both sources can either constrain or facilitate the way organizations operate and managers behave. The trap in A: the sources are two (social structure + national culture), not one. C is the opposite of the book: they differ across societies and change inside them over time (the examples of the United States, young people in Asia, and Eastern Europe). B is upside down: the caste systems of India and Tibet = high stratification, and New Zealand and the United States = low stratification.

4. Falling birth and death rates and the aging of the Baby Boomer generation have increased the demand for home health care, recreation, and medical services. These changes are best described as:
  1. demographic forces
  2. sociocultural forces
  3. economic forces
  4. political and legal forces
Answer

A. This is the aging of the population with its three causes from the book, and demographic forces = the outcomes of changes in the characteristics of a population, such as age. And the opportunity is exactly the one the book names: home health care, recreation, and the medical industries. The trap in B: sociocultural = pressures from social structure and values (like the health and fitness wave that brought organic food) — a change in values, not in the age of the population. C: economic = interest rates, inflation, unemployment, and growth. D: political and legal = changes in laws.

5. A government sells its state-owned national railway company to private investors. This is an example of:
  1. deregulation, a political and legal force
  2. privatization, a political and legal force
  3. a tariff imposed on imported goods
  4. an economic force, because the sale involves money
Answer

B. Privatization of organizations once owned by the state — and railways are one of the book's own examples (airlines, railways, and utility companies). The trap in A: deregulation = the state removes its control and restrictions from an industry it used to control; it does not sell an organization it owns. C: a tariff is a tax on imports (you meet it in lesson 3). D is the “it involves money” trap: the sale is the result of a change in laws and regulations, so it is political and legal, not economic.
