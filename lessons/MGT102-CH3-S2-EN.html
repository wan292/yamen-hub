Chapter 3 — Values and Attitudes

MGT 102 · Chapter 3 · Section 2 of 4: Values and Attitudes
Objective LO3-2: Explain what values and attitudes are, and describe their impact on managerial action.

This section is “two definitions that look the same, and the difference is in the last two words”

In the previous lesson you met personality traits — the enduring features of a manager. Now we move to three things that capture how a manager experiences the job as a person: values, attitudes, and moods and emotions (lesson 3). This lesson covers the first two.

Focus from now: this chapter is full of pairs of definitions that look word for word the same — terminal against instrumental, and job satisfaction against organizational commitment. The exam gives you the definition and asks for the term, or gives you a scenario and asks which concept it fits. Take the English definitions exactly as they are from the EXAM BOX, and do not translate them for yourself in the exam room.

How to use this page: tap or hover any underlined word to see the Arabic. Words with a solid line are vocabulary to learn — they show a translation plus a short explanation, and they are collected in the table at the end. Words with a dotted line just give you a quick translation if you get stuck. The green EXAM BOX text has no help on purpose — read it the way it will appear on the exam.

Values, Attitudes, and Moods and Emotions
Three lenses — each one answers a different question
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Values = what the manager is trying to achieve and how he thinks he should behave. Attitudes = what he thinks and feels about his job and his organization. Moods and emotions = how he actually feels while he is managing.
🧠
Three answers from one man at his desk: what he wants from life, what he thinks of this job, and how he feels right now.
EXPLANATION

The book opens the section with four questions about the manager: what is he striving to achieve? How does he think he should behave? What does he think about his job and his organization? And how does he actually feel at work? The answers come from three groups: values, attitudes, and moods and emotions.

GroupIn ArabicIt answersWhere you study it
Valuesالقيم What the manager is trying to achieve through work, and how he thinks he should behave this lesson
Attitudesالاتجاهات His thoughts and feelings about his specific job and organization this lesson
Moods and emotionsالمزاج والمشاعر How he actually feels when he is managing lesson 3 (LO3-3)

These three are highly personal, but the book tells you why we care about them: they explain how the manager behaves, how he treats and responds to other people, and how he contributes to effectiveness in the organization through the four functions — planning, leading, organizing, and controlling. That is a direct link back to Chapter 1, and the link itself comes in the exam.

EXAM BOX

Values, attitudes, and moods and emotions capture how managers experience their jobs as individuals. Values describe what managers are trying to achieve through work and how they think they should behave. Attitudes capture their thoughts and feelings about their specific jobs and organizations. Moods and emotions encompass how managers actually feel when they are managing.

Although these three aspects of managers' work experience are highly personal, they also have important implications for understanding how managers behave, how they treat and respond to others, and how, through their efforts, they help contribute to organizational effectiveness through planning, leading, organizing, and controlling.

TRAP
  • The count is three groups, not four. Moods and emotions are counted as one group in this division.
  • Attitudes are about the specific job and organization, while values are about the whole of life. A choice that says “values = what the manager thinks about his job” is wrong.
  • Do not mix this chapter with Chapter 1: here we talk about the manager as a person, not about the levels of management and their roles.

Values: Terminal and Instrumental
One is the goal of a lifetime, one is the way you behave — and the numbers are 18 and 18
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A terminal value = a goal you want to reach in your whole life. An instrumental value = the way you want to behave while you travel toward that goal.
🧠
A trip: terminal is the station where you arrive (a terminal is the last stop), and instrumental is your style of driving on the road.
EXPLANATION — TWO KINDS OF VALUES

For this book, personal values come in two kinds only:

TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Terminal value
قيمة غائية		 A lifelong goal or objective that an individual seeks to achieve. a lifelong goal or objective the person wants to reach.
Instrumental value
قيمة وسيلية		 A mode of conduct that an individual seeks to follow. a mode of conduct the person wants to follow.
Norms

Value system
نظام القيم		 The terminal and instrumental values that are guiding principles in an individual's life. the terminal and instrumental values working as guiding principles in the person's life.

The logical order they like to ask about: terminal values often lead to the formation of norms. The book's examples of norms: behaving honestly or courteously. So the arrow runs from the terminal value → to the norm, not the other way round.

EXPLANATION — Milton Rokeach and the numbers

Milton Rokeach, a leading researcher in the area of human values, is the man behind the numbers you must memorize:

  • He identified 18 terminal values and 18 instrumental values that describe any person's value system.
  • The method: the person rank orders the terminal values from 1 (most important as a guiding principle in one's life) to 18 (least important), and then rank orders the instrumental values from 1 to 18 in the same way.
  • That gives a good picture of his value system: what he is striving to achieve in life and how he wants to behave.
  • Rokeach's research is nearly 50 years old, but some of his findings are still applicable today.

Terminal values that are especially important for managers (three examples from the book): a sense of accomplishment, equality, and self-esteem. The book's example: a manager who thinks a sense of accomplishment is particularly important might focus his energies on making a lasting contribution to the organization — like developing a new product or opening a new foreign subsidiary.

Instrumental values that are important for managers (five — memorize the count):

The book's example: a manager who considers being honest and responsible to be of paramount importance may become the driving force for steps that make sure every member of the organization behaves ethically.

The bottom line: a manager's value system signifies what he is trying to accomplish and become in his personal life and at work — which is why the book calls value systems fundamental guides to a manager's behavior and to his efforts at planning, leading, organizing, and controlling.

From the chapter opener (A Manager's Challenge): Marianne Harrison, CEO of John Hancock, had her sales team spend a whole month answering phone calls from customers. That step made them feel what matters to the customer, and it embedded customer focus as a company value — a live example of a manager who shapes the values of her organization.

EXAM BOX

A terminal value is a personal conviction about lifelong goals or objectives; an instrumental value is a personal conviction about desired modes of conduct or ways of behaving. Terminal values often lead to the formation of norms, which are unwritten, informal codes of conduct, such as behaving honestly or courteously, that prescribe how people should act in particular situations and that are considered important by most members of a group or an organization.

Milton Rokeach, a leading researcher in the area of human values, identified 18 terminal values and 18 instrumental values that describe each person's value system. By rank ordering the terminal values from “1 (most important as a guiding principle in one's life)” to “18 (least important as a guiding principle in one's life)” and then rank ordering the instrumental values from 1 to 18, people can give good pictures of their value systems.

TRAP
  • The first pair in the chapter: terminal = a goal or objective, instrumental = a mode of conduct. Any choice that swaps them is wrong. Your trick: a terminal is the last station, so it is the goal.
  • The numbers are 18 + 18. Any choice with 10, 16, 20, or “18 in total” is wrong. And the ranking runs from 1 to 18, where 1 means the most important, not the least.
  • Norms are unwritten and informal. A choice that says written, formal rules is wrong — that describes official policies, not norms.
  • The direction: terminal values → norms. If a choice reverses it and says norms produce terminal values, it is wrong.
  • A value system is both kinds together (terminal + instrumental) as guiding principles — not a third kind of value.
  • The names in this chapter: Rokeach = values (18 + 18), McClelland = the three needs, Carl Jung = the MBTI, William Marston = DiSC. Do not swap the names.

Attitudes and Job Satisfaction
A three-word definition, and only two attitudes really matter
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
An attitude is a collection of feelings and beliefs. The two most important attitudes in management are job satisfaction (about your current job) and organizational commitment (about your organization as a whole).
🧠
One worker loves his desk and his coworkers but not the company; another hates his tasks but is proud of the company.
EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITIONS

The shortest definition in the whole chapter, memorize it word for word: an attitude is a collection of feelings and beliefs. Like everyone else, managers have attitudes about their jobs and organizations, and these attitudes affect how they approach their jobs. And the book says two attitudes are the most important in this context — job satisfaction and organizational commitment.

Job satisfaction = the collection of feelings and beliefs that managers have about their current jobs. A manager with a high level of job satisfaction:

  • generally likes his job.
  • feels he is fairly treated.
  • believes his job has many desirable features: interesting work, good pay and job security, autonomy, or nice coworkers.

And a point they love on the exam: levels of job satisfaction tend to increase as one moves up the hierarchy — so upper managers, in general, are more satisfied than entry-level employees. And a manager's satisfaction can range from very low to very high.

EXPLANATION — THE NUMBERS (the book gives them in detail)

You would think that in tough economic times — high unemployment and layoffs everywhere — people who still have jobs would be satisfied with them. But this is not necessarily the case. Here are the book's numbers in order:

YearThe numberThe story

Figure 3.3 — Sample Items from Two Measures of Job Satisfaction

This is Figure 3.3 in the book, and it shows two measures of job satisfaction. Top: sample items from the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire — 12 items that start with On my present job, this is how I feel about…, and the person checks one of 5 options beside each item: Very dissatisfied / Dissatisfied / Can't decide whether satisfied or not / Satisfied / Very satisfied. Bottom: The Faces Scale — workers select the face which best expresses how they feel about their job in general, and the faces are 11 faces numbered from 11 (the happiest, on the left) down to 1 (the saddest, on the right). How to read it: this measures an attitude about the current job — it is not a personality-trait measure like the ones in the previous lesson.

EXPLANATION — why do we want a satisfied manager? (two reasons)

The book says: for at least two reasons — memorize the count:

  1. He exceeds expectations and performs OCBs. A satisfied manager is more likely to exceed expectations for his organization or to perform organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs) = behaviors that are not required of organizational members but that contribute to and are necessary for organizational efficiency, effectiveness, and competitive advantage. The book calls them “above and beyond the call of duty” behaviors, and gives three examples:
    • putting in long hours when needed.
    • coming up with creative ideas and overcoming obstacles to implement them — even when that is not part of his job.
    • going out of his way to help a coworker, an employee, or a superior — even when it costs him a considerable personal sacrifice.
  2. He is less likely to quit. A highly satisfied manager may never even think about looking for another position, while a dissatisfied manager is always on the lookout for new opportunities. And turnover hurts the organization because it causes a loss of the experience and knowledge the manager has gained about the company, the industry, and the business environment.

A growing source of dissatisfaction for lower-level and middle managers, and for non-managerial employees, is the threat of unemployment and increased workloads from organizational restructurings, including layoffs. Organizations that try to improve efficiency through restructuring and layoffs often eliminate a sizable number of first-line and middle management positions. That decision hurts the managers who are laid off, and it also reduces the satisfaction of the ones who remain — they fear being the next to be let go, and their workloads often increase dramatically.

That is why the book says the way managers handle a layoff is of paramount importance — not only for the employees who are let go, but also for the employees who survive the layoff and keep their jobs. And these are a few of the ways the book gives for managing a layoff humanely:

  1. showing compassion and empathy for layoff victims.
  2. giving them as much advance notice as possible about the layoff.
  3. providing clear information about severance benefits.
  4. helping them in their job search efforts.
EXAM BOX

An attitude is a collection of feelings and beliefs. Two of the most important attitudes in this context are job satisfaction and organizational commitment.

Job satisfaction is the collection of feelings and beliefs that managers have about their current jobs. Levels of job satisfaction tend to increase as one moves up the hierarchy in an organization. Upper managers, in general, tend to be more satisfied with their jobs than entry-level employees.

In general, it is desirable for managers to be satisfied with their jobs, for at least two reasons. First, satisfied managers may be more likely to exceed expectations for their organizations or perform organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs)—behaviors that are not required of organizational members but that contribute to and are necessary for organizational efficiency, effectiveness, and competitive advantage. A second reason it is desirable for managers to be satisfied with their jobs is that satisfied managers may be less likely to quit.

TRAP
  • Job satisfaction is about their current jobs. Memorize the last two words, because the definition of organizational commitment starts with exactly the same wording.
  • OCBs are not required. A choice that says behaviors that are required of organizational members is wrong — and that is the most famous trap on this concept. But be careful: they are not required and they are necessary for efficiency, effectiveness, and competitive advantage — both words sit in the same definition.
  • Satisfaction increases as you move up the hierarchy. A choice that says entry-level employees are more satisfied than upper managers is wrong.
  • The second reason is less likely to quit, not “works faster” and not “obeys orders more”. And a “how many reasons” question has the answer 2.
  • The satisfaction numbers are easy to mix: 1987 = 61.1% · 2009 = 45% (the low) · 2019 = 54% · November 2020 = 56%. And in 2009 unemployment was 10% and underemployment 17.3%.
  • In 2020 the satisfaction of remote employees was not significantly different from that of other workers. A choice that says “remote employees were much less satisfied” is wrong.

Organizational Commitment
The same wording as job satisfaction — the difference is in the last four words
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Organizational commitment = your feelings and beliefs about the organization as a whole — not about your own job.
🧠
A club fan: he can be angry about one match (the job) and still be proud of the club and wear its badge (the organization).
EXPLANATION

Organizational commitment = the collection of feelings and beliefs that managers have about their organization as a whole. A manager who is committed to his organization:

  • believes in what his organization is doing.
  • is proud of what it stands for.
  • feels a high degree of loyalty toward it.

And two results: committed managers are more likely to go above and beyond the call of duty to help their company, and they are less likely to quit.

When is commitment especially strong? When employees and managers truly believe in organizational values — and this also leads to a strong organizational culture (the topic of lesson 4 in this chapter).

The link to Chapter 1 (it comes in the exam): organizational commitment helps a manager perform two of Mintzberg's roles in particular — figurehead and spokesperson. Why? Because it is much easier to persuade people, inside and outside the organization, of the merits of what the organization has done and is seeking to accomplish when the manager truly believes in and is committed to his organization.

EXPLANATION — Attitudes in Context: attitudes are not purely personal

Attitudes are not purely a personal characteristic — they can be shaped by external conditions:

  • Working conditions shape job satisfaction and commitment. Situations with high stress and low control over outcomes contribute to dissatisfaction and to low levels of organizational commitment.
  • The levels may vary from country to country, in line with the opportunities and challenges tied to different economic conditions, laws and regulations, and social norms.
  • The book's example: the level of organizational commitment in a country may depend on the extent to which the country has legislation affecting firings and layoffs, and on how geographically mobile its citizens are.
EXAM BOX

Organizational commitment is the collection of feelings and beliefs that managers have about their organization as a whole. Managers who are committed to their organizations believe in what their organizations are doing, are proud of what these organizations stand for, and feel a high degree of loyalty toward their organizations. Committed managers are more likely to go above and beyond the call of duty to help their company and are less likely to quit. Organizational commitment can be especially strong when employees and managers truly believe in organizational values; it also leads to a strong organizational culture.

Organizational commitment is likely to help managers perform some of their figurehead and spokesperson roles. Attitudes are not, of course, purely a personal characteristic; they can be shaped by external conditions.

TRAP
  • The number-one trap of the whole chapter: the two definitions look like copies of each other — The collection of feelings and beliefs that managers have about… — and the difference is at the end: their current jobs = job satisfaction, their organization as a whole = organizational commitment.
  • If the scenario has “proud of the company / believes in its mission / loyalty” → organizational commitment. If it has “likes his tasks / his pay / his coworkers / feels fairly treated” → job satisfaction.
  • The two roles that commitment helps are figurehead and spokesperson — not negotiator, not monitor, and not resource allocator.
  • Commitment leads to a strong organizational culture — the arrow in this sentence runs from commitment to culture, and remember that culture is its own topic in lesson 4.
  • Attitudes can be shaped by external conditions too. A choice that says attitudes are purely a personal characteristic is wrong.

Burnout
What happens to negative attitudes when they go on too long
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Negative attitudes become burnout when the stress reaches a point where the worker is exhausted, detached from or cynical about the work, and can no longer work effectively.
🧠
A phone on 2% since morning: the screen lights up but it can do nothing until you stop and charge it.
EXPLANATION — the Management Insight box

The box is called Pandemic Highlights the Hazards of Burnout. Across most industries, the COVID-19 pandemic created the conditions tied to burnout: highly demanding work under uncertainty, together with a sense of powerlessness to achieve the desired results.

What happened to the workers? They were sent home, called in, furloughed, rescheduled, overscheduled, and asked to cover for absent colleagues — and they were expected to balance all of this with the needs of their children and families. So it is no surprise that they reported increasing levels of stress and dissatisfaction.

During the pandemic, employers blamed stress and burnout for a surge of employees leaving their jobs. The problem was especially troubling in health care, where it caused severe shortages of nurses and other frontline workers.

What managers tried (with varying success)What Gallup's research says matters more
  • Four-day workweeks or flexible schedules — hoping for more rest and a better work–life balance.
  • Setting boundaries on when employees are expected to answer email or join online meetings.
  • Raising salaries or moving dissatisfied employees into higher-paying jobs.
  1. Reducing workloads.
  2. Improving communication.
  3. Equipping managers to be more supportive.

And managers themselves suffer: Gallup finds higher rates of burnout among managers than among non-management workers. Managers are especially likely to say that expectations of them are unclear, that performance feedback is unhelpful, that their workdays are full of interruptions, and that they have no chance to spend time doing what they do best.

But a manager does have a chance to improve his own conditions — for example, by:

  1. Delegating work.
  2. Collaborating with others.
  3. Pursuing opportunities for career development.
  4. Having a voice in decisions.
EXAM BOX

Negative attitudes become burnout when the stress at work reaches a point where workers are exhausted, feel detached from or cynical about their work, and no longer can work effectively.

Across most industries, the COVID-19 pandemic created conditions associated with burnout: highly demanding work under uncertainty, coupled with a sense of powerlessness to achieve the desired results. Gallup's research into worker engagement finds that other actions are more significant: reducing workloads, improving communication, and equipping managers to be more supportive.

TRAP
  • The definition of burnout has three signs together: exhausted + detached or cynical + no longer can work effectively. A choice that stops at “tired” or “work stress” without the detachment and the loss of effectiveness is incomplete.
  • Burnout comes out of negative attitudes — it is not a personality trait like negative affectivity in the previous lesson. The exam mixes them on purpose.
  • Gallup names three more significant actions: reducing workloads · improving communication · equipping managers to be more supportive. Raising salaries and the four-day week are things managers tried, with varying success — they are not what Gallup put first.
  • Burnout among managers is higher than among non-management workers. A choice that reverses this is wrong.
  • The book's examples of how a manager can improve his own conditions all start with the manager himself: delegate · collaborate · career development · a voice in decisions.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 1, go back to the trap “terminal against instrumental”; if you miss question 3, go back to “the last two words of the definition”.

1. Milton Rokeach identified 18 terminal values and 18 instrumental values. A manager's personal conviction that she should always be ambitious, responsible, and self-disciplined in the way she behaves is best described as:
  1. A terminal value
  2. An instrumental value
  3. A norm
  4. A value system
Answer

B. The key words are the way she behaves = a mode of conduct, and that is exactly the definition of an instrumental value. On top of that, ambitious, responsible, and self-disciplined are three of the five instrumental values the book lists for managers. The trap in A: a terminal value is a lifelong goal, like a sense of accomplishment — not a way of behaving. C is a trap too: a norm is an unwritten code that belongs to a group or an organization, not a personal conviction. And D is a trap: a value system is both kinds together as guiding principles, not one single value.

2. Which of the following is NOT true of organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs)?
  1. They are required of all organizational members
  2. They contribute to organizational efficiency and effectiveness
  3. They are necessary for competitive advantage
  4. Satisfied managers are more likely to perform them
Answer

A. The book's definition starts with behaviors that are not required of organizational members — so they are not required, and that is why the book calls them “above and beyond the call of duty”. The trap: the question uses NOT, and B and C are taken word for word from the same definition so that you rush and pick one of them. D is the first of the two reasons why a manager's satisfaction is desirable. Remember the double idea inside the definition: not required but necessary for efficiency, effectiveness, and competitive advantage.

3. A manager truly believes in what her company is doing, is proud of what it stands for, and feels a high degree of loyalty toward it, even on days when her own tasks frustrate her. Her feelings are best described as:
  1. Job satisfaction
  2. Organizational commitment
  3. A terminal value
  4. An organizational norm
Answer

B. “Believes + proud + loyalty” is the book's own description of a committed manager, and the definition is about the organization as a whole. The trap in A: the two definitions look like copies of each other (The collection of feelings and beliefs that managers have about…) and the difference is in the last words — current jobs against organization as a whole. On top of that, the question says her daily tasks frustrate her, so her job satisfaction is not high while her commitment is. And C is a trap: a terminal value is a lifelong goal, not a feeling toward an organization.

4. According to Figure 3.3, the Faces Scale measures job satisfaction by asking workers to:
  1. Rank 18 values from 1 to 18
  2. Select the face that best expresses how they feel about their job in general
  3. Rate 12 items as very dissatisfied, dissatisfied, can't decide, satisfied, or very satisfied
  4. Rate how much each of 12 mood words describes how they felt at work last week
Answer

B. The second part of Figure 3.3 is called The Faces Scale, and its instruction is word for word: Workers select the face which best expresses how they feel about their job in general — 11 faces numbered from 11 down to 1. The trap in C: that describes the first part of the same figure (the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire, 12 items and 5 options) — the figure holds two measures, do not mix them. A is the Rokeach trap, and D is the trap of the mood measure that comes in the next lesson.

5. Nurses in a hospital report that they are exhausted, feel detached from and cynical about their work, and can no longer work effectively. According to the chapter, their negative attitudes have become:
  1. Negative affectivity
  2. Low organizational commitment
  3. Burnout
  4. Turnover
Answer

C. The three signs in the question are half of the definition of burnout, word for word: exhausted + detached from or cynical about their work + no longer can work effectively. And the book's own example was about health care and the shortage of nurses. The trap in A: negative affectivity is an enduring personality trait from the Big Five (lesson 1), not a result of work stress. And D is a trap: turnover is quitting and leaving — a possible result of burnout, not burnout itself.
