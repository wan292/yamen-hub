MGT 102 · Chapter 7 · Section 1 of 4: The Nature of Managerial Decision Making
Objective LO7-1: Understand the nature of managerial decision making, differentiate between programmed and nonprogrammed decisions, and explain why nonprogrammed decision making is a complex, uncertain process.

FROM THE CHAPTER OPENING — A Manager's Challenge: Karen Lynch's Bold Decisions at CVS Health

The chapter opens with a question: How do managers make decisions that add value? Its example is Karen Lynch, the chief executive of CVS Health:

  • CVS is the fourth-largest U.S. company with almost 10,000 stores. Lynch describes herself as decisive; the aunt who raised her after her mother's death taught her that decisions are normal and you accept their outcomes. Her words: “When you make decisions as often as I do, you're going to make a bad call. You just have to readjust.”
  • 2018: CVS (a drugstore chain) acquired Aetna (a health insurance company) as part of a strategy to offer health care services. When Lynch became CEO three years later, she expanded the services from vaccinations, testing, and pharmacy to offices for primary care and mental health care — the goal is not only new income; she wants health care to be more widely available and less costly (which also helps the Aetna insurance business).
  • Three types of stores: (1) stores turned into primary-care clinics staffed by physicians — the fastest growth, especially in areas with many people eligible for Medicare, (2) HealthHUBs — screenings and preventive services plus health care products for sale, (3) traditional drugstores with a small MinuteClinic.
  • February 2021: she became CEO in the middle of the pandemic, just as vaccines were becoming available. She made CVS a community source of vaccines and COVID tests, with the motto “employees first, customers second”. 90,000 headquarters employees worked from home, and store workers got protective equipment. She and her team stood in stores as greeters to hear employees and customers, learned that the pharmacists were overwhelmed, and set up separately staffed in-store clinics for vaccinations.
  • For the second year in a row, Lynch was number 1 on Fortune's list of the 50 Most Powerful Women (2022).

Why does the book start with her? Because the decisions managers make at all levels change the company's growth and the well-being of its employees, customers, and other stakeholders — but these decisions are hard because they are fraught with uncertainty. This is the idea the whole section builds on.

EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITION AND WHERE DECISIONS COME FROM

Every time a manager plans, organizes, directs, or controls the organization's activities, he makes a stream of decisions. The book's example: if you open a new restaurant, you must decide where to locate it, what kinds of food to provide, whom to hire, and so on. So decision making is a basic part of every task a manager does.

From the last three chapters: one of the manager's main jobs is to manage the organizational environment. Forces in the external environment create opportunities and threats, and opportunities and threats also arise inside the organization as its resources are used. To deal with them, the manager must decide — that is, select one solution from a set of alternatives.

Programmed and Nonprogrammed Decision Making
Routine with a ready rule against new with no rule — and then you need intuition or reasoned judgment
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Every decision is either programmed: routine, almost automatic, and follows ready rules; or nonprogrammed: nonroutine, comes with an unusual or new opportunity or threat, and has no rule — so the manager relies on intuition or reasoned judgment.
🧠
Ordering office paper = a ready button you press every time. An expert chess move = no button; you must feel or think.

EXPLANATION — PROGRAMMED DECISIONS AND DECISION RULES
  • Programmed decisions = decisions that were made so many times in the past that managers developed rules or guidelines to apply when certain situations inevitably happen. These rules are called decision rules — fixed guidelines the manager follows automatically in recurring decisions.
  • Why is it called “programmed”? Because the manager does not need to make a new judgment every time — he relies on long-established decision rules.
#The book's three examplesThe rule
1A school principal asks the school board to hire a new teacherwhenever student enrollment goes up by 40 students
2A manufacturing supervisor hires new workers whenever existing workers' overtime goes up by more than 10%
3An office manager orders basic office supplies (paper and pens)whenever the inventory drops below a certain level — and he probably orders the same amount each time

The two rules of the paper example (the book writes them like this):

  • Rule 1: When the storage shelves are three-quarters empty, order more copy paper. — when the shelves are three-quarters empty, order paper.
  • Rule 2: When ordering paper, order enough to fill the shelves. — when you order, order enough to fill the shelves.
  • Rules can regulate all routine activities: for example, how a worker should do a certain task, and the quality standards that raw materials must meet to be acceptable.
  • Most decisions about the day-to-day running of an organization are programmed. The book's examples (4): how much inventory to hold, when to pay bills, when to bill customers, and when to order materials and supplies.
  • When it happens: a programmed decision happens when the manager has the information he needs to create rules. And there is little ambiguity — you can easily tell when the stockroom is empty, or count the new students in class.
EXPLANATION — NONPROGRAMMED DECISIONS
  • Imagine the manager is not sure that a course of action will lead to the outcome he wants — or worse: he is not even sure what he is trying to achieve. Obviously, you cannot make rules to predict uncertain events.
  • Nonprogrammed decisions are made in response to unusual or novel opportunities and threats, when there are no ready-made decision rules. Rules do not exist because the situation is unexpected or uncertain, and the manager lacks the information he needs to build a rule.
  • The book's examples (6): investing in a new technology · developing a new kind of product · launching a new promotional campaign · entering a new market · expanding internationally · starting a new business — like Karen Lynch and CVS Health did with their HealthHUBs.
  • The book's photo caption: Nonprogrammed decision making covers areas with no previous benchmarks or rubrics, such as making expert chess moves. — that is, areas with no earlier benchmarks or rubrics, like an expert chess move.

EXAM BOX

Programmed decision making is a routine, virtually automatic process. Programmed decisions are decisions that have been made so many times in the past that managers have developed rules or guidelines to be applied when certain situations inevitably occur. Programmed decision making occurs when managers have the information they need to create rules that will guide decision making.

Nonprogrammed decision making is required for these nonroutine decisions. Nonprogrammed decisions are made in response to unusual or novel opportunities and threats. Nonprogrammed decision making occurs when there are no ready-made decision rules that managers can apply to a situation.

They may rely on their intuition—feelings, beliefs, and hunches that come readily to mind, require little effort and information gathering, and result in on-the-spot decisions. Or they may make reasoned judgments—decisions that require time and effort and result from careful information gathering, generation of alternatives, and evaluation of alternatives. “Exercising” one's judgment is a more rational process than “going with” one's intuition. Thus, the likelihood of error is much greater in nonprogrammed decision making than in programmed decision making.

TRAP
  • Programmed = routine + ready rules + available information + little ambiguity. Nonprogrammed = nonroutine + unusual or novel + no rules + a much greater likelihood of error. Any scenario with “whenever X happens, do Y” = programmed.
  • Most day-to-day decisions = programmed. But in the rest of the chapter, decision making means nonprogrammed — do not mix them up.
  • Intuition = little effort and information + an on-the-spot decision. Reasoned judgment = time and effort + gathering information, generating alternatives, and evaluating them. Do not swap them.
  • Both are ways to make a nonprogrammed decision (when there are no rules) — not a programmed one. And both often are flawed: intuition is not always wrong, and reasoned judgment is not always right.
  • Link the examples: firefighting = expert intuition (no time). A proposed merger = reasoned judgment (there is time but no rules). Both are nonprogrammed decisions.
  • The numbers in the examples: 40 students · more than 10% overtime · shelves three-quarters empty.

The Classical Model
How you “should” decide if you had all the information and a mind that could handle it all
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The classical model is a prescriptive model = it tells you how you should make decisions: list all the alternatives and their consequences, rank them by your preferences, and choose the optimum decision (the best one).
🧠
You want a phone, so you compare every phone in the world on every feature and pick the 100% best — nice on paper only.
EXPLANATION — BEFORE THE TWO MODELS: WHAT ARE THEY FOR?

The book presents the two models (classical and administrative) together and says: they reveal many of the assumptions, complexities, and pitfalls that affect decision making, and they show the factors a decision maker must be aware of to improve the quality of his decisions. But be careful: the two models are just guides that help the manager understand the decision process — in real life the process is typically not cut-and-dried.

EXPLANATION — THE TWO DEFINITIONS AND THE PREMISE
TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Classical model
النموذج الكلاسيكي		 A prescriptive approach to decision making based on the assumption that the decision maker can identify and evaluate all possible alternatives and their consequences and rationally choose the most appropriate course of action. a prescriptive approach that assumes the decision maker can identify and evaluate all the possible alternatives and their consequences, and choose the most appropriate course of action rationally.
Optimum decision
القرار الأمثل		 The most appropriate decision in light of what managers believe to be the most desirable consequences for the organization. the most appropriate decision, based on what managers believe are the most desirable consequences for the organization.
  • Prescriptive = specifies how decisions should be made — it says how a decision should be made, not how it is really made. And it is one of the earliest models of decision making.
  • Managers who use it make a series of simplifying assumptions about the nature of the decision process.
  • The premise: once the manager recognizes that a decision must be made, he should be able to generate a complete list of all alternatives and consequences and choose the best. So it assumes the manager has all the information he needs for the optimum decision.
  • It also assumes the manager can easily list his preferences for each alternative and rank them from least preferred to most preferred to reach the optimum decision.
  • The book's summary at the end of the chapter puts it in 3: complete information + processing it in an objective, rational way + reaching an optimum decision.

EXAM BOX

Keep in mind, however, that the classical and administrative models are just guides that can help managers understand the decision-making process.

One of the earliest models of decision making, the classical model, is prescriptive, which means it specifies how decisions should be made. The premise of the classical model is that once managers recognize the need to make a decision, they should be able to generate a complete list of all alternatives and consequences and make the best choice. In other words, the classical model assumes managers have access to all the information they need to make the optimum decision, which is the most appropriate decision possible in light of what they believe to be the most desirable consequences for the organization. Furthermore, the classical model assumes managers can easily list their own preferences for each alternative and rank them from least to most preferred to make the optimum decision.

The classical model of decision making assumes that decision makers have complete information; can process that information in an objective, rational manner; and make optimum decisions.

TRAP
  • Classical = prescriptive = how a decision should be made (should be made) — not a description of what happens in real life. And the book calls both models just guides.
  • There are 3 assumptions, and each is linked to a step: List ↔ all information is available · Rank ↔ mental facility · Select ↔ knows what is best for the future. The trap is to swap the assumptions between the steps.
  • The ranking in the figure goes from least preferred to most preferred — from least to most, and by personal preferences.
  • Optimum decision = the most appropriate decision in light of what they believe are the best consequences — not “a decision that is guaranteed to succeed”. It is the goal of the classical model; the administrative model says the manager rarely reaches it.
  • Bounded rationality, satisficing, and incomplete information are not from the classical model — they are concepts of the administrative model. The classical model says the opposite: complete information and a mind that can process it.

EXPLANATION — MARCH AND SIMON'S OBJECTION AND THE DEFINITION

James March and Herbert Simon disagreed with the underlying assumptions of the classical model, and they had two objections:

  1. Managers in the real world do not have access to all the information they need to decide.
  2. And even if all the information were available, many managers would not have the mental or psychological ability to absorb it and evaluate it correctly.

So they developed the administrative model to explain why decision making is always an uncertain and risky process by nature — and why managers rarely can decide in the way the classical model prescribes.

TRAP

  • The concepts are exactly 3: bounded rationality · incomplete information · satisficing. Any choice that puts optimum decision or prescriptive as a concept of the administrative model is wrong.
  • Bounded rationality = limits of the mind (cognitive limitations). Incomplete information = limits of the information. Two different terms in the same model — the book says that even if your ability to evaluate had no limit, the information would still be incomplete.
  • The abilities it limits: interpret, process, and act on information — 3 verbs. And the cause: limitations of human intelligence.
  • The administrative model explains why — it does not say how things should be done. And the result: satisfactory rather than optimum.

The Ethics in Action box: Taking Risks to Help Others and the Planet
  • The problem: plastic thrown away in the oceans. And the irony: its biggest sources are poor nations — they consume less plastic, but they lack efficient systems to collect it and bury or recycle it.
  • David Katz saw an opportunity in it and was willing to take a risk: the great volume of plastic had economic value, and he wanted a business that makes money by getting plastic out of the oceans and into recycling plants.
  • He founded Plastic Bank: collection centers run by locals + online bank accounts for collectors. You bring bottles → your account is credited electronically → you see the money on your phone and pay with it (mobile payments). Many of them are saving for the first time in their lives.
  • The numbers: since its first centers in Haiti in 2015, it has improved the lives of more than 4,200 families and recovered and recycled more than 1.5 billion pounds of plastic waste. It has expanded to the Philippines, Indonesia, Brazil, Egypt, Cameroon, and Thailand.
  • The challenge that remains: what to do with the bottles? He found workers easily, but demand for used plastic is harder: new plastic (virgin plastic) is cheaper than recycled plastic, and some countries prohibit recycled plastic for food and drinks. So Plastic Bank partners with global companies such as S.C. Johnson that want to show their commitment to sustainability: they fund the centers and commit to using more recycled plastic in their packaging.
  • The lesson: a great idea can come from one person, but to succeed it may need a group of people who can take risks and are comfortable with uncertainty.

What they ask: “Figure 7.3 illustrates which concept?” = ambiguous information — not uncertainty (here the information exists but can be understood in more than one way), and not bounded rationality.

EXPLANATION — REASON 3: TIME CONSTRAINTS AND INFORMATION COSTS

Managers have neither the time nor the money to search for all the possible alternative solutions and evaluate all their potential consequences. The book's example — Ford Motor Company:

  • A purchasing manager has one month to choose a supplier for a small engine part.
  • There are 20,000 potential suppliers for this part in the United States alone.
  • In the time available, he cannot contact every supplier and ask each one for its terms (price, delivery schedules, and so on).
  • And even if the time were available, the cost of getting the information — including the manager's own time — would be prohibitive.
EXAM BOX

Information is incomplete because the full range of decision-making alternatives is unknowable in most situations, and the consequences associated with known alternatives are uncertain. In other words, information is incomplete because of risk and uncertainty, ambiguity, and time constraints (see Figure 7.2).

Risk is present when managers know the possible outcomes of a particular course of action and can assign probabilities to them. When uncertainty exists, the probabilities of alternative outcomes cannot be determined and future outcomes are unknown. Managers are working blind. Uncertainty plagues most managerial decision making.

A second reason information is incomplete is that much of the information managers have at their disposal is ambiguous information. Its meaning is not clear—it can be interpreted in multiple and often conflicting ways.

The third reason information is incomplete is that managers have neither the time nor the money to search for all possible alternative solutions and evaluate all the potential consequences of those alternatives.

TRAP
  • Risk = you know the outcomes and can give them probabilities (drugs 10% / 90%). Uncertainty = the probabilities cannot be determined, working blind (Newton 1993). The words can assign probabilities → risk; “the first time” or “no data” → uncertainty.
  • The definition of uncertainty is one word: Unpredictability. If you see it in a choice, that is it.
  • Ambiguous information = the information exists but can be understood in multiple and conflicting ways (Figure 7.3). It is not the same as uncertainty (there is no information about the probabilities at all).
  • The reasons in Figure 7.2 are 3 only. Bounded rationality and satisficing are not among them — they are other concepts in the same model.
  • Ford: one month · 20,000 suppliers · in the United States alone · a small engine part. And even if there were time, the cost is prohibitive.
  • Newton: the first launch failed because of technical problems, and the improved version was more successful. And Plastic Bank (David Katz) is a box about taking risks and being comfortable with uncertainty — from Haiti 2015.

THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Satisficing = searching for and choosing an acceptable, or satisfactory, response to a problem or opportunity, instead of trying to make the best decision — you explore only a limited sample of the alternatives.

EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITION AND WHY MANAGERS DO IT

March and Simon said: managers do not try to discover every alternative when they face 6 things: (1) bounded rationality, (2) an uncertain future, (3) unquantifiable risks, (4) considerable ambiguity, (5) time constraints, (6) high information costs. Instead, they use a strategy called satisficing = exploring a limited sample of all potential alternatives.

EXPLANATION — MORE ART THAN SCIENCE
  • March and Simon said: managerial decision making is often more art than science.
  • In the real world, the manager must rely on his intuition and judgment to make what seems to him the best decision in the face of uncertainty and ambiguity.
  • Managerial decision making is often fast-paced: the manager uses his experience and judgment to make crucial decisions under conditions of incomplete information.
  • There is nothing wrong with this approach, but the decision maker must know that human judgment is often flawed — and that is why even the best managers sometimes make poor decisions. This is the bridge to lesson 2: the six steps and cognitive biases.

March and Simon argued that managers do not try to discover every alternative when faced with bounded rationality, an uncertain future, unquantifiable risks, considerable ambiguity, time constraints, and high information costs. March and Simon argued that managers exhibit bounded rationality, rarely have access to all the information they need to make optimum decisions, and consequently satisfice and rely on their intuition and judgment when making decisions.

TRAP
  • Satisficing = acceptable, or satisfactory — not best and not optimum. And it searches a limited sample — not all the alternatives.
  • Do not mix them up: satisficing = the strategy (how you search and choose). Bounded rationality = the mental limit that pushes you to it. Both belong to the administrative model, but they are not the same thing.
  • Its price in the Ford example: a superior supplier may be missed — and still the book calls the action reasonable.
  • More art than science — art more than science, not the other way round. The book says nothing wrong with this approach, but human judgment is often flawed → even the best managers sometimes make poor decisions.
  • Any scenario where someone “looked at a few options and took the first one that works” = satisficing. Any scenario where someone “compared all the alternatives and chose the best” = classical / optimum.

1. Every time a restaurant's stock of rice drops below 20 bags, the manager orders 50 more bags—the same amount every time—without having to think about it. This is best described as:
  1. nonprogrammed decision making
  2. intuition
  3. programmed decision making
  4. satisficing
Answer

C. There is a ready rule (below 20 bags → order 50) that repeats with the same amount every time — just like the book's example of the office manager who orders paper when the stock drops below a certain level and probably orders the same amount of supplies each time. This is programmed: routine, virtually automatic, and following decision rules. The trap in A: nonprogrammed is for new or unusual situations with no rule. B is a trap too: intuition is a way to decide when there is no rule — and here there is a rule. And D: satisficing = choosing a satisfactory option from a sample of alternatives, and here there is no search among alternatives at all.

2. A company is the first ever to bring a totally new kind of product to market, so there is no body of data from which its managers can calculate the probability of a successful launch. The managers are making this decision under conditions of:
  1. uncertainty
  2. risk
  3. ambiguous information
  4. programmed decision making
Answer

A. The first company to bring a totally new product, with no data to calculate the probability from = exactly the Apple Newton example (1993): uncertainty — the probabilities of alternative outcomes cannot be determined, and managers are working blind. The trap in B: risk is when you can give the outcomes probabilities (like the drugs with a 10% chance of success, from the experience of thousands of drugs). C: ambiguous information = information that exists but can be read in more than one way (Figure 7.3), and here the information does not exist at all. And D: programmed = routine with rules — the exact opposite of this situation.

3. According to Figure 7.1, the step "Rank each alternative from least preferred to most preferred according to personal preferences" is based on which assumption?
  1. All information about alternatives is available to managers.
  2. Managers know what future course of action is best for the organization.
  3. Managers will choose a satisfactory rather than an optimum alternative.
  4. Managers possess the mental facility to process this information.
Answer

D. In Figure 7.1 each step on the left has an arrow to an assumption on the right, and the second step (Rank…) points to Assumes managers possess the mental facility to process this information. The trap in A: this is the assumption of the first step (List all…). B is the assumption of the third step (Select…). And C is not in the figure at all: a “satisfactory” choice comes from the administrative model (satisficing), while the classical model looks for the optimum.

4. According to Figure 7.2, which of the following is NOT a reason why information is incomplete?
  1. Risk and uncertainty
  2. Bounded rationality
  3. Ambiguous information
  4. Time constraints and information costs
Answer

B. Figure 7.2 has only three boxes, and their arrows point into the Incomplete information circle: A, C, and D. Bounded rationality is the trap because it comes from the same administrative model — but it is a separate concept about the limits of the mind, not a reason the information is incomplete. The book says: even if a manager's ability to evaluate information had no limit, the information would still be incomplete. The question uses NOT, so look for the one that is not in the figure.

5. A hiring manager receives 900 applications for one job. With little time, he reviews 15 of them, interviews three, and hires the first candidate who is good enough for the job. According to March and Simon, this manager is:
  1. making the optimum decision
  2. satisficing
  3. following the classical model
  4. making a programmed decision
Answer

B. He looked at a small sample (15 out of 900) and chose the first one that “works” = satisficing: exploring a limited sample of all potential alternatives and choosing an acceptable, or satisfactory response. The trap in A and C: the optimum decision and the classical model need all the alternatives and their consequences to reach the best — and he saw only 15. And D: programmed = a ready rule for a repeated situation; here there is no rule — he searches and chooses. The price is like the Ford example: he may have missed a better candidate.
