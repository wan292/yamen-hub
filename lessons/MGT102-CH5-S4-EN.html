EXPLANATION — Step 1: Secure top management commitment
  • As you saw in lesson 2: top management's commitment to diversity is crucial for the success of any diversity initiative.
  • Top managers need to develop two things: the correct ethical values and performance- or business-oriented attitudes — so they can make appropriate use of their human resources.
  • Notice the link with lesson 2: managing diversity is an ethical imperative and a business imperative — and here is the same pair: ethical values + business-oriented attitudes.
EXPLANATION — Step 2: Strive to increase the accuracy of perceptions

The book says this is part of developing the right values and attitudes. What does the manager do?

What he doesThe book's words
He tries consciously to be open to other viewpoints, looks for them, and encourages his employees to do the same be open to other points of view and perspectives, seek them out
People who are open to other perspectives put their beliefs and knowledge to an important reality test, and they are more inclined to modify them when necessary an important reality test
He is not afraid to change his views about a person, an issue, or an event, and he encourages his employees to change their views when disconfirming evidence appears disconfirming evidence
He and all members avoid snap judgments about people; a judgment comes only after enough relevant information is gathered avoid making snap judgments

Link it to lesson 3: schemas change slowly and need a lot of information that contradicts them — and this step is what opens the door to that information.

EXAM BOX

Various kinds of barriers arise to managing diversity effectively in organizations. Some barriers originate in the person doing the perceiving; others are based on the information and schemas that have built up over time concerning the person being perceived. To overcome these barriers and effectively manage diversity, managers (and other organizational members) must possess or develop certain attitudes and values as well as the skills needed to change other people’s attitudes and values.

TRAP

Diversity Awareness, Diversity Skills, and Flexibility
Steps 3–5: know your bias, know how to communicate, and be open without losing yourself
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Awareness = you know your own biases and other people's perspectives. Skills = you know how to communicate and work with different people. Flexibility = you are open to other ways without suppressing your own style, and you make room for employees' different needs.
🧠
Awareness: a mirror that shows your bias. Skills: a translator between you and your coworker. Flexibility: a chair that adjusts for everyone without breaking.
EXPLANATION — Step 3: Increase diversity awareness

It is natural for managers and others to see people from their own perspective, because their own feelings, thoughts, attitudes, and experiences guide their perceptions and interactions. But appreciating diversity requires people to become aware of other perspectives, attitudes, and experiences.

Diversity awareness programs try to increase managers' and workers' awareness of two things:

  1. their own attitudes, biases, and stereotypes
  2. the differing perspectives of diverse managers, employees, coworkers, and customers

And their goals in the book are six (memorize the count):

#The goal (the book's words)What it means
1Providing organizational members with accurate information about diversity give members accurate information about diversity
2Uncovering personal biases and stereotypes uncover your personal biases and stereotypes
3Assessing personal beliefs, attitudes, and values and learning about other points of view assess your beliefs, attitudes, and values, and learn about other points of view
4Overturning inaccurate stereotypes and beliefs about different groups overturn wrong stereotypes and beliefs about different groups
5Developing an atmosphere in which people feel free to share their differing perspectives and points of view—even going so far as to mentally walk in other people’s shoes build an atmosphere where people feel free to share their perspectives — even to walk in other people's shoes in their minds (put themselves in someone else's place)
6Improving understanding of others who are different from oneself improve your understanding of people who are different from you

Two tips from the book:

  • Sometimes simply taking time to interact with someone different raises awareness. At social functions or at lunch, people sit with the people they feel most comfortable with. If everyone made an effort to interact with people they ordinarily would not, mutual understanding would improve.
  • In large organizations, top managers are often far removed from entry-level employees — they may not understand what these employees do day in and day out, or the challenges and obstacles they face. So some managers have taken concrete steps to understand the experiences, attitudes, and perspectives of frontline employees.
EXPLANATION — Step 4: Increase diversity skills

Efforts to increase diversity skills focus on improving how managers and their employees interact with each other, and on improving their ability to work with different kinds of people. The big issue here is communication. The book lists five differences between diverse members (we count them to help you memorise; the book runs them together in one sentence):

#What differs (the book's words)In Arabic
1different communication stylesأساليب التواصل
2language fluencyطلاقة اللغة
3use words differentlyيستخدمون الكلمات بشكل مختلف
4nonverbal signals… facial expressions and body languageالإشارات غير اللفظية — تعابير الوجه ولغة الجسد
5how they perceive and interpret informationكيف يدركون ويفسّرون المعلومات
  • If the organization wants to use the skills of its entire workforce, managers and employees must learn to communicate well. And a good starting point: educating them about differences in ways of communicating.
  • Diversity education helps in two ways: (1) a better understanding of how people may interpret certain kinds of comments, and (2) employees learn how to resolve misunderstandings.
  • Members should feel comfortable enough to solve communication problems as they happen, instead of letting problems grow and fester without acknowledgment.
EXPLANATION — Step 5: Encourage flexibility

Managers and their employees must learn to be open to different approaches and ways of doing things. But watch the limit the book puts on it:

Flexibility does not meanFlexibility does mean
that members suppress their personal styles they are open to different approaches and perspectives, they do not feel threatened by them, and they have the patience and flexibility to understand and appreciate diverse perspectives

Also, the manager — to the extent feasible — should be flexible enough to incorporate the different needs of diverse employees:

  • Religion: as you learned in lesson 1, people of some religions need time off for holidays that are normal workdays in the United States — the manager must anticipate these needs and respond with flexibility (for example, letting them skip the lunch hour so they can leave early).
  • Other examples (the book says they are “just a few of the many ways”): flexible work hours, the option to work from home, and cafeteria-style benefit plans (Chapter 12) — the employee chooses the benefits that suit him, like choosing your food in a cafeteria.
  • The goal: respond to employees' different needs and at the same time keep them effective contributors to the organization.
EXAM BOX

Many diversity awareness programs in organizations strive to increase managers’ and workers’ awareness of (1) their own attitudes, biases, and stereotypes and (2) the differing perspectives of diverse managers, employees, coworkers, and customers.

Efforts to increase diversity skills focus on improving how managers and their employees interact with each other and improving their ability to work with different kinds of people.

Managers and their employees must learn how to be open to different approaches and ways of doing things. This does not mean organizational members have to suppress their personal styles. Rather, it means they must be open to, and not feel threatened by, different approaches and perspectives and must have the patience and flexibility to understand and appreciate diverse perspectives.

TRAP
  • Awareness ≠ skills: awareness = you know your attitudes and biases and other people's perspectives. Skills = you actually interact and communicate (communication styles, language, nonverbal signals, misunderstandings). A scenario with “facial expressions and body language” or “resolving misunderstandings” → skills. A scenario with “uncover your bias” or “put yourself in someone else's place” → awareness.
  • The goals of awareness programs are six. A choice like “Eliminating all differences among employees” or “Teaching employees to adopt the majority culture” is not one of them.
  • Flexibility does not mean suppress their personal styles — this is the most famous trap in this step. Flexibility = openness and patience without feeling threatened.
  • Cafeteria-style benefit plans, flexible work hours, and work from home = examples of flexibility (step 5), not of rewards (step 9).
  • “Mentally walk in other people’s shoes” = one of the goals of awareness programs (number 5) — not the definition of mentoring or flexibility.

Evaluation, Numbers, Empowerment, and Rewards
Steps 6–9: measure fairly, count who is where, let everyone speak up, and reward those who manage diversity well
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Evaluate with objective indicators · count women and minorities at every level and ask why · build an organizational culture with zero tolerance for discrimination · and tie rewards to managing diversity.
🧠
A fair scale to evaluate, a notebook to count, a whistle for every employee to blow on discrimination, and a bonus envelope for the winners.
EXPLANATION — Step 6: Pay close attention to how organizational members are evaluated
  • Whenever possible, rely on objective performance indicators (Chapter 12) — because they are less subject to bias.
  • When objective indicators are not available or not appropriate, the manager makes sure of these things (the book does not number them; we count three points to help you memorise):
    1. enough time and attention go into evaluating employees' performance.
    2. evaluators are held accountable for their evaluations.
    3. vague performance standards are avoided.
EXPLANATION — Step 7: Consider the numbers

You look at the numbers of members of different minority groups and women in:

  • various positions
  • various levels in the hierarchy
  • locations that differ in their desirability
  • any other relevant categories

These numbers tell the manager important information about possible problems and ways to fix them. And if certain groups are underrepresented in particular jobs or units, the manager must understand why and solve any problem he finds.

FROM THE BOOK'S BOX — Management Insight: What Gets in the Way of Promoting Women?

The book puts this box here to show you what can come out when you analyze employment data:

The numberWhat it means
87 : 100the gap starts at the first level of management: 87 women are promoted to first-line management for every 100 men
One-fourththe pattern accelerates, and in upper management only one-fourth of the positions are held by women

Companies set diversity goals, trained people on unconscious biases, and encouraged female employees to be more assertive about asking for promotions — and still they keep missing their female employees' leadership potential. Research gives two reasons, and from them come two ideas for a solution:

The reasonThe suggested solution
1 Three Australian economists: organizations invite candidates to put themselves forward, and women are much less likely than men with similar experience to speak up. Asking them to be bolder does not help much if they were raised to value modesty or to avoid risk. Replace the opt-in method (sign yourself up) with an opt-out approach: everyone with a given set of experience and accomplishments is considered a candidate unless he asks to be excluded. Research on decision making says people are less likely to pass up an opportunity if they have to opt out themselves.
2 A researcher at MIT Sloan School of Management: in a large retail chain, evaluations said women outscored men on performance but ranked lower on “potential” — and promotions were strongly linked to potential. When women and men had the same potential score, the women on average later outperformed those men — a missed opportunity for the company. In other words, promotions were based on scores that undervalued the female employees. Improve the assessment of “potential”: potential is a vague concept, and to be a valid basis for important decisions it needs a precise and relevant definition. And keep data on assessments, promotions, and later performance to find patterns that improve future decisions.

And the benefit the book mentions: promotion decisions that are fairer to employees and at the same time put the best candidates in management.

EXPLANATION — Step 8: Empower employees to challenge discriminatory behaviors, actions, and remarks
  • When a manager or employee witnesses another member being treated unfairly, he should be encouraged to speak up and rectify the situation.
  • Top managers make this happen by building an organizational culture (Chapter 3) with zero tolerance for discrimination.
  • As part of this culture, members feel empowered to challenge discriminatory behavior — whether it is aimed at them or they see it aimed at another employee.
EXPLANATION — Step 9: Reward employees for effectively managing diversity
  • The logic: if managing diversity is a valued organizational objective, employees should be rewarded for their contributions to it.
  • The book's example: after settling a major race discrimination lawsuit, the Coca-Cola Company now ties managers' pay to their achievement of diversity goals.
  • Other companies that tie managers' compensation to diversity goals: McDonald's, General Motors, and Dentsu.

Vague performance standards should be avoided.

TRAP
  • In evaluation: objective first (objective performance indicators) because it is less subject to bias. If there is none, the conditions (not numbered in the book; we count three to help you memorise) are: enough time and attention · evaluators held accountable · no vague standards. A choice that says “use vague standards to allow flexibility” is the opposite of the book.
  • Consider the numbers = you look at the distribution (positions · levels · locations · other categories) and understand why — not force quotas.
  • In the box: 87 per 100 = the first level of management (first-line). One-fourth = upper management. And women are higher on performance and lower on potential — do not reverse them. The solution is opt-out, not opt-in.
  • Empowerment includes the witness too: whether the behavior is directed at them or they witness it. A choice that says “only the victim may object” is incomplete.
  • Coca-Cola tied managers' pay to diversity goals after settling a race discrimination lawsuit — an example of reward (step 9), not of training or mentoring.

Steps 10–11: training with more than one tool that never stops, and an experienced guide who takes the less experienced by the hand

EXPLANATION — Step 11: Encourage mentoring of diverse employees

The problem: African Americans and other underrepresented groups are still less likely to reach high-level positions, and for those who do, the climb up the corporate ladder takes longer than it does for white men.

The researcher: David Thomas — formerly a professor at Harvard Business School and now the president of Morehouse College in Atlanta. He studied the careers of underrepresented groups in corporate America, and one of his main conclusions: mentoring is very important for underrepresented groups, and most of those who reached high levels did so through a solid network of mentors and contacts.

  • Mentor = the experienced one who gives. Protégé = the less experienced one who receives. The exam flips them easily.

  • Organizations use outside consultants in addition to their in-house experts — not “instead of” them.
  • Experiential exercises aim to uncover hidden biases; accurate information debunks stereotypes.

Sexual Harassment
Unethical and illegal; only two forms, and four steps a manager uses to end it
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Sexual harassment has two forms: quid pro quo = a trade (“do this, or else…”), and hostile work environment = a work climate that is intimidating, hostile, or offensive. And the manager, together with HR, ends it with four steps: policy · fair complaint procedure · fast correction · training.
🧠
Quid pro quo: a forced deal, “this for that.” Hostile: the office air itself is poisoned, even if nobody asked you for anything.
EXPLANATION — HOW BIG IS THE PROBLEM?

Sexual harassment seriously damages the people who are harassed and the reputation of the organization where it happens, and it can cost organizations billions.

  • The #MeToo movement: it began in late 2017 after many women in the film industry accused Hollywood entertainment mogul Harvey Weinstein of sexual harassment and assault over the previous three decades. Women were encouraged to tweet about their experiences, and in the first 24 hours the hashtag was used more than 12 million times. In 2020 he was convicted of sex crimes in New York and is now serving a 23-year prison sentence, and later he was found guilty of 3 of 7 charges in a trial in Los Angeles. The book's photo caption says the movement still helps bring the issue out of the shadows and into the mainstream and raise public awareness.
  • But harassment is not limited to the entertainment industry. The book's numbers:
The numberWhat it means
54% – 81%most women report experiencing some level of sexual harassment at work
58% – 72%of female victims do not report workplace harassment
75%of adults believe their companies take harassment seriously
30%only this share of women strongly agreed that their employers handled the incidents properly
56%of men believe that reported harassment goes unpunished
nearly $300 millionrecovered by the EEOC for victims between 2018 and 2021 — even though victims are reluctant to report
  • The victim can be of any gender, and the harasser does not have to be of the opposite sex.
  • But women are the most frequent victims, especially those in male-dominated occupations or in positions stereotypically linked to certain gender relationships — like a female employee reporting to a male manager.
  • Sexual harassment is not only unethical but also illegal. And the manager has an ethical obligation to make sure that he, his coworkers, and his employees never engage in it, even unintentionally.

EXPLANATION — Steps Managers Can Take to Eradicate Sexual Harassment (four)

Managers, in collaboration with the HR staff, have an ethical obligation to eradicate harassment in their organizations. There are many ways, and the book gives four steps:

#The step in EnglishWhat it means + the details
1Develop and clearly communicate a sexual harassment policy endorsed by top management. Make a harassment policy that is endorsed by top management and communicate it clearly. It prohibits both forms (quid pro quo and hostile work environment), and it has four parts:
  1. examples of unacceptable behavior
  2. a procedure employees use to report harassment
  3. an explanation of the disciplinary actions that will be taken when harassment happens
  4. a commitment to educate and train members about harassment
2Use a fair complaint procedure to investigate charges of sexual harassment. Use a fair complaint procedure to investigate. The procedure must (four conditions):
  1. be managed by a neutral third party
  2. deal with complaints promptly and thoroughly
  3. protect victims and treat them fairly
  4. make sure alleged harassers are treated fairly too
3When it has been determined that sexual harassment has taken place, take corrective actions as soon as possible. When it is proven that harassment happened, take corrective actions as soon as possible. They vary with the severity of the harassment. If it was extensive, prolonged, of a quid pro quo nature, or severely objectionable in some other way → the action may include firing the harasser.
4Provide sexual harassment education and training to all organizational members, including managers. Provide harassment education and training to all members — including managers. And most Fortune 500 firms now provide it to their employees.

The Purple Campaign: an organization whose mission is to address workplace harassment in three ways: (1) stronger corporate policies, (2) better laws, (3) empowering people to create real and lasting change in their work environments and communities. Its partners: Amazon, Expedia, General Electric, Airbnb, Instacart, Lyft, Uber, Spotify.

EXAM BOX

Sexual harassment is not only unethical but also illegal. There are two basic forms of sexual harassment: quid pro quo sexual harassment and hostile work environment sexual harassment.

Quid pro quo sexual harassment occurs when a harasser asks or forces an employee to perform sexual favors to keep a job, receive a promotion, receive a raise, obtain some other work-related opportunity, or avoid receiving negative consequences such as demotion or dismissal.

Hostile work environment sexual harassment can be more subtle. It occurs when organizational members face an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment because of their sex.

Steps that managers can take to eradicate sexual harassment include development and communication of a sexual harassment policy endorsed by top management, use of fair complaint procedures, prompt corrective action when harassment occurs, and sexual harassment training and education.

TRAP
  • Two forms of harassment, four steps to end it, four parts of the policy inside step 1, and four conditions of the complaint procedure inside step 2. Three lists of four — do not mix them.
  • Quid pro quo = there is an exchange (a job, a promotion, a raise, or avoiding demotion or dismissal) → the more extreme type. Hostile work environment = a general climate with no exchange → the more subtle type. If the scenario has jokes, pictures, or comments about appearance and no “exchange” → hostile.
  • The fair complaint procedure protects both the victim and the accused. The fourth condition (alleged harassers are fairly treated) is the one students forget, and a choice that says “assume the accused is guilty” is wrong. And it is managed by a neutral third party — not the victim's manager and not the accused's manager.
  • Firing (firing the harasser) in step 3 is for serious cases (extensive · prolonged · quid pro quo · severely objectionable) — not a rule for every case.
  • Training is for all members, including managers. And the policy is endorsed by top management — not by HR alone.
  • The victim can be of any gender, and the harasser does not have to be of the opposite sex. And Tesla ($137 million → $3.2 million) is an example of a hostile environment based on racism, not sex.

1. According to Table 5.4, which of the following is NOT one of the steps managers can take to promote the effective management of diversity?
  1. Consider the numbers
  2. Require employees to suppress their personal styles
  3. Encourage mentoring of employees with diverse backgrounds
  4. Pay close attention to how employees are evaluated
Answer

B. Table 5.4 has 11 items, and none of them is about suppressing your personal style — in fact, in the step Encourage flexibility the book says clearly This does not mean organizational members have to suppress their personal styles. The trap: the word “flexibility” makes you feel you must give up your style. A, C, and D are items in the table word for word (the 7th, the 11th, and the 6th). And the question uses NOT, so do not pick the first item you recognize.

2. A company runs workshops that teach employees how coworkers may differ in communication styles, language fluency, and nonverbal signals such as facial expressions and body language, and how to resolve misunderstandings as they occur. These workshops are mainly aimed at:
  1. Increasing diversity awareness
  2. Increasing diversity skills
  3. Securing top management commitment
  4. Considering the numbers
Answer

B. Communication (styles, language, nonverbal signals) + resolving misunderstandings = diversity skills — improving how people interact with each other and their ability to work with different kinds of people. The trap in A: awareness is close, but it is about knowing your own attitudes, biases, and stereotypes and other people's perspectives, not about the communication skill itself. C is about top managers and their values, and D is about counting women and minorities in positions and levels.

3. Rana, a senior manager with 20 years of experience at a bank, meets regularly with Khalid, a new analyst from an underrepresented group. She advises him on how to advance in the organization and in his career, and they have built a close, supportive relationship in which he feels free to share his fears and concerns. In this relationship, Khalid is best described as the:
  1. Mentor
  2. Protégé
  3. Liaison
  4. Spokesperson
Answer

B. Khalid is the less experienced member who receives the advice and guidance = protégé. Rana, the experienced one who gives, = mentor. The trap in A: reversing the roles — the most common mistake with this definition. C and D are traps from lesson 2: liaison and spokesperson are manager roles in Table 5.2, not the two sides of a mentoring relationship. And notice the question mentions “a close relationship where he shares his fears” — this is the side Thomas says is equally important.

4. Workers in a warehouse often tell lewd jokes and make sexually oriented remarks about a female coworker’s appearance. No one has asked her for anything or threatened her job, but she finds the workplace intimidating and offensive, and it is hurting her ability to do her job. This is best described as:
  1. Quid pro quo sexual harassment
  2. Hostile work environment sexual harassment
  3. Overt discrimination
  4. Implicit bias
Answer

B. Lewd jokes + sexual remarks about appearance + an intimidating… or offensive environment that hurts her work, and no exchange = hostile work environment sexual harassment. The trap in A: quid pro quo needs a trade (a promotion, a raise, keeping the job, or avoiding punishment), and the question says clearly that no one asked her for anything or threatened her job. C is a trap from lesson 3: overt discrimination = knowingly and willingly denying opportunities and outcomes, not an offensive climate. And D is an unconscious bias, while the jokes and comments here are open actions.

5. A fair complaint procedure to investigate charges of sexual harassment should do all of the following EXCEPT:
  1. Be managed by a neutral third party
  2. Ensure that complaints are dealt with promptly and thoroughly
  3. Protect and fairly treat victims
  4. Assume that alleged harassers are guilty until the investigation ends
Answer

D. The four conditions in the book: a neutral third party · prompt and thorough · protect and fairly treat victims · and fair treatment of the accused (ensure that alleged harassers are fairly treated). D is the opposite of the fourth condition. The trap: students think a fair procedure protects only the victim and forget the accused. A, B, and C come word for word from the list. And do not mix this list with the four parts of the policy (examples · reporting · disciplinary actions · education).
