Chapter 3 — Moods, Emotions, and Emotional Intelligence

MGT 102 · Chapter 3 · Section 3 of 4: Moods, Emotions, and Emotional Intelligence
Objective LO3-3: Appreciate how moods and emotions influence all members of an organization.
Objective LO3-4: Describe the nature of emotional intelligence and its role in management.

This section is all about telling apart two words that look the same

So far in this chapter you studied personality traits, then values and attitudes. Now comes the last piece of the manager as a person: how the manager actually feels while managing — moods and emotions — and then the ability that lets the manager deal with those feelings: emotional intelligence.

There are only two kinds of questions here: definition questions (they give you a definition and ask for the term, or they give you a scenario and ask which term fits), and figure numbers (how many items? which item goes with which mood? what are the four dimensions?). Memorize the numbers from now: three differences between a mood and emotions, 12 items in Figure 3.4 (6 positive + 6 negative), 16 items and four dimensions in Figure 3.5, two halves in the definition of emotional intelligence, and three interpersonal roles that it helps with.

Moods and Emotions
The difference is three words: strength · cause · length
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A mood is a general feeling that walks with you for hours or a whole day; an emotion is a stronger and shorter feeling that is tied directly to something that happened.
🧠
Someone hits your car: the hot second of anger is the emotion. The heavy rest of the day is the mood.
EXPLANATION

The book opens the section with a simple example: just as you are sometimes in a bad mood and at other times in a good mood, so are managers. Then it gives you two very short definitions — learn them word for word in English, because they come on the exam as they are:

TermThe definition, word for wordWhat it means
mood A feeling or state of mind. General, longer, and usually not tied to one clear cause.
emotions Intense, relatively short-lived feelings. Strong feelings that do not last long, and that are tied to their cause.

And the difference between them is exactly three points — not more, not fewer:

  1. Strength: emotions are more intense than moods.
  2. The link to the cause: emotions are often directly linked to whatever caused the emotion; a mood is not necessarily linked to one cause.
  3. Length: emotions are more short-lived (they end faster).

The relation between the two (a point they love on the exam): once whatever has triggered the emotion has been dealt with, the feeling does not disappear — it may linger in the form of a less intense mood.

The book's own example: a manager gets very angry when an employee has engaged in an unethical behavior. Once the manager decides how to address the problem, the anger decreases in intensity — but the manager stays in a bad mood for the rest of the day, even without thinking directly about the incident. So: the emotion is gone, the mood is still there.

EXAM BOX

A mood is a feeling or state of mind. When people are in a positive mood, they feel excited, enthusiastic, active, or elated. When people are in a negative mood, they feel distressed, fearful, scornful, hostile, jittery, or nervous.

Emotions are more intense feelings than moods, are often directly linked to whatever caused the emotion, and are more short-lived. However, once whatever has triggered the emotion has been dealt with, the feelings may linger in the form of a less intense mood.

TRAP
  • Do not flip the two definitions. mood = a feeling or state of mind (general, longer, not necessarily tied to one cause). emotions = intense, relatively short-lived feelings. A choice that says "moods are intense and short-lived" is wrong.
  • The differences are three: strength, link to the cause, length. Any choice that adds a fourth difference (for example "moods happen only to employees") is wrong.
  • The right direction: an emotion turns into a less intense mood — not the opposite. A choice that says "a mood turns into a more intense emotion" is wrong.
  • Do not mix mood with attitude (the section before). An attitude = a collection of feelings and beliefs about your job and your organization; a mood = how you feel right now.

Positive and Negative Mood
Two separate scores — not two ends of one line
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Each mood has its own words; personality makes one mood more likely, but the situation also has a say.
🧠
A pay raise puts almost anyone in a good mood. The situation can beat the trait for a day.
EXPLANATION — THE WORDS OF EACH MOOD

The book gives you the very words a person feels in each mood, and these words are the items of the scale in Figure 3.4. Learn which word belongs to which mood:

Positive moodNegative mood
excited · enthusiastic · active · elated distressed · fearful · scornful · hostile · jittery · nervous

What is the link between mood and personality? (a direct link back to the first section of the chapter)

  • A person who scores high on negative affectivity is especially likely to experience negative moods — likely, not always.
  • People's situations or circumstances also determine their moods. The book's example: receiving a raise is likely to put most people in a good mood regardless of their personality traits.
  • And word for word from the book: people who are high on negative affectivity are not always in a bad mood, and people who are low on extraversion still experience positive moods.
Figure 3.4 — A Measure of Positive and Negative Mood at Work

This is Figure 3.4 in the book. The person answers 12 items, and shows how well each item describes how they felt at work during the past week, on a 5-point scale: 1 = Very slightly or not at all · 2 = A little · 3 = Moderately · 4 = Quite a bit · 5 = Very much.
The 12 items in order: 1. Active · 2. Distressed · 3. Strong · 4. Excited · 5. Scornful · 6. Hostile · 7. Enthusiastic · 8. Fearful · 9. Peppy · 10. Nervous · 11. Elated · 12. Jittery.
Scoring: you sum items 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 11 (six items) to get the positive mood score — the higher the score, the more positive mood is experienced at work. And you sum items 2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12 (six items) to get the negative mood score.
The most important point when you read it: positive and negative are two separate scores, not two ends of one line — so a person can score high on both. And what gets asked: the number 12 and the 6 + 6 split, and which word belongs to which mood (Elated → positive, Jittery → negative).

EXAM BOX

People who score high on negative affectivity are especially likely to experience negative moods. People's situations or circumstances also determine their moods; however, receiving a raise is likely to put most people in a good mood regardless of their personality traits. People who are high on negative affectivity are not always in a bad mood, and people who are low on extraversion still experience positive moods.

TRAP
  • The word "always" is a trap. The book says especially likely and not always. A choice that says "people high on negative affectivity are always in a bad mood" is wrong.
  • Do not mix Figure 3.4 with Figure 3.2: Figure 3.4 = 12 items about mood at work, a 5-point scale. Figure 3.2 (the first section) = 40 items about four of the Big Five traits.
  • The items are 6 positive + 6 negative = 12. Any other number is wrong. And watch out: Strong and Peppy are positive (items 3 and 9) even though the book does not name them in the paragraph.
  • Scornful and Hostile are negative — they do not mean "strong" or "firm". Do not get lost in the translation.

How Moods and Emotions Affect Behavior
And the biggest one: a negative mood is not all bad
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A mood is not a private thing — it moves from the manager to the employees, and it affects performance, creativity, ethical decisions, and even who dares to talk to whom.
🧠
An employee at the boss's door: boss looks happy, she walks in; boss looks angry, she keeps the paper.
EXPLANATION — FIVE RESEARCH FINDINGS THE BOOK GIVES

This is the core of LO3-3: how moods and emotions affect all members of the organization, not just the manager.

  1. Performance and staying in the job: the employees of managers who experience positive moods at work may perform at somewhat higher levels and be less likely to resign and leave the organization than the employees of managers who do not tend to be in a positive mood at work.
  2. Creativity: under certain conditions creativity might be enhanced by positive moods, whereas under other conditions negative moods might push people to work harder to come up with truly creative ideas. And recent research says an employee is especially likely to be creative when he experiences both mood states (at different times) on the job, and when the work environment is supportive of creativity.
  3. Ethical decision making: researchers at Princeton University found that when people try to solve difficult personal moral dilemmas, the parts of their brains that are responsible for emotions and moods are especially active.
  4. A mood is a signal: emotions and moods give the manager and all employees important information and signals about what is going on in the workplace:
    • Positive → a signal that things are going well, so it leads to more expansive, and even playful, thinking.
    • Negative → a signal that there are problems in need of attention and areas for improvement, so people become more detail-oriented and focused on the facts at hand.
    • And some studies say that critical thinking and devil's advocacy (one person on purpose takes the side against the idea, to test it and break it) may be promoted by a negative mood, and that managers in negative moods sometimes make especially accurate judgments.
  5. Its effect on relationships: managers and all members of an organization need to realize that how they feel affects how they treat others and how others respond to them. The book's example: an employee is more likely to approach the manager with a somewhat unusual but potentially useful idea if she thinks the manager is in a good mood. And the opposite: when managers are in very bad moods, their employees might try to avoid them at all costs.

And right after this paragraph the book points you to Figure 3.4 as an example of a scale that can measure the extent to which a person experiences positive and negative moods at work.

EXAM BOX

Research has found that moods and emotions affect the behavior of managers and everyone else in an organization. The employees of managers who experience positive moods at work may perform at somewhat higher levels and be less likely to resign and leave the organization than the employees of managers who do not tend to be in a positive mood at work. Under certain conditions, creativity might be enhanced by positive moods, whereas under other conditions negative moods might push people to work harder to come up with truly creative ideas.

Positive emotions and moods signal that things are going well and thus can lead to more expansive, and even playful, thinking. Negative emotions and moods signal that there are problems in need of attention and areas for improvement. So when people are in negative moods, they tend to be more detail-oriented and focused on the facts at hand. Some studies suggest that critical thinking and devil's advocacy may be promoted by a negative mood, and sometimes especially accurate judgments may be made by managers in negative moods.

TRAP
  • The biggest trap in the section: "a negative mood is always bad" is wrong. The book says it pushes people to the details and the facts, it promotes critical thinking and devil's advocacy, and it sometimes gives more accurate judgments.
  • Creativity: the right answer is not "positive only". The book says both states contribute in different ways, and the best case is an employee who experiences both at different times plus a work environment that supports creativity.
  • Princeton = the study about ethical decision making and the brain. Do not mix it with Gallup (job satisfaction and burnout, in the section before).
  • "somewhat higher levels" — the book is careful. A choice that says a positive mood "guarantees high performance" is wrong.
  • The effect is on everyone (all members of an organization), exactly as LO3-3 says. A choice that says mood affects only the manager is wrong.

Emotional Intelligence
Two halves in the definition: your own feelings + other people's feelings
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Emotional intelligence = the ability to understand and manage your own moods and emotions, and the moods and emotions of other people — both halves must be in the answer.
🧠
A captain calms his own nerves before the match, then reads his players' fear and lifts them. Two halves.
EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITION AND WHAT IT GIVES

The book enters this topic through the section before: to understand the effects of managers' and all employees' moods and emotions, it is important to take into account their levels of emotional intelligence.

The definition — learn it word for word: emotional intelligence — The ability to understand and manage one's own moods and emotions and the moods and emotions of other people.

So it is an ability — not a fixed personality trait. It has two verbs (understand + manage) and two halves (yourself + other people). Put those four together and you will not get it wrong.

What does it give the manager? The book lists it in this order:

  1. A manager with a high level of emotional intelligence is more likely to understand how he is feeling and why, and is more able to manage his feelings effectively.
  2. When he experiences stressful feelings such as fear or anxiety, emotional intelligence lets him understand why and manage these feelings so they do not get in the way of effective decision making.
  3. It helps him perform his important roles, and the book names the interpersonal roles: figurehead · leader · liaison — the three interpersonal roles of Mintzberg from Chapter 1. And understanding how your employees feel, why they feel that way, and how to manage these feelings is central to developing strong interpersonal bonds with them.
  4. For work that involves encounters with people from different cultures, this effort is more complex but just as important (the details are in the next concept).
  5. It has the potential to contribute to effective leadership in multiple ways, and it can help managers make lasting contributions to society.
  6. It helps managers understand and relate well to other people, helps them maintain their enthusiasm and confidence, and helps them energize their employees so the organization attains its goals.
  7. Recent theorizing and research suggest that it is especially important in awakening employee creativity.

The book's main example: Satya Nadella, the CEO of Microsoft. He is credited with drastically changing the company's organizational culture: from a culture filled with arrogance and cut-throat rivalries among various departments and functions, to a culture filled with openness, collaboration, and innovation — and how? by focusing on self-awareness and empathy. And the book closes the section with one line: managers themselves are increasingly recognizing the importance of emotional intelligence.

Figure 3.5 — A Measure of Emotional Intelligence

This is Figure 3.5 in the book. The person shows how much he agrees or disagrees with each item. The items are 16, and the scale has 7 points (not 5!): 1 = Totally disagree · 2 = Disagree · 3 = Somewhat disagree · 4 = Neither agree nor disagree · 5 = Somewhat agree · 6 = Agree · 7 = Totally agree.
Scoring — four subscales, four items each:
1. Self-emotions appraisal (judging your own feelings) = items 1, 5, 9, 13
2. Others-emotions appraisal (judging other people's feelings) = items 2, 6, 10, 14
3. Use of emotion = items 3, 7, 11, 15
4. Regulation of emotion (how well you regulate your feelings) = items 4, 8, 12, 16
How to read it: these four are a practical breakdown of the definition — the "yourself" half (1, 3 and 4) and the "other people" half (2). Example items: "I have a good sense of why I have certain feelings most of the time." (subscale 1) · "I am a good observer of others' emotions." (subscale 2) · "I am a self-motivating person." (subscale 3) · "I am able to control my temper so that I can handle difficulties rationally." (subscale 4) · "I am sensitive to the feelings and emotions of others." (subscale 2).
What gets asked: the names of the four subscales, the number 16, and the 1–7 scale.

EXAM BOX

Emotional intelligence is the ability to understand and manage one's own moods and emotions and the moods and emotions of other people. Managers with a high level of emotional intelligence are more likely to understand how they are feeling and why, and they are more able to effectively manage their feelings. When managers are experiencing stressful feelings and emotions such as fear or anxiety, emotional intelligence lets them understand why and manage these feelings so they do not get in the way of effective decision making.

Emotional intelligence also can help managers perform their important roles such as their interpersonal roles (figurehead, leader, and liaison). Emotional intelligence helps managers understand and relate well to other people. It also helps managers maintain their enthusiasm and confidence and energize their employees to help the organization attain its goals.

TRAP
  • The definition has two halves. If a choice says "the ability to understand and manage one's own moods and emotions" and stops there — it is incomplete and it is not the answer. It must also say and the moods and emotions of other people.
  • Emotional intelligence is an ability — not a fixed personality trait like the Big Five. A choice that calls it a personality trait is wrong.
  • The three roles are interpersonal: figurehead · leader · liaison. Do not put monitor there (that one is informational) or negotiator (that one is decisional).
  • The scale: Figure 3.5 has 7 points, while Figure 3.2 and Figure 3.4 have 5 points. The exam loves this detail.
  • The four subscales have nothing to do with DiSC (dominance · influence · steadiness · conscientiousness) or with the MBTI. Do not mix the "fours".
  • Satya Nadella = emotional intelligence that changed an organizational culture (it links LO3-4 to LO3-5). His tools: self-awareness and empathy.

Emotional Intelligence Varies by Culture
Feelings are similar — what changes is the expression
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
People in all cultures feel the same things, but every culture teaches its members what to show in front of others.
🧠
A Chinese employee says nothing about a new project. His American manager reads it as "not interested" — but in his culture, showing off is rude.
EXPLANATION — THE MANAGING GLOBALLY BOX

Definitions of emotional intelligence are based on the work of U.S. researchers, but the idea has attracted the interest of managers, psychologists, and educators around the world. And while managing and reading emotions is important globally, the specific emotional information tends to vary by culture.

The exampleThe cultureWhat happens
enthusiasm
الحماس		 United States People express it openly, and this is considered both normal and desirable. Hiring managers look favorably on job candidates who express enthusiasm, and people who act excited about a new project or product are admired for being devoted to the company's vision.
Britain British businesspeople tend to tone down their emotions.
China Chinese people might interpret enthusiastic behavior as showing off rather than fitting in with the group.
smiling
الابتسامة		 North America People smile often to signal they are friendly and well intentioned.
Northern Europe This looks odd, because there a smile has the more limited purpose of signaling happiness.
Japan A smile is more often a way to cover up embarrassment or unhappiness.

Why do they differ? (the most important line in the box): evidence suggests that differences such as these are less about what people feel — feelings are similar across cultures — and more about what they learn to express around others. And those differences in expression are tied to cultural values (a link back to the second section of the chapter):

  • British people value mature self-control, so they don't broadcast enthusiasm.
  • In the United States, enthusiasm is more compatible with the high value placed on individual success and happiness.
  • In much of Asia, group harmony is valued over individuality, so open celebrations of personal success can seem inappropriate.

An important result: as people practice different culture-based behaviors, they tend to develop different areas of strength in emotional intelligence. Travis Bradberry, president of TalentSmart, compared test scores of U.S. and Chinese managers and found that the Chinese managers scored higher in aspects of emotional intelligence focused on the feelings of others (that is the others-emotions appraisal subscale in Figure 3.5).

How do you build emotional intelligence across cultures? Three steps:

  1. Recognize that unexpected behavior might indicate that emotions are being expressed differently. The book's example: if a U.S. manager notices that a Chinese employee seems unenthusiastic about a new project, the manager should check the assumption that absence of enthusiastic behavior signals lack of interest.
  2. Follow-up questions can be an effective way to find out the truth.
  3. Whenever situations involve interacting with people from different cultures, it is worthwhile to learn each culture's emotional norms.
EXAM BOX

Although definitions of emotional intelligence are based on the work of U.S. researchers, the idea has attracted the interest of managers, psychologists, and educators around the world. While managing and reading emotions is important globally, the specific emotional information tends to vary by culture.

Evidence suggests that differences such as these are less about what people feel (feelings are similar across cultures) and more about what they learn to express around others. Those differences in expression are tied to cultural values. TRAP
  • The difference is in the expression, not in the feeling. A choice that says "people in different cultures feel different emotions" is wrong. The book is clear: feelings are similar across cultures.
  • Who scored higher on the "feelings of others" side? The Chinese managers. Do not flip it to the Americans.
  • Open enthusiasm is desirable in the U.S., toned down by the British, and read as showing off by the Chinese. Three cultures, three reactions — do not swap them.
  • Smiling: North America = friendly · Northern Europe = happiness only · Japan = covering up embarrassment. These are three more regions they like to swap.
  • Travis Bradberry = TalentSmart. Do not mix him with Satya Nadella (Microsoft) or with the Princeton researchers.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 1 or 5, go back to the trap "an emotion turns into a less intense mood".

1. A manager becomes very angry when an employee is caught behaving unethically. After the manager decides how to handle the problem, the anger fades, but the manager stays in a bad mood for the rest of the day without thinking about the incident. This is best described as:
  1. An attitude turning into a value
  2. An emotion lingering in the form of a less intense mood
  3. A mood becoming a more intense emotion
  4. High negative affectivity replacing low emotional intelligence
Answer

B. This is the book's own example, word for word: once whatever has triggered the emotion has been dealt with, the feeling stays in the form of a less intense mood. The trap is in C: the same words, but the direction is flipped — the emotion is the stronger and shorter one, and it is the one that turns into a mood, not the other way round. A is a trap that mixes terms from the section before (attitude and value). And D pushes a personality trait and an ability into a scenario that mentions neither of them.

2. According to Figure 3.4, which of the following items is summed into the positive mood score?
  1. Jittery
  2. Scornful
  3. Peppy
  4. Distressed
Answer

C. In Figure 3.4 the positive mood score is the sum of items 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 11, and Peppy is item 9. All the others are from the six negative items (2, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12): Distressed = 2, Scornful = 5, Jittery = 12. The trap: the word Peppy is strange to you, so you run away from it — and Strong (item 3) is positive too, even though the book does not name it in the paragraph. Remember: 12 items = 6 + 6, on a 5-point scale.

3. A manager notices that she is feeling anxious before a difficult meeting, works out why, and calms herself down so the anxiety does not spoil her decision. She also senses that two of her employees are afraid of the change and reassures them. She is best described as having a high level of:
  1. Negative affectivity
  2. Internal locus of control
  3. Self-esteem
  4. Emotional intelligence
Answer

D. The question puts both halves of the definition in front of you: understanding and managing your own feelings (the anxiety before the meeting) and other people's feelings (the employees' fear). And "so it does not get in the way of effective decision making" is the book's own sentence about fear or anxiety. The trap is in A: the word anxious pulls you to negative affectivity, but that is a trait that makes you experience negative feelings — not an ability to manage them. B and C are traits from the first section (who controls your fate, and how you value yourself); neither of them has the "other people's feelings" half.

4. According to Figure 3.5, the measure of emotional intelligence is scored as four subscales. Which of the following is one of them?
  1. Steadiness
  2. Others-emotions appraisal
  3. Openness to experience
  4. Judging versus perceiving
Answer

B. The four subscales in Figure 3.5 are: Self-emotions appraisal (1, 5, 9, 13) · Others-emotions appraisal (2, 6, 10, 14) · Use of emotion (3, 7, 11, 15) · Regulation of emotion (4, 8, 12, 16). All the traps are "fours" from other sections: A Steadiness is from DiSC, D Judging versus perceiving is from the MBTI, and C Openness to experience is from the Big Five. And remember: Figure 3.5 = 16 items on a 1–7 scale, not 5.

5. Which of the following statements about moods, emotions, and emotional intelligence is NOT supported by the chapter?
  1. When people are in negative moods, they tend to be more detail-oriented and focused on the facts at hand.
  2. Emotional intelligence can help managers perform their interpersonal roles of figurehead, leader, and liaison.
  3. Differences in emotional expression across cultures mean that people in different cultures feel different emotions.
  4. Receiving a raise is likely to put most people in a good mood regardless of their personality traits.
Answer

C. The book says the opposite, word for word: feelings are similar across cultures — what differs is what people learn to express, and those differences are tied to cultural values. The trap: the question uses NOT and gives you three correct sentences so you rush and pick one of them. A is correct (a negative mood takes you to the details, the facts, and critical thinking), B is correct (Mintzberg's three interpersonal roles), and D is correct (the pay-raise example — the situation beats the trait).
