Chapter 6 — The Changing Global Environment

MGT 102 · Chapter 6 · Section 3 of 4: The Changing Global Environment
Objective LO6-4: Explain why the global environment is becoming more open and competitive, and identify the forces behind the process of globalization that increase the opportunities, complexities, challenges, and threats managers face.
Objective LO6-5: Discuss why national cultures differ and why it is important that managers be sensitive to the effects of falling trade barriers and regional trade associations on the political and social systems of nations around the world.

This section is “the world became one open market: what drives it, how you enter it, and which barriers fell”

The two lessons before this one described the environment for you: the task environment and the general environment. Globalization and the Four Forms of Capital
The world became one market, driven by people, money, resources, and power moving between countries
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Globalization = specific and general forces working together to integrate and connect the economic, political, and social systems of countries, so countries become more interdependent and more similar. And what drives it = the ebb and flow of capital in its four forms: human, financial, resource, and political.
🧠
Four trucks cross the border carrying people, money, and steel and car parts — and the fourth carries a diplomat with soldiers guarding them.
EXPLANATION — WHY HAS THE GLOBAL ENVIRONMENT BECOME “OPEN”?
  • The book opens the section with a strong sentence: the 21st century has banished the idea that the world is made of separate countries and markets, separated physically, economically, and culturally. Today companies compete in a truly global marketplace, and it is the source of the opportunities and threats managers must respond to.
  • Managers always face the challenges of global competition, and the book gives three examples: (1) establishing operations in a country abroad, (2) obtaining inputs from suppliers abroad, (3) managing in a different national culture.
  • Because of falling trade barriers, managers now see the global environment as open: companies are free to buy from and sell to any company and any country they choose, and free to compete with each other to attract customers all over the world.
  • All large companies must build an international network of operations and subsidiaries to build global competitive advantage. The book's examples: Coca-Cola and PepsiCo have competed aggressively for decades to build the strongest “global beverage empire”, and Toyota and Honda have built hundreds of car plants around the world.

The section's plan as the book announces it (it is also the order of the concepts in this lesson):

  1. The open environment = a result of globalization and the flow of capital (this concept).
  2. The four ways companies expand internationally (concepts 2, 3, and 4).
  3. How economic, political, and legal changes such as lowering barriers to trade and investment increased globalization (concept 5).
  4. How declining barriers of distance and culture sped up globalization, and what this means for managers (concept 6).
  5. And at the end of the chapter: countries still differ widely because they have different cultural values and norms — this is lesson 4.
EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITION OF GLOBALIZATION AND WHAT IT PRODUCES

The book says: perhaps the most important reason the global environment has become more open and competitive is the increase in globalization.

  • Result one — being interdependent: nations and peoples become interdependent because the same forces affect them in similar ways. And the fates of peoples in different countries become interlinked as the world's markets and businesses become more connected.

  • Notice the words specific and general forces: specific and general forces together, not only the general ones. They remind you of the chapter's division you studied in lessons 1 and 2: the forces of the task environment and the forces of the general environment.
EXPLANATION — WHAT DRIVES GLOBALIZATION? CAPITAL AND ITS FOUR FORMS

The book asks: what makes companies such as IKEA, Toyota, and Microsoft want to enter an uncertain global environment? The answer: the path of globalization is shaped by the ebb and flow of capital. Managers, employees, and companies such as Hyundai and Samsung are motivated to profit by using their skills to make products that customers around the world want to buy.

EXPLANATION — HOW FAST CAPITAL FLOWS: A GOOD SIDE AND A BAD SIDE

Most of the economic progress linked to globalization is the result of these four flows and the interactions between them, as countries compete on the world stage to protect and raise their standards of living and to serve the political goals and social causes their cultures espouse.

What the book says
The positive side
In a positive sense		 The faster the flow, the more capital is used where it creates the most value: people move to where their skills earn more money, investors switch to the stocks or bonds that pay higher dividends or interest, and companies find lower-cost sources of inputs.
The negative side
In a negative sense		 A fast flow also means a country or region can find itself in trouble when companies and investors move their capital to other countries — often those with lower labor costs or rapidly expanding markets.

And when capital leaves a country, there are three results (memorize them): (1) higher unemployment, (2) recession, (3) a lower standard of living for its people.

EXAM BOX

Perhaps the most important reason the global environment has become more open and competitive is the increase in globalization. Globalization is the set of specific and general forces that work together to integrate and connect economic, political, and social systems across countries, cultures, or geographic regions. The result of globalization is that nations and peoples become increasingly interdependent because the same forces affect them in similar ways.

The path of globalization is shaped by the ebb and flow of capital—valuable wealth-generating assets or resources that people move through companies, countries, and world regions to seek their greatest returns or profits.

The four principal forms of capital that flow between countries are these: Human capital: the flow of people around the world through immigration, migration, and emigration. Financial capital: the flow of money capital across world markets through overseas investment, credit, lending, and aid. Resource capital: the flow of natural resources, parts, and components between companies and countries, such as metals, minerals, lumber, energy, food products, microprocessors, and auto parts. Political capital: the flow of power and influence around the world using diplomacy, persuasion, aggression, and force of arms to protect the right or access of a country, world region, or political bloc to the other forms of capital.

When capital leaves a country, the results are higher unemployment, recession, and a lower standard of living for its people.

TRAP
  • The forms of capital are exactly four: human · financial · resource · political. Any choice such as social capital, technological capital, or intellectual capital = not on the list.
  • Human capital here = the flow of people themselves (immigration and migration) — not “training employees”. And microprocessors and auto parts = resource capital, not financial. And aid and lending = financial.
  • Political capital = power and influence (diplomacy, persuasion, aggression, arms) that protects access to the other forms — it is the one that “guards” the other three.
  • The result of globalization: countries become interdependent and similar — a choice that says “more independent” or “more different” = the opposite of the book. (But careful: lesson 4 says cultures still differ widely despite this similarity.)
  • The forces in the definition are specific and general together — not only general.
  • When capital leaves: higher unemployment + recession + a lower standard of living (three). And the fast flow has two sides, not only a positive one.

Global Strategy vs. Multidomestic Strategy
One product with the same marketing for the whole world — or a product and marketing made for each country?
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Before you choose how to enter a foreign market, answer one question: how much do you customize your product and marketing for each country? Global strategy = the same product and the same marketing everywhere (cost savings). Multidomestic strategy = customize for each country (market share or higher prices, but higher costs). The advantages of one = the disadvantages of the other, in reverse.
🧠
Rolex sells with the same ad everywhere, just in a new language; Maggi noodles failed in India until they tasted of curry.
EXPLANATION — THE BASIC QUESTION AND THE TWO DEFINITIONS

Just as planning vertical integration, diversification, or concentrating on the core business is a hard task, corporate-level managers must also decide on the appropriate way to compete internationally. And the question that confronts any organization that sells abroad and competes in more than one national market is:

To what extent should the organization customize features of its products and marketing campaign to different national conditions?
That is: how much should the organization change its product features and its marketing campaign to fit each country's conditions?

Global strategy
الاستراتيجية العالمية		 Selling the same standardized product and using the same basic marketing approach in each national market. You sell the same standardized product and use the same basic marketing approach in every national market. The company does little, if any, customization.
Multidomestic strategy
الاستراتيجية متعدّدة المحلية		 Customizing products and marketing strategies to specific national conditions. You customize products and marketing strategies to each specific country's conditions.
EXPLANATION — THE EXAMPLES, ADVANTAGES, AND DISADVANTAGES (MEMORIZE THE TABLE)
Global strategyMultidomestic strategy
The book's example Panasonic: sells the same basic TVs, camcorders, and DVD and MP3 players in every country, often with the same marketing approach. Unilever (a European food and household products company): its German division sells a different range of food products with different marketing than its North American division, to appeal to German customers.
Main advantage Significant cost savings because you do not customize the product and marketing for each country. By customizing to local conditions, managers may gain market share or charge higher prices.
Main disadvantage Because you ignore national differences, you are vulnerable to local competitors that differentiate their products to suit local tastes. Customization raises production costs and puts the company at a price disadvantage, because it often has to charge higher prices than competitors following a global strategy.
Who it suits Products sold with the same marketing by only changing the language: Rolex watches, Ralph Lauren or Tommy Hilfiger clothing, Chanel or Armani clothing, accessories, and perfume, Apple computers, Chinese-made plastic toys, and U.S.-grown rice and wheat. And organizations that serve business customers often choose it, because their needs (better quality and lower cost) are consistent from place to place. The book gives no list here; its examples all come from food (Unilever's German division, and the Kellogg's and Nestlé lesson in India) — markets where local taste matters.

The book says word for word: the advantages and disadvantages of multidomestic are the opposite of those of global, and the choice between them calls for trade-offs.

An example of the cost of ignoring local taste — India: global food makers Kellogg's and Nestlé entered the Indian processed food market (worth more than $300 billion a year) and did not know how to customize their products to Indian taste, so at first they had large losses. Kellogg's did not notice that most Indians eat a cooked breakfast because milk is usually not pasteurized; today, with pasteurized and canned milk more available, it offers cereals made from basmati rice flavored with mango. And Nestlé's Maggi noodles did not please Indians until they got a “marsala” (mixed curry spice) flavor, and today they are a staple in Indian diets.

EXPLANATION — THE MANAGING GLOBALLY BOX: WHEN GROWTH BECOMES A PROBLEM (SUMMARY)

The book says expanding abroad can be a huge success, but even a surge in demand can become a management problem:

  • In 2020, when the pandemic arrived in the United States, the fear was massive layoffs and a recession. But businesses turned out more agile and resilient than feared, the federal government helped, and the economy bounced back quickly and spending soared — but through different channels: more online buying, from entertainment to home goods, and from travel to electronics.
  • This sudden change caused major headaches for companies with global networks of suppliers and distributors: U.S. ports were backed up with ships from Asia, shortages of semiconductors paused car assembly, Whirlpool moved from building based on forecasts and orders to “build whatever you can with the parts on hand”, and Volvo Construction Equipment's Pennsylvania plant kept rescheduling production based on parts arriving from 17 countries, sometimes pausing for hours or a whole day.
  • The difficulties were especially big for makers of electric vehicles (also swept up by waves of technological and social change): demand is rising faster than battery materials, U.S. automakers are investing in battery factories but investment in materials is still early, and most battery-component production is in China.
  • The options are limited: semiconductors are near full capacity, and building new capacity (semiconductors, mining nickel, processing lithium) takes months or years and a major investment. So General Motors and others are forming partnerships or acquiring businesses abroad, and the rest buy whenever they can — at higher prices and higher storage costs.

And the book's photo on the same page: despite the headwinds, the economic outlook for Africa is positive, with growth expected above 3%, while economic, political, social, and environmental challenges and globalization speed up change in cities like Lagos, Nigeria.

EXAM BOX

To what extent should the organization customize features of its products and marketing campaign to different national conditions?

If managers decide that their organization should sell the same standardized product in each national market in which it competes, and use the same basic marketing approach, they adopt a global strategy. But if managers decide to customize products and marketing strategies to specific national conditions, they adopt a multidomestic strategy. Panasonic has traditionally pursued a global strategy, selling the same basic TVs, camcorders, and DVD and MP3 players in every country in which it does business and often using the same basic marketing approach. Unilever, the European food and household products company, has pursued a multidomestic strategy. Thus, to appeal to German customers, Unilever's German division sells a different range of food products and uses a different marketing approach than its North American division.

The major advantage of a global strategy is the significant cost savings associated with not having to customize products and marketing approaches to different national conditions. Organizations that serve business customers often adopt a global strategy because customer needs such as improving quality and lowering costs are consistent from place to place.

The major disadvantage of pursuing a global strategy is that by ignoring national differences, managers may leave themselves vulnerable to local competitors that differentiate their products to suit local tastes.

The major advantage of a multidomestic strategy is that by customizing product offerings and marketing approaches to local conditions, managers may be able to gain market share or charge higher prices for their products. The major disadvantage is that customization raises production costs and puts the multidomestic company at a price disadvantage because it often has to charge prices higher than the prices charged by competitors pursuing a global strategy.

TRAP
  • The name misleads: multidomestic = customized for each country (as if you had a “domestic market” in every country). Global = one size for all. Do not swap them.
  • The advantages and disadvantages are reversed: global's advantage is cost savings and its disadvantage is local competitors; multidomestic's advantage is market share or higher prices and its disadvantage is higher production costs + a price disadvantage.
  • Panasonic = global, Unilever = multidomestic. And Kellogg's and Nestlé in India = an example of the disadvantage of ignoring local taste (they did not customize, so they lost), not a success story.
  • Business customers → usually global, because their needs are consistent. A choice that says “business customers need customization for each country” = wrong.
  • Do not mix up global strategy (a way to compete) with globalization (forces that connect countries) or with global outsourcing (buying inputs from suppliers abroad — lesson 1).

EXAM BOX

In general, four basic ways to operate in the global environment are exporting and importing, licensing and franchising, strategic alliances, and wholly owned foreign subsidiaries. We briefly discuss each one, moving from the lowest level of foreign involvement and investment required of a global organization and its managers, and the least amount of risk, to the high end of the spectrum (see Figure 6.3).

The least complex global operations are exporting and importing. A company engaged in exporting makes products at home and sells them abroad. Few risks are associated with exporting because a company does not have to invest in developing manufacturing facilities abroad. A company engaged in importing sells products at home that are made abroad (products it makes itself or buys from other companies).

In licensing, a company (the licenser) allows a foreign organization (the licensee) to take charge of both manufacturing and distributing one or more of its products in the licensee's country or world region in return for a negotiated fee. The advantage of licensing is that the licenser does not have to bear the development costs associated with opening up in a foreign country; the licensee bears the costs. The risks associated with this strategy are that the company granting the license has to give its foreign partner access to its technological know-how and so risks losing control of its secrets.

Whereas licensing is pursued primarily by manufacturing companies, franchising is pursued primarily by service organizations. In franchising, a company (the franchisor) sells to a foreign organization (the franchisee) the rights to use its brand name and operating know-how in return for a lump-sum payment and share of the franchiser's profits. The advantage of franchising is that the franchiser does not have to bear the development costs of overseas expansion and avoids the many problems associated with setting up foreign operations. The downside is that the organization that grants the franchise may lose control over how the franchisee operates, and product quality may fall.

TRAP
  • The order from LOW to HIGH: exporting/importing → licensing/franchising → alliances/joint ventures → wholly owned. And do not read the figure from the right!
  • Exporting = make here, sell abroad; importing = sell here something made abroad. And do not mix them up with global outsourcing from lesson 1 (= buying or producing inputs or final products from suppliers abroad to lower costs and improve quality or design): that one is about suppliers in the task environment, not a way to expand on the ladder of Figure 6.3.
  • Licensing = manufacturing + a negotiated fee + the risk to technical secrets. Franchising = services + a lump sum and a share of profits + the risk to quality and reputation. If the scenario has “brand name” and “hotels/restaurants” → franchising.
  • The one ending in -or/-er (licenser, franchisor) = the one who gives; the one ending in -ee (licensee, franchisee) = the foreign one who takes.
  • In both, the foreign partner bears the development costs — a choice that says “the licenser bears the costs of building the factory” = wrong.

Strategic Alliances, Joint Ventures, and Wholly Owned Foreign Subsidiaries
Share with a foreign partner… or carry it all alone: all the profits and all the risks
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Strategic alliance = you pool your resources and know-how with a foreign company and you share the rewards and risks. Joint venture = a kind of alliance where you set up a new business with shared ownership. Wholly owned foreign subsidiary = you build production operations abroad alone, with no local partner: all the profits are yours and all the risks are yours.
🧠
Opening a new restaurant 50-50 with your cousin = joint venture. Opening a branch in Dubai that is fully yours, with no partner = wholly owned.

TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Strategic alliance
التحالف الاستراتيجي		 An agreement in which managers pool or share their organization's resources and know-how with a foreign company, and the two organizations share the rewards and risks of starting a new venture. An agreement where managers combine or share their organization's resources and know-how with a foreign company, and the two organizations share the rewards and risks of starting a new venture.
Joint venture
المشروع المشترك		 A strategic alliance among two or more companies that agree to jointly establish and share the ownership of a new business. A strategic alliance between two or more companies that agree to set up a new business jointly and share its ownership.
  • Why an alliance? It is a way to overcome the loss-of-control problems of exporting, licensing, and franchising.
  • Sharing resources lets the company (for example, a U.S. one): (1) use the high-quality skills of foreign manufacturers, (2) use the specialized knowledge of foreign managers about local customers' needs, (3) reduce the risks of the venture.
  • At the same time, the terms of the alliance give it more control over how the product is produced or sold abroad than it would have as a franchiser or licenser.
  • Two forms of alliance: (1) a written contract between two or more companies to exchange resources, or (2) the creation of a new organization = this is the joint venture.
  • In a joint venture: involvement abroad increases because the alliance usually includes a capital investment in production facilities abroad, but risk is reduced (Risk, however, is reduced). And the Internet and global teleconferencing provide the communication and coordination the partners need.

The General Mills example (memorize the numbers): it has two international joint ventures: (1) Cereal Partners Worldwide (CPW) = a 50–50 joint venture with Nestlé that markets breakfast cereals in more than 130 countries, combining the expertise of General Mills (the second-largest cereal maker in North America) with Nestlé's worldwide presence and distribution strength. (2) Häagen-Dazs Japan. In fiscal year 2022, the two joint ventures brought General Mills more than $2.1 billion in sales.

EXPLANATION — STEP 4: THE WHOLLY OWNED FOREIGN SUBSIDIARY
TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Wholly owned foreign subsidiary
الشركة التابعة المملوكة بالكامل		 Production operations established in a foreign country independent of any local direct involvement. Production operations set up in a foreign country with no local direct involvement at all.
  • The book's example: Japanese car-component companies set up their own operations in the United States to supply Japanese carmakers there (Toyota and Honda) with high-quality parts.
  • When you work alone without any foreign company, you get all the rewards and carry all the risks.
  • The disadvantage: much more expensive than the other ways, because it needs a higher foreign investment and gives the manager many more threats.
  • The advantages (three): (1) high potential returns because you do not share your profits with a foreign organization, (2) it reduces the level of risk because your managers have full control over every part of the subsidiary, (3) it protects your technology and know-how from foreign organizations.
  • Who uses it? Large, well-known companies with plenty of resources — DuPont, GM, and P&G — use it extensively.

Watch out for an apparent contradiction: the book says this way has “many more threats” and at the same time it “reduces the level of risk”. The difference (our explanation): the threats are bigger because you have put a huge investment alone into a foreign environment, but the risk of losing control and leaking technology is lower because control is full. And in an order question, rely on Figure 6.3: it is the highest step in involvement, investment, and risk.

EXAM BOX

One way to overcome the loss-of-control problems associated with exporting, licensing, and franchising is to expand globally by means of a strategic alliance. In a strategic alliance, managers pool or share their organization's resources and know-how with those of a foreign company, and the two organizations share the rewards or risks of starting a new venture in a foreign country.

A joint venture is a strategic alliance between two or more companies that agree to jointly establish and share the ownership of a new business. An organization's level of involvement abroad increases in a joint venture because the alliance normally involves a capital investment in production facilities abroad in order to produce goods or services outside the home country. Risk, however, is reduced.

When managers decide to establish a wholly owned foreign subsidiary, they invest in establishing production operations in a foreign country independent of any local direct involvement.

Operating alone, without any direct involvement from foreign companies, an organization receives all of the rewards and bears all of the risks associated with operating abroad. This method of international expansion is much more expensive than the others because it requires a higher level of foreign investment and presents managers with many more threats. However, investment in a foreign subsidiary or division offers significant advantages: It gives an organization high potential returns because the organization does not have to share its profits with a foreign organization, and it reduces the level of risk because the organization's managers have full control over all aspects of their foreign subsidiary's operations. Moreover, this type of investment allows managers to protect their technology and know-how from foreign organizations. Large, well-known companies like DuPont, GM, and P&G, which have plenty of resources, make extensive use of wholly owned subsidiaries.

TRAP
  • Every joint venture = a strategic alliance, but not every alliance = a joint venture: an alliance can be only a written contract; a JV must have a new business with shared ownership.
  • An alliance gives more control than licensing and franchising (it is the solution to the loss-of-control problem) — not less.
  • In a JV: involvement goes up and risk goes down — both together, as the book says.
  • Wholly owned = no local partner at all (independent of any local direct involvement). If the scenario has “a partner” or “shared ownership” → not wholly owned. (The class booklet's definition says a foreign business that is wholly owned by the organization that establishes or acquires it — the same idea; memorize the book's wording.)
  • Wholly owned: all the rewards and all the risks, the most expensive, and three advantages: high returns (no profit sharing) · full control that reduces risk · protects technology and know-how.
  • CPW = joint venture (General Mills + Nestlé, 50–50, 130+ countries). The Japanese parts companies in the U.S. = wholly owned. And DuPont appears twice: a licensing example (India) and a user of wholly owned subsidiaries.

Declining Barriers to Trade and Investment
A tax on imports protects a little in the short run and hurts a lot in the long run
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Tariff = a tax a government puts on imported goods (and sometimes exported ones). Countries learned from the Great Depression that barriers hurt, so they adopted the free-trade doctrine: each country specializes in what it produces most efficiently. They applied it through GATT, which was dissolved and replaced by the WTO.
🧠
You put a tax on your neighbor's cars? He puts a tax on your dates. In the end you both lose — just like the 1930s.
EXPLANATION — THE TARIFF: WHAT IT IS AND WHY IT IS USED

One of the main factors that sped up globalization by freeing the movement of capital = the decline in barriers to trade and investment. In the 1920s and 1930s many countries built formidable barriers to international trade and investment, believing this was the best way to protect their economic well-being, and most of them were high tariffs on imports of manufactured goods.

Tariff

EXPLANATION — WHY REMOVE TARIFFS? (THE RETALIATION CHAIN)
  1. When one country imposes an import tariff, other countries do the same → a series of retaliatory moves, and countries raise their barriers against each other step by step.
  2. In the 1920s this behavior lowered world demand and helped bring the Great Depression of the 1930s and massive unemployment.
  3. Since the 2009 economic crisis, most governments worked hard in the 2010s not to fall into the trap of raising tariffs to protect jobs in the short run, because they know the long-run result = losing even more jobs.
  4. The rule: governments that raise tariff barriers reduce employment and undermine economic growth in the end, because capital and resources will always move to their most highly valued use — wherever it is in the world.
EXPLANATION — GATT AND THE RISE OF FREE TRADE

After World War II, advanced Western industrial countries — having learned from the Great Depression — committed themselves to removing barriers to the free flow of resources and capital between countries. This commitment was reinforced by accepting the principle that free trade — not tariff barriers — is the best way to foster a healthy domestic economy and low unemployment.

EXAM BOX

A tariff is a tax that a government imposes on goods imported into one country from another. The aim of import tariffs is to protect domestic industries and jobs, such as those in the auto or steel industry, from overseas competition by raising the price of these products from abroad.

The reason for removing tariffs is that, very often, when one country imposes an import tariff, others follow suit, and the result is a series of retaliatory moves as countries progressively raise tariff barriers against each other. Governments of countries that resort to raising tariff barriers ultimately reduce employment and undermine the economic growth of their countries because capital and resources will always move to their most highly valued use—wherever that is in the world.

The free-trade doctrine predicts that if each country agrees to specialize in the production of the goods and services that it can produce most efficiently, this will make the best use of global capital resources and will result in lower prices. For example, if Indian companies are highly efficient in the production of textiles and U.S. companies are highly efficient in the production of computer software, then, under a free-trade agreement, capital would move to India and be invested there to produce textiles, while capital from around the world would flow to the United States and be invested in its innovative software companies.

Historically, countries that accepted this free-trade doctrine set as their goal the removal of barriers to the free flow of goods, services, and capital between countries. They tried to achieve this through an international treaty known as the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT). For example, the Uruguay Round involved 117 countries and succeeded in lowering tariffs by more than 30% from the previous level. It also led to the dissolution of GATT and its replacement by the World Trade Organization (WTO), which continues the struggle to reduce tariffs and has more power to sanction countries that break global agreements. The latest round of negotiations, called the Doha Round, effectively ended in 2015, when trade ministers failed to agree to keep the negotiations and discussions alive.

TRAP
  • The book's glossary definition: imported or, occasionally, exported goods — a tariff is not only on imports. A choice that says “a tax on exports only” = wrong, and one that says “on imports and sometimes exports” = right.
  • Tariff = a barrier (a tax). Free-trade doctrine = the idea of specializing and removing barriers. If the scenario has “each country specializes in what it produces efficiently” → free-trade doctrine; if it has “a tax that raises the price of imports” → tariff.
  • The aim of tariffs = to protect domestic industries and jobs in the short run; their long-run result = less employment and weaker growth.
  • GATT was dissolved and replaced by the WTO — they do not work side by side. And the WTO has more power to sanction. Uruguay succeeded (117 countries, 30%+), and Doha failed (2015) — do not swap them.
  • Trump: 25% steel and 10% aluminum (do not swap them), May 2018. And the free-trade doctrine itself is a reason for more global outsourcing — the book admits its downside.

Distance, Culture, and Regional Trade Agreements
The world got closer and competition got harder; regional agreements bring opportunity and threat together
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Distance and culture used to close the world and keep managers focused on their domestic market (the Unilever story), and the communications and transportation revolution broke them. The result: bigger opportunities to sell and buy, but tougher competition. And regional agreements (NAFTA → USMCA, CAFTA, the African Union, SADC, the Gulf Cooperation Council) bring opportunities and threats together.
🧠
In Lever's day, India was six weeks away by ship; today New York is closer to Tokyo than it once was to Philadelphia.
EXPLANATION — THE UNILEVER STORY: THE TWO BARRIERS

Historically, barriers of distance and culture closed the global environment and kept managers focused on their domestic market. Unilever (a global consumer goods company now with more than 400 brands) shows this:

  • It was founded in London in the 1880s by William Lever (a Quaker), and by the early 1900s it had subsidiaries in most countries of the British Empire: India, Canada, and Australia.
  • Barrier one — distance: Lever had a hands-on, autocratic style (he did things himself and gave orders like a dictator), and he found his far-flung empire hard to control. The reason: communication over great distances was hard — six weeks to reach India by ship from England, and international telephone and telegraph services were unreliable.
  • Barrier two — culture: the difficulty of doing business in societies separated from Britain by barriers of language and culture. Each country has different national beliefs, values, and norms, and a management approach that worked in Britain did not necessarily work in India or Persia (now Iran) → management practices had to be tailored to each national culture.
  • After Lever's death in 1925, top management decentralized decision-making authority to the managers of the national subsidiaries so each could develop an approach that suited its country. The negative result: the subsidiaries became distant and remote from one another, and this reduced Unilever's performance.
EXPLANATION — HOW DID THE BARRIERS FALL AFTER WORLD WAR II?
  • Since the end of World War II, continuing advances in communications and transportation technology have reduced the barriers of distance and culture.
  • The global communications revolution came through four developments: satellites, digital technology, the Internet and global computer networks, and video teleconferencing → sending huge amounts of information and reliable, secure, and instantaneous communication between any people and companies in the world. So any global organization — a tiny garment factory in Li & Fung's network or a huge company like IKEA and Unilever — can do business anywhere, anytime and search for customers and suppliers around the world.
  • The transportation revolution: the most important innovation = the growth of commercial jet travel. New York is now closer to Tokyo in travel time than it was to Philadelphia in the days of the 13 colonies — so controlling far-flung international businesses is much easier than in Lever's era.
  • Reducing cultural distance: the Internet and its millions of websites help build global networks and media that create a worldwide culture, which in some cases has diluted unique national cultures. And networks such as CNN, MTV, ESPN, BBC, and HBO can be watched in many countries, and Hollywood films are streamed via the Internet across the globe.
EXPLANATION — THE EFFECTS OF FREE TRADE ON MANAGERS, AND REGIONAL AGREEMENTS
  • Lower barriers to trade and investment + declining barriers of distance and culture = enormous opportunities for companies to grow their market through exports and investments abroad. The more open economy gave the chance to sell abroad and the chance to buy more from other countries.
  • But the manager's job became more challenging because of the increased intensity of competition that goes hand in hand with lower barriers.

The opportunitymanufacturing organizations reduce their costs in two ways: (1) shift production to the lowest-cost location inside the area (U.S. auto and textile companies moved production to Mexico), or (2) serve the whole region from one location instead of separate operations in each country.

1. A U.S. hotel company sells a local company in Chile the rights to use its brand name and operating know-how to run hotels, in return for a lump-sum payment and a share of the profits. This way of expanding internationally is best described as:
  1. licensing
  2. franchising
  3. a joint venture
  4. exporting
Answer

B. This is franchising exactly: brand name + operating know-how in return for a lump sum + a share of profits, and it is the book's Hilton-in-Chile example; hotels are services. The trap in A: licensing belongs to manufacturing companies (the partner makes and distributes the product) and its payment is a negotiated fee — like DuPont in India. C: a joint venture has a new business with shared ownership, and the question has no shared ownership. And D: exporting = make in your country and sell abroad; no rights are sold.

2. According to Figure 6.3, which sequence correctly orders the four ways to expand internationally from the LOWEST to the HIGHEST level of foreign involvement and investment and degree of risk?
  1. Wholly owned foreign subsidiary → strategic alliances, joint ventures → licensing and franchising → importing and exporting
  2. Licensing and franchising → importing and exporting → strategic alliances, joint ventures → wholly owned foreign subsidiary
  3. Importing and exporting → licensing and franchising → strategic alliances, joint ventures → wholly owned foreign subsidiary
  4. Importing and exporting → strategic alliances, joint ventures → licensing and franchising → wholly owned foreign subsidiary
Answer

C. In Figure 6.3 the boxes go from the left (LOW) to the right (HIGH): Importing and exporting → Licensing and franchising → Strategic alliances, joint ventures → Wholly owned foreign subsidiary. The trap in A: the order is reversed — this is what happens if you read the figure from the right, as in Arabic. B puts licensing before exporting, but exporting is the least complex way, with the least risk. And D puts alliances before licensing, but the alliance came to solve the loss-of-control problem of licensing and franchising, and it needs more investment.

3. Which of the following is NOT one of the four principal forms of capital that flow between countries?
  1. Human capital
  2. Resource capital
  3. Political capital
  4. Social capital
Answer

D. The four in the book: human (people: immigration and migration), financial (money: investment, credit, lending, and aid), resource (natural resources, parts, and components), political (power and influence that protect access to the rest). Social capital is not one of them — the trap is that it looks like the words social systems in the definition of globalization. A, B, and C are all on the list, and the question uses NOT — so do not pick the first name you recognize.

4. A food company customizes the products it sells and the marketing it uses in every country to suit local tastes. Compared with a competitor that sells one standardized product worldwide, what is the MAJOR disadvantage this company is likely to face?
  1. Customization raises its production costs and puts it at a price disadvantage.
  2. It is vulnerable to local competitors that differentiate their products to suit local tastes.
  3. It risks losing control of its technological secrets to a licensee.
  4. It must share its profits with a foreign joint venture partner.
Answer

A. The company follows a multidomestic strategy (customizing for each country), and its main disadvantage according to the book is: customization raises production costs and puts it at a price disadvantage against a competitor that follows a global strategy. The trap in B: this is exactly the disadvantage of a global strategy (the opposite) — like Kellogg's and Nestlé in India. C is the risk of licensing, and D belongs to the joint venture — both are ways to enter a market, not a customization strategy.

5. Country A's companies are highly efficient at producing textiles, and Country B's companies are highly efficient at producing computer software. Under a free-trade agreement, capital flows to A to make textiles and to B to make software, and the prices of both products fall. This prediction comes from:
  1. a tariff
  2. the free-trade doctrine
  3. a multidomestic strategy
  4. the Doha Round
Answer

B. This is the book's own example (India for textiles and the U.S. for software): the free-trade doctrine = each country specializes in what it produces most efficiently → the best use of resources and lower prices. The trap in A: a tariff is a tax that raises the price of imports — the exact opposite idea. C is a company strategy (customizing products for each country), not a theory of trade between countries. And D: the Doha Round is a negotiation round that failed in 2015, not an idea.
