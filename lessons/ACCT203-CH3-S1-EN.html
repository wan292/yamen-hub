Chapter 3 — Timing and Reporting

ACCT 203 · Principles of Financial Accounting · Chapter 3: Adjusting Accounts for Financial Statements · Section 1 of 4: Timing and Reporting
Objective C1: Explain the importance of periodic reporting and the role of accrual accounting.

This section answers two questions: “why do we cut a company's life into periods?” and “why does accounting record a thing when it happens, not when the cash moves?”

In Chapter 2 you recorded FastForward's external transactions in the journal, posted them, and prepared the trial balance. But at the end of the month some accounts have balances that are not ready for the financial statements: insurance that has used up one month, supplies that were partly used, salaries that are owed but not paid… things that happened inside the company that nobody recorded. All of Chapter 3 is about adjusting accounts before the statements come out, and this section (C1) is the base that explains why we make adjustments at all.

The Accounting Period
We split a company's life into months, quarters, and years so the information arrives on time
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Information is valuable because of its timing. So we assume that a company's activity can be divided into periods (the time period assumption) and prepare a report for each period: annual and interim. And the fiscal year = any 12 months in a row or 52 weeks — it does not have to end on December 31.
🧠
Like university grades: you don't wait for graduation; each academic year ends with a result (annual), with term results and quizzes between (interim).
EXPLANATION — WHY DO WE SPLIT TIME INTO PERIODS?

The book starts with one sentence: the value of information is linked to its timeliness. Useful information must reach decision makers frequently, so the accounting system prepares reports at regular intervals.

Time period assumption = we assume that the organization's activities can be divided into specific time periods — in the book's words, it presumes this. The book gives you four examples:

  1. a month
  2. a three-month quarter (3 months)
  3. a six-month interval (6 months)
  4. a year

EXPLANATION — THE FISCAL YEAR DOES NOT HAVE TO BE JANUARY TO DECEMBER

The annual reporting period is not always a calendar year ending on December 31. An organization can use a fiscal year = any 12 consecutive months or 52 weeks. The book's example: Gap's fiscal year consistently ends in the last week of January or the first week of February every year.

So how does a company choose when its year ends? The book links it to the seasonal variation in sales:

The companyWhat it choosesThe book's example
its sales do not change much with the seasons (little seasonal variation) usually the calendar year as its fiscal year — it ends on December 31 Netflix
its sales are seasonal (seasonal variations) usually a natural business year — it ends when sales are at their lowest level for the year Target and Nordstrom (retailers): around January 31, after the holidays

Why the lowest level? Think about it: after the holiday season the store is quiet, the stock is low, and the employees are free to close the books and count the stock — the easiest time to close the year.

EXAM BOX

The value of information is linked to its timeliness. Useful information must reach decision makers frequently. To provide timely information, accounting systems prepare reports at regular intervals. The time period assumption presumes that an organization's activities can be divided into specific time periods such as a month, a three-month quarter, a six-month interval, or a year. Most organizations use a year as their primary accounting period. Reports covering a one-year period are known as annual financial statements. Many organizations also prepare interim financial statements covering one, three, or six months of activity.

Companies with little seasonal variation in sales often use the calendar year as their fiscal year. TRAP
  • The time period assumption is an assumption that activity is divided into periods — not a recognition principle, and not a rule about when cash comes in.
  • Interim = 1, 3, or 6 months — less than a year. A choice that says “interim = one year” is wrong; a year is called annual.
  • Fiscal year = any 12 consecutive months or 52 weeks. A choice that says “it must start on January 1” or “it must end on December 31” is wrong (remember Gap).
  • The natural business year ends when sales are at their lowest level — not the highest. And it is the seasonal companies that use it; companies with little seasonal variation use the calendar year (Netflix).
  • Do not mix up the examples: Gap = a fiscal year ending late January / early February · Netflix = calendar year · Target and Nordstrom = natural business year around January 31.

Accrual Basis versus Cash Basis
When do you record revenue and expense: when the thing happens, or when the cash moves?
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The accrual basis records revenue when the service or product is delivered to the customer, and an expense when it is incurred (and matched with the revenue). The cash basis only sees cash: revenue when cash comes in, and an expense when cash goes out.
🧠
Your salary arrives on the 27th, but you worked the whole month. Cash basis records the day it arrived; accrual records the days you worked.
FROM THE CHAPTER OPENER — Leading the Way: Sword & Plough

Emily Núñez Cavness co-founded Sword & Plough while she was deployed with the army in Afghanistan. A Skype meeting of hers was even interrupted by mortar fire. She and her sister Betsy recycle military surplus materials and make them into totes, handbags, backpacks, and accessories. They have already recycled 30,000 pounds of materials.

The accounting point: the sisters say part of their success is due to the accounting system — moving from a cash system to an accrual system gave them more timely information. And accounting adjustments raised the value of their revenue and expense data, and they used them to set prices better and manage costs. So the whole idea of this section is in one story: accrual + adjustments = more accurate and faster information.

EXPLANATION — WHY DO ACCOUNTS NEED ADJUSTING?

After you record the external transactions and events, many accounts need adjustments before their balances appear in the financial statements. Why? Because the internal transactions and events are not yet recorded. Remember from Chapter 1: an internal transaction = an exchange inside the entity itself, like using up supplies.

The big question: when do we record revenue and expense? There are two systems:

TRAP
  • Do not reverse them: accrual = when the thing happens (delivery / using up), cash = when the cash moves. A choice that says “accrual records revenue when cash is received” is the definition of the cash basis.
  • On the cash basis any cash paid counts, even if you prepaid it for next year's costs (like Roma's 6,750). On the accrual basis this amount is not an expense this year.
  • The book gives two advantages of accrual: it reflects performance better + it increases comparability between periods. A choice that gives one of them to the cash basis is wrong.
  • Why do we need adjustments? Because the internal transactions are not yet recorded — not because the external transactions have errors.

Recognizing Revenues and Expenses
The two principles that every adjustment follows
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
We divide a company's activity into periods, but not all activities are complete when we prepare the statements → we need adjustments to get proper balances. And the adjustments follow two principles: revenue recognition (revenue in the period the service is provided) and expense recognition, or matching (expense in the same period as the revenue it helped bring).
🧠
Revenue counts the day you hand over the work, not the day the customer pays. The expense follows the revenue it helped bring.
EXPLANATION — THE TWO PRINCIPLES
The principleThe English definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Revenue recognition principle
مبدأ الاعتراف بالإيراد		 Requires that revenue be recorded when goods or services are provided to customers and at an amount expected to be received from customers. revenue is recorded when goods or services are provided to customers, and at the amount expected to be received from them — so two conditions: when and how much.
Expense recognition (or matching) principle
مبدأ الاعتراف بالمصروف (المقابلة)		 Requires that expenses be recorded in the same accounting period as the revenues that are recognized as a result of those expenses. the expense is recorded in the same accounting period as the revenue that was recognized because of that expense.

The role of adjustments here: they ensure revenue is recognized (reported) in the time period when the services and products are provided. The same goes for expenses: FastForward's insurance protects the company while it works and earns revenue every month, so its expense ($100) is put in each month — this is the matching that the cash basis does not do.

We use two principles in the adjusting process: revenue recognition and expense recognition.

Revenue recognition principle requires that revenue be recorded when goods or services are provided to customers and at an amount expected to be received from customers. Adjustments ensure revenue is recognized (reported) in the time period when those services and products are provided.

TRAP

  • The principle has two conditions: when (when provided) + how much (the amount expected to be received from the customer).
  • Expense recognition has a second name: the matching principle — the same principle. The expense follows the revenue, not the cash.
  • The book says there are two principles in the adjusting process: revenue recognition and expense recognition. The time period assumption is an assumption, not one of them.
  • Cash received before the service (the Thunder ticket) = not revenue yet; a service provided before the cash (Tesla, Deloitte) = revenue now.

Framework for Adjustments
Every adjustment in the chapter follows the same method
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
There are four types of adjustments for transactions and events that extend over more than one period, and all of them follow 3 steps: what is the balance now? → what should it be? → record an adjusting entry that takes you from the first to the second. And the adjusting entry touches an income statement account and a balance sheet account — and never touches Cash.
🧠
At month-end you take each account and ask: what is its balance now? What should it be? The difference between them = the adjusting entry.

EXPLANATION — THE THREE STEPS (EXHIBIT 3.4) AND THE ADJUSTING ENTRY
Exhibit 3.4 — Three-Step Process for Adjusting Entries
Step 1:Determine what the current account balance equals.
Step 2:Determine what the current account balance should equal.
Step 3:Record an adjusting entry to get from step 1 to step 2.

In simple words: 1) determine what the account balance is now; 2) what should its balance be? 3) record an adjusting entry that takes you from the first to the second. You use this method in every adjustment in Sections 2 and 3.

An adjusting entry = an entry made at the end of the accounting period that reflects a transaction or event that is not yet recorded. Its golden rule: it affects one or more income statement accounts (revenue or expense) and one or more balance sheet accounts (asset or liability) — and never the Cash account.

EXAM BOX

Four types of adjustments exist for transactions and events that extend over more than one period.

Adjustments are made using a 3-step process, as shown in Exhibit 3.4.
Step 1: Determine what the current account balance equals.
Step 2: Determine what the current account balance should equal.
Step 3: Record an adjusting entry to get from step 1 to step 2.

Each adjusting entry made at the end of an accounting period reflects a transaction or event that is not yet recorded. An adjusting entry affects one or more income statement accounts and one or more balance sheet accounts (but never the Cash account).

TRAP
  • There are four types: two deferrals (deferral of expense, deferral of revenue) + two accruals (accrued expense, accrued revenue). Any choice with a type for cash or for equipment is not one of them.
  • An adjusting entry never has Cash. Any choice with a debit or credit to Cash = not an adjusting entry.
  • Every adjusting entry = an income statement account + a balance sheet account. A choice with “two balance sheet accounts” or “two income statement accounts” is wrong.
  • The order of the steps: what the balance is now → what it should be → record the entry. Do not start with the entry.
  • Adjusting entries are made at the end of the accounting period, and they record something not yet recorded — they are not corrections of errors.
  • Do not flip deferral and accrual: deferral = cash before recognition (prepaid / unearned), accrual = cash after recognition (incurred but not paid / earned but not received).

C puts the 2,400 in the wrong year. And D reverses the two bases.

3. In its first year, a company earned revenues of $60,000 ($52,000 received in cash). It incurred expenses of $35,000 ($30,000 paid in cash). It also prepaid $4,000 cash for costs that will not be expensed until next year. What is its first-year net income under the cash basis and under the accrual basis?
  1. Cash basis $18,000; accrual basis $25,000
  2. Cash basis $22,000; accrual basis $25,000
  3. Cash basis $25,000; accrual basis $18,000
  4. Cash basis $18,000; accrual basis $21,000
Answer

A. Cash basis = receipts minus all payments: 52,000 − (30,000 + 4,000) = 18,000. Accrual basis = revenue earned minus expense incurred: 60,000 − 35,000 = 25,000. The trap in B: it forgot the 4,000 prepaid on the cash basis (52,000 − 30,000 = 22,000). C reverses the two bases. And D subtracted the 4,000 on the accrual basis — but it is a prepaid asset, not this year's expense. It is exactly the same idea as Roma in the second concept.

4. On December 20, a company completes and delivers $8,000 of services to a customer, who will pay in February. Under accrual basis accounting, how much revenue does the company recognize in December?
  1. $0, because revenue is recorded in February when the cash is received
  2. $8,000, because revenue is recorded when the services are provided
  3. $4,000, because the revenue is split between December and February
  4. $0, because the $8,000 is recorded as unearned revenue in December
Answer

B. The revenue recognition principle: revenue is recorded when the service is provided and at the amount expected to be received — like the Tesla car in Exercise 3-2. The trap in A: this is cash basis thinking. D is a trap: unearned revenue is when cash is received before the service — here it is the opposite, the service comes before the cash (the customer now owes the company: on December 20 it is recorded as Dr. Accounts Receivable, Cr. Revenue; it would be accrued revenue only if it were still unrecorded at the end of the period, Section 3). And C has no basis in the principle at all — the whole service was provided in December.

5. Which statement about adjusting entries is correct?
  1. They are recorded whenever cash is received or paid during the period
  2. Each one affects one or more income statement accounts and one or more balance sheet accounts, but never the Cash account
  3. Each one affects two balance sheet accounts, such as Cash and Prepaid Insurance
  4. They are needed because external transactions were recorded with errors
Answer

B. This is the book's sentence word for word: an income statement account + a balance sheet account, and never Cash — like the insurance entry: Insurance Expense and Prepaid Insurance. The trap in A: adjusting entries are made at the end of the period and have nothing to do with cash movement. C has Cash and two balance sheet accounts — two mistakes. And D is a trap: we need them because the internal transactions are not yet recorded, not because there are errors.
