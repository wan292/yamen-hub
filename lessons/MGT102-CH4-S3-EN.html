Chapter 4 — Sources of Managerial Ethics

MGT 102 · Chapter 4 · Section 3 of 4: Sources of Managerial Ethics
Objective LO4-5: Identify the four main sources of managerial ethics.

A manager's ethics do not fall from the sky — they come from four springs

In the two previous lessons you learned what ethics are, who the stakeholders are, and the four rules you use to judge a decision. Now the book opens this section with a question: why is a company like Microsoft known for its ethics, while a company like Wells Fargo got into trouble again and again? The book's answer: four sources shape the ethics of any manager and any company — society, the occupation, the individual, and the organization.

Focus from now on two things: (1) the number is four — this chapter is full of fours, and the only exception is the six stakeholder groups. (2) occupational against organizational: the two names look similar in English, but the first is about your profession and the second is about your company, and the exam plays on this. The lesson also has two figures and a table — Figure 4.4, Table 4.2, and Figure 4.5 — and all of them come up in questions.

The Four Sources of Managerial Ethics
Four streams that flow into one result called business ethics
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The differences in ethics between people, employees, companies, and countries are decided by four sources: societal · occupational · individual · organizational — and all of them flow into business ethics.
🧠
Four rivers flow into one lake called business ethics: from your country, your profession, your home, and your company.

There are four main determinants of differences in ethics among people, employees, companies, and countries: societal ethics, occupational ethics, individual ethics, and organizational ethics—especially the ethics of a company's top managers (see Figure 4.4).

TRAP
  • The count is 4 sources. Do not mix it with the 6 stakeholder groups (lesson 1), the 4 decision rules (lesson 2), or the 4 approaches to social responsibility (lesson 4). The question “how many sources of managerial ethics?” has the answer 4, not 6.
  • What sits in the center of Figure 4.4 is business ethics — not one of the sources. If a choice says organizational ethics is in the middle, it is wrong; that is the box at the bottom.
  • The phrase especially the ethics of a company's top managers is attached to organizational ethics, not to individual ethics.
  • Fake sources they put in a “which is NOT” question: legal ethics, stakeholder ethics, utilitarian ethics, economic ethics — none of them is one of the four.

Societal Ethics
Your country's standards that you absorbed since childhood — and they differ from country to country
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Societal ethics = the standards that govern how members of a society deal with one another in matters of fairness, justice, poverty, and the rights of the individual.
🧠
The bakery line: no written law stops you from cutting in front of others, but the whole neighborhood knows the rule and follows it.
EXPLANATION

The definition has four issues: fairness, justice, poverty, and the rights of the individual. Memorize them, because the wrong choices swap one of them for something else.

Where do they come from? The book's verb is emanate from — and they come from two places:

  • A society's laws, customs, and practices.
  • The unwritten values and norms that influence how people interact with each other.

How do they work? People in a certain country may behave ethically automatically, because they have internalized them — that is, they made certain values, beliefs, and norms a part of their morals — and these values tell them how to behave when they face an ethical dilemma.

They differ from society to society (Societal ethics vary among societies) — the book's examples:

GroupWhat the book says
Denmark, Iceland, Sweden, Switzerland Known as some of the most ethical countries in the world, with strong values about social order and about creating a society that protects the welfare of all its citizens.
Many economically struggling countries Bribery is standard practice to get things done — like getting lighting installed or a contract awarded.
The U.S. and other economically advanced countries Bribery is considered unethical and has been made illegal.

But even “ethical” countries have corruption — the Samherji example:

  • In Iceland — which is praised as one of the most ethical countries — Samherji, one of the country's largest fishing conglomerates, ran a massive bribery scheme that reached Africa.
  • It allegedly bribed government officials in Namibia and Angola for the rights to massive fishing quotas in their waters.
  • It set up shell companies in tax havens — UAE, Mauritius, and Cyprus — to launder the money from the corrupt deals.

The lesson: calling a country “ethical” describes its society, but it does not guarantee that every company in it is clean.

EXAM BOX

Societal ethics are standards that govern how members of a society should deal with one another in matters involving issues such as fairness, justice, poverty, and the rights of the individual. Societal ethics emanate from a society's laws, customs, and practices and from the unwritten values and norms that influence how people interact with each other.

Societal ethics vary among societies. In the United States and other economically advanced countries, bribery is considered unethical and has been made illegal.

TRAP
  • The four issues in the definition: fairness · justice · poverty · rights of the individual. If you get a definition with profit or work-related activities, it is not societal (the second one is the definition of occupational).
  • The source is not only the laws — it is also the unwritten values and norms. A choice that says societal ethics come only from a society's laws is wrong.
  • The example of bribery between countries = societal ethics (they vary among societies). Do not put it under individual or organizational.
  • The four ethical countries: Denmark · Iceland · Sweden · Switzerland. And the corruption example, Samherji, came from Iceland itself — not from a poor country.

Occupational Ethics
The rules of your specialty — the doctor, the accountant, and the engineer each have their own
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Occupational ethics = the standards that govern how members of a profession, trade, or craft behave while they do their work.
🧠
A doctor who refuses to order an X-ray you do not need: that is his profession's ethics, not his country's or his hospital's.
EXPLANATION

The definition has three words you must know: a profession, trade, or craft + when performing work-related activities. The book's examples:

  • Medical ethics govern how doctors and nurses should treat their patients. A doctor is expected to perform only necessary medical procedures and to act in the patient's interest, not his own.
  • The ethics of scientific research: scientists must run their experiments and present their findings in ways that ensure the validity of their conclusions.
  • Punishments: like society, most professional groups can impose punishments for violations of the standards — doctors and lawyers can be prevented from practicing their professions if they disregard professional ethics and put their own interests first.

Inside the organization: occupational rules and norms often govern how employees like lawyers, researchers, and accountants make decisions to serve stakeholder interests. The employee internalizes the rules of his occupational group — exactly as he internalizes the rules of society — and follows them automatically. And because most people follow established rules of behavior, people often take ethics for granted — until they are broken.

EXPLANATION — when they break: the Volkswagen scandal

The book's example of breaking occupational ethics: when engineers change software to disguise the harmful effects of products. That is what happened in the diesel emission scandal — the company was caught rigging some of its diesel engines to pass U.S. emissions tests. Memorize the numbers:

EXAM BOX

Occupational ethics are standards that govern how members of a profession, trade, or craft should conduct themselves when performing work-related activities. For example, medical ethics govern how doctors and nurses should treat their patients.

Like society at large, most professional groups can impose punishments for violations of ethical standards. Doctors and lawyers can be prevented from practicing their professions if they disregard professional ethics and put their own interests first.

Employees internalize the rules and norms of their occupational group (just as they do those of society) and often follow them automatically when deciding how to behave. Table 4.2 lists some failures or lapses in professional ethics according to the type of functional manager.

TRAP
  • Occupational ≠ organizational. Occupational = your profession (doctor, lawyer, accountant, engineer); organizational = your company (the company's code). The VW engineers who rigged the software = an occupational example.
  • The words professional ethics in the title of Table 4.2 = occupational ethics. Do not count it as a fifth source.
  • The most dangerous trap in the table — health and safety comes twice: “compromising workplace health and safety to reduce costs” = manufacturing, and “failing to respond to employee concerns about health and safety violations” = human resource.
  • Excessive expenses and perks = accounting and finance, not human resource. And encroaching on privacy and drug testing = human resource, not marketing.
  • The VW numbers: 2017 · $2.8B fine · $35B+ costs · ~500,000 cars · 8+ employees. The $2.8 billion is the criminal penalty, and the $35 billion is the total costs — do not swap them.

Individual Ethics
Your own standards — from your home, your friends, your school, and your religion
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Individual ethics = your personal standards and values that decide how you see your responsibility toward others, and how you act when your own interests are at stake.
🧠
A child raised in a mobster's house sees anything that protects his family as “ethical” — those are his ethics, and society punishes them.
EXPLANATION

The key phrase in the definition: when their own self-interests are at stake. Where do individual ethics come from? The book lists:

SourceIn Arabic
Familyتأثير العايلة
Peersالأقران — ربعك وزملاءك
Upbringing in generalالتربية عموماً
Lifetime experiences through membership in social institutions مؤسسات اجتماعية such as schools and religions

The book's shocking example: if you are the son or daughter of a mobster, and your upbringing and education take place in an organized crime setting, this affects how you judge situations. You may come to believe that any act — even murder — is ethical if it helps your family or friends. These are your ethics, but they are clearly not the ethics of the wider society, so they are subject to sanction.

The same in companies: managers and employees may come to believe that what they do to promote or protect their organization is more important than any harm they cause other stakeholders — so they behave unethically or illegally, and when this is discovered, they could be sanctioned.

The book's example — the city of Chicago against DoorDash and Grubhub: the city sued both for allegedly deceiving customers and using unfair business practices. The complaint says the two platforms:

  1. advertise delivery services for restaurants without the owners' consent.
  2. conceal the lower prices that restaurants offer directly to customers outside the delivery platforms.
  3. use a “bait and switch” process: they attract customers with low delivery fees, then add extra fees when the customer is about to place the order.

Both companies called the lawsuits “baseless” (without any basis), and as of the writing of the chapter the lawsuits are still pending but moving forward in the courts. And Chicago announced a $10 million settlement with Uber Eats for similar unethical practices.

The book's conclusion: many decisions one person finds unethical — like using animals for cosmetics testing — may be acceptable to another person. And if the behavior is not illegal, people either agree to disagree, or try to impose their beliefs on others and make them the law. In all cases, people should develop and follow the ethical criteria described earlier (the four rules — lesson 2) to balance their self-interest against the interests of others.

EXAM BOX

Individual ethics are personal standards and values that determine how people view their responsibilities to other people and groups and, thus, how they should act in situations when their own self-interests are at stake. Sources of individual ethics include the influence of one's family, peers, and upbringing in general. The experiences gained over a lifetime—through membership in social institutions such as schools and religions, for example—also contribute to the development of the personal standards and values that a person uses to evaluate a situation and decide what is the morally right or wrong way to behave.

In all cases, however, people should develop and follow the ethical criteria described earlier to balance their self-interests against those of others when determining how they should behave in a particular situation.

TRAP
  • Schools and religions are a source of individual ethics (through lifetime experiences in social institutions) — not of societal ethics. The word “social” here will trick you if you do not pay attention.
  • If the scenario has family, peers, upbringing → individual. If it has the country's laws and customs → societal. If it has the rules of a profession → occupational. If it has the company's code → organizational.
  • The phrase self-interests are at stake is the fingerprint of the individual ethics definition. And the definition starts with personal standards and values, while societal and occupational start with standards that govern.
  • Bait and switch = low fees attract you, then extra fees at the last minute. And the $10 million settlement was with Uber Eats — not with DoorDash or Grubhub (their lawsuits are still pending).

Organizational Ethics and the Company Credo
The company's own ethics — they start with the founders and top managers and are written in the credo
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Organizational ethics = the guiding practices and beliefs through which a company and its managers see their responsibility toward stakeholders — and their most famous form is the credo (a code of ethics).
🧠
The J&J credo is a line of four: patients first, then employees, then communities, and stockholders at the end of the line.
EXPLANATION — THE DEFINITION AND THE CREDO

The individual ethics of a company's founders and top managers are especially important in shaping the organization's code of ethics. Organizations whose founders had a vital role in creating a highly ethical code of behavior (four by name): UPS, Procter & Gamble, Johnson & Johnson, and the Prudential Insurance Company.

Johnson & Johnson's code of ethics — called its credo — reflects a well-developed concern for its stakeholders. And the credo has three purposes, according to the book:

  1. To deter self-interested, unethical behavior.
  2. To show managers and employees that the company will not tolerate people who put their personal interests above the interests of other stakeholders and ignore the harm they inflict on them.
  3. To show that those who act unethically will be punished.
Figure 4.5 — Johnson & Johnson's Credo

This is Figure 4.5 — the full text of the J&J code under the title Our Credo. It has 4 paragraphs = 4 responsibilities ordered by priority. The first paragraph starts with We believe our first responsibility is to the patients, doctors and nurses…, and the last paragraph starts with Our final responsibility is to our stockholders. How to read it: the order itself is the message — customers/patients first, and stockholders last. And notice that the credo says stockholders, not shareholders (the same meaning). What they ask: Who is the first responsibility? Who is the last? Or they give you a sentence from a paragraph (fair share of taxes) and ask which group it belongs to.

EXPLANATION — why does an employee go wrong? And when does a credo help?

Managers or workers may behave unethically if they feel pressured by the situation and by unethical top managers. People usually face ethical issues when they weigh their personal interests against the effects of their actions on others.

The book's example: a manager knows his promotion to vice president is likely if he can secure a $100 million contract, but getting the contract needs a $1 million bribe to the contract giver. He tells himself: this will protect my career and future, and what harm would it do anyway? Bribery is common, and if I do not pay, someone else surely will.

The research finding (memorize it): people who feel they have the most at stake — in a career sense or a monetary sense — are the most likely to act unethically. And exactly in this situation, a strong code of organizational ethics helps the person do the right thing. A positive example: The New York Times has a detailed code of ethics written by its editors to ensure the integrity and honesty of its journalists as they report sensitive information.

The role of top managers: if they consistently endorse the principles of the credo, they can prevent employees from going astray. Two things are needed from them:

  1. Model the behavior they expect from their employees.
  2. Educate employees in the ethical behavior the organization expects.

Companies can also embed ethical behavior in their processes (the Manager as a Person box below). And employees are much more likely to act unethically when a credo does not exist or is disregarded.

A negative example — Arthur Andersen (an accounting firm): it did not follow its credo at all. Its unscrupulous partners ordered middle managers to shred records that proved their wrongdoing. The middle managers knew it was wrong, but they obeyed because they responded to the personal power and status of the partners — not to the company's code. They were afraid of losing their jobs if they did not do it, and they lost them anyway when the company was forced to close because of its unethical practices.

EXPLANATION — the Manager as a Person box: Do People Just Need a Nudge?

Organizations often assume that ethical behavior = teaching the standards + punishing the violators. Both are needed, but education and punishment alone often fail. Why? Because sometimes the employee works in a system where the immediate rewards of unethical behavior are bigger than the rewards of the ethical choice — an employee who closes a sale gets immediate, enthusiastic praise, while any investigation into how he closed it might come much later, or never.

ConceptWhat it isThe book's example
Nudging making a desired action easier to choose Retirement savings plans: many employees did not join (maybe because joining would shrink their paychecks). Companies that made joining the default — so anyone who wanted out had to ask — saw more employees take part
When a nudge fails or backfires A simple view of nudging can fail or even backfire A company that wants to save resources sends email reminders: (1) they interest only employees who already care about the environment · (2) frequent reminders annoy people, so they resist · (3) nudges based on detailed data about people without their knowledge and consent are seen as unethical
Behavioral risk management A way to promote ethical behavior that uses various research methods to understand how a company's systems and processes shape employee behavior — nudges are part of it It looks for 3 red flags: blaming employees personally when something goes wrong · distributing rewards unfairly · a lack of trust between colleagues
Example of a solution An insurance company whose employees felt their goals and their coworkers' goals were competing They created an email signature panel with the names, faces, and titles of all the team members serving the client — so every time an employee sent an email, he remembered that he represents a team
EXPLANATION — the board of directors watches the top managers

Top managers play a crucial role in deciding a company's ethics, so the board of directors must scrutinize the reputations and ethical records of top managers before appointing them:

  • It is the board's responsibility to decide whether a prospective CEO has the maturity, experience, and integrity to lead the company and be entrusted with its capital and wealth — on which the fate of all stakeholders depends.
  • A track record of success alone is not enough — he might have achieved that success through unethical or illegal means.
  • It is true that the best predictor of future behavior is often past behavior, but the board must be on guard against unprincipled executives who use unethical means to rise to the top — so continuous monitoring of their behavior is necessary.
  • This scrutiny led to the dismissal of many executives for: excessive personal loans, stock options, inflated expense accounts, and even sexual misconduct.

The end-of-chapter exercise (Managing Ethically — Double Standard in Ingredients?, linked to LO4-5): Unilever was criticized by customers in China because its Magnum ice cream there had inferior ingredients compared to the European version (milk powder instead of condensed milk, and more vegetable oil). The question it asks: why can an organization's values and norms become too strong and lead to unethical behavior, and how can a company stop its values from becoming so inwardly focused that managers forget their responsibility to stakeholders?

EXAM BOX

Organizational ethics are the guiding practices and beliefs through which a particular company and its managers view their responsibility toward their stakeholders. The individual ethics of a company's founders and top managers are especially important in shaping the organization's code of ethics.

Johnson & Johnson's code of ethics—its credo—reflects a well-developed concern for its stakeholders (see Figure 4.5). Company credos, such as that of Johnson & Johnson, are meant to deter self-interested, unethical behavior; to demonstrate to managers and employees that a company will not tolerate people who, because of their own poor ethics, put their personal interests above the interests of other organizational stakeholders and ignore the harm they are inflicting on others; and to demonstrate that those who act unethically will be punished.

Research seems to suggest that people who realize they have the most at stake in a career sense or a monetary sense are the ones most likely to act unethically. Employees are much more likely to act unethically when a credo does not exist or is disregarded.

Increasingly, managers seeking alternatives consider “nudging” employees—that is, making a desired action easier to choose. This approach to promoting ethical conduct, often called behavioral risk management, uses various research methods to understand how a company's systems and processes shape employee behavior.

Top managers play a crucial role in determining a company's ethics.

TRAP
  • The J&J credo: first responsibility = patients, doctors, nurses, and all users of the products, and last responsibility = stockholders. The order: customers → employees → communities → stockholders. Any choice that puts stockholders first or employees first is wrong.
  • Organizational ethics are shaped by the individual ethics of the founders and top managers — this link between individual and organizational comes up as a question. And the definition is about the company's responsibility toward stakeholders.
  • The credo's purposes are three: deter · show no tolerance · show punishment. “Increase profits” or “attract investors” is not one of them.
  • The most likely to go wrong: those with the most at stake in a career or money sense — not new employees and not people with nothing to lose.
  • Nudging = making the right choice easier (like the default option), not forcing and not punishing. And behavioral risk management = research methods to understand how systems and processes affect behavior — not just a training course.
  • Arthur Andersen = an example of a disregarded credo, and the middle managers obeyed the partners' power, not the code.

Business ethicsThe result that the four sources flow intothe box in the center of Figure 4.4 — a result, not a source
Societal ethicsStandards that govern how members of a society deal with one anotherfour issues: fairness · justice · poverty · rights of the individual; they come from laws and unwritten norms
InternalizeTo make values, beliefs, and norms a part of your own moralsthen you act automatically — it happens with society and with the profession
Occupational ethicsStandards for members of a profession, trade, or craft when performing work-related activitiesthe VW example; it is not organizational
Medical ethicsHow doctors and nurses should treat their patientsan occupational example: only necessary procedures, in the patient's interest
Professional ethicsThe name used in the title of Table 4.2= occupational ethics; 4 categories and 13 failures, and only sales and marketing has 4
Individual ethicsPersonal standards and values for when your own self-interests are at stakesources: family, peers, upbringing, schools and religions
Bait and switchAttracting customers with low fees, then adding extra fees before they orderDoorDash and Grubhub; the $10 million settlement was with Uber Eats
Organizational ethicsThe guiding practices and beliefs through which a company and its managers view their responsibility toward stakeholdersshaped by the ethics of the founders and top managers
Code of ethics / credoA company's written statement of its ethicsthree purposes: deter · show no tolerance · show punishment; employees go wrong more when there is no credo or it is disregarded
Johnson & Johnson's CredoThe J&J code of ethics in Figure 4.5patients and users first → employees → communities → stockholders last
NudgingMaking a desired action easier to chooseeasier, not forced (the retirement default example)
Behavioral risk managementUsing research methods to understand how a company's systems and processes shape employee behaviorabout systems and processes — not just a training course
Red flagsThe warning signs that behavioral risk management looks forthree: blaming employees personally · unfair rewards · lack of trust in colleagues
