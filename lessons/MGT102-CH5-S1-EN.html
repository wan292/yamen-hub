FROM THE CHAPTER OPENER — A Manager's Challenge: How the NFL Is Tackling Diversity Head On

The chapter opens with a question: What steps can an organization take to ensure diversity among its workforce? Its example is the National Football League (NFL), which wants its staff and teams to come from the same people who play the game and cheer for their teams:

  • It measures with numbers: it analyzes data on its employees by race, ethnicity, gender, and generational categories. It found that 39% of its coaches in 2021 were from a racial or ethnic minority (up from 35% the year before), but the share of minority head coaches stayed at 9%, and the number of minority offensive coordinators actually declined in the same period.
  • Hiring goals: every NFL team must employ an offensive assistant who is a woman or a member of an ethnic or racial minority — because past promotions to head coach came mostly from people with experience in coaching the offense, so this puts them on the path.
  • Rooney Rule (since 2003): a team that wants to hire a head coach must interview at least two external candidates from a racial or ethnic minority group. The league recently added that key decision makers in hiring coaches and general managers must be trained in inclusive hiring.
  • 2022 — sports medicine: it partnered with the NFL Physicians Society and the Professional Football Athletic Trainer Society, and in the first year it focused on Black medical students from historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs): 16 internships with 8 teams. The reason: research shows that health outcomes of diverse patients tend to be better when the medical staff is diverse — so diversity serves the players' health.
  • Progress and the gap: the share of female coaches rose, but just 9% of head coaches are Black compared with 58% of players, and a 25% increase in female coaches at training camp was only from 12 women to 15 — the percentage can fool you.

The lesson the book wants: maybe the NFL's problem is that it focused too narrowly on hiring. Fully embracing diversity will require more than just targets. In organizations that really benefit from diversity, diverse employees are fully included and heard, not just recruited and hired.

EXPLANATION — WHAT DOES MANAGING DIVERSITY WELL MEAN? (Overview)

The book says the effective management of diversity and inclusion is more than hiring diverse employees. It means three things:

  1. learning to appreciate and respond appropriately to the needs, attitudes, beliefs, and values that diverse people bring to an organization.
  2. correcting misconceptions about why and how groups of employees differ from one another.
  3. finding the most effective way to use the skills and talents of diverse employees.

The environment itself is becoming diverse in every way: not only the global workforce, but suppliers and customers too. So managers need to manage diversity proactively to attract and retain the best employees and compete in a global environment. The book's example: managers at the audit and consulting firm Deloitte started a program that encourages suppliers from minority ethnic groups to compete for its business, and the firm sponsors schools and colleges that supply well-trained recruits.

Sometimes well-intentioned managers inadvertently treat various groups of employees differently, even though there are no performance-based differences between them. The whole chapter explains why this happens and the steps that prevent it.

EXPLANATION — THE FOUR REASONS (LEARN THEM IN ORDER)

The book says there are several reasons diversity stays a pressing concern, both in the popular press and for managers and organizations — and it lists four points:

#The reasonWhat the book says exactly
1Ethical imperative In many societies there is strong ethical pressure that minorities and other underrepresented groups must get equal opportunities and be treated fairly and justly. And unfair treatment is also illegal.
2It improves organizational effectiveness When managers manage diversity well, they encourage other managers to treat people fairly and justly, and they realize that diversity is an important organizational resource that can help the organization gain a competitive advantage. Research says there are specific situations in which diversity can enhance performance — for example, in teams — but simply having a diverse workforce may not guarantee higher performance. Managers are still exploring the impact of diversity on the workplace.
3Unfair treatment continues There is substantial evidence that diverse people still face unfair treatment at work because of biases, stereotypes, and overt discrimination — you will study all three in lesson 3. The evidence:
  • A review of many studies: hiring discrimination against African Americans has not changed significantly over the past 25 years in the United States, and Latinos saw only a moderate drop.
  • A survey of U.S. workers: “pay secrecy” in private-sector companies may reduce women's bargaining power on salaries, and it may be a factor in the ongoing gender wage gap.
4The glass ceiling is beginning to crack The invisible barrier that stops women and minorities from being promoted to top positions is beginning to crack — but much work still needs to be done. The numbers: in November 2022 only 32 leaders of Fortune 500 companies were women (6.4%), and only two Black women run top organizations: Roz Brewer (CEO of Walgreens Boots Alliance) and Thasunda Brown Duckett (CEO of TIAA).

And the book's Summary and Review at the end of the chapter puts them in one sentence: diversity is a pressing concern for managers and organizations for business and ethical reasons — the same idea you will come back to in lesson 2.

Diversity raises important ethical issues and social responsibility issues (see Chapter 4). It is also a critical issue for organizations—one that if not handled well can bring an organization to its knees, especially in our increasingly global environment.

Effectively managing a diverse team of employees can improve organizational effectiveness. When managers effectively manage diversity, they not only encourage other managers to treat diverse members of an organization fairly and justly but also realize that diversity is an important organizational resource that can help an organization gain a competitive advantage. Current research suggests there are specific situations in which diversity can enhance performance, for example, in teams. However, simply having a diverse workforce may not guarantee higher performance.

TRAP

  • The book's sentence: simply having a diverse workforce may not guarantee higher performance. A choice that says guarantees higher performance is the opposite of the book. Diversity raises performance in specific situations like teams.

  • Glass ceiling ≠ overt discrimination: the second is deliberate and open discrimination (lesson 3); the first is a barrier no one can see.

EXPLANATION — AGE
  • According to the U.S. Census Bureau, the median age in the United States is the highest it has ever been: 38.8 years.
  • It is projected that by 2060 close to 24% of the population will be 65 or older.
  • The law: the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 prohibits age discrimination (details in Table 5.1 — concept 5).

What does the manager do? (three things)

  1. He is vigilant so that no employee is discriminated against because of age.
  2. He makes sure the policies and procedures he has in place treat all workers fairly, regardless of their ages.
  3. Managing diversity well means employees of different ages learn from each other, work well together, and take advantage of the unique perspective each one has to offer.

EXPLANATION — STAKEHOLDERS ARE DIVERSE TOO (DIVERSITY OF THE ENVIRONMENT)

The book makes the picture bigger: the growing racial and ethnic diversity in an organization's environment (customers and suppliers) affects its decision making and effectiveness. Managers and organizations are more and more reminded that stakeholders are diverse and expect organizational decisions and actions to reflect this diversity.

The book's example — the entertainment industry: many advocacy groups continue to lobby the entertainment industry to increase the diversity in TV programming, acting, writing, and producing. Hispanics are more than 19% of the U.S. population (more than 62 million potential viewers), but less than 8% of the characters in prime-time TV shows are Hispanic — according to a Hollywood diversity report by UCLA's College of Social Sciences. And the pressure keeps growing on networks, streaming services, and the media in general to reflect the diversity of the population.

EXPLANATION — RELIGION
  • Title VII of the Civil Rights Act prohibits discrimination based on religion (and also on race/ethnicity, country of origin, and sex — see Table 5.1 and Chapter 12).
  • On top of it, in 1997 the federal government issued “The White House Guidelines on Religious Exercise and Expression in the Federal Workplace”. These guidelines technically apply only in federal offices, but large corporations frequently rely on them.
  • The guidelines require employers to make reasonable accommodations for religious practices, such as observances of holidays, as long as doing so does not entail major costs or hardships.

What does the manager do in practice? He is aware of different religions and their beliefs, and pays special attention to when religious holidays fall:

  • He does not schedule critical meetings on a holy day for members of a certain faith.
  • He is flexible in giving time off for religious observances.
  • Small but powerful accommodations: letting an employee leave work early on certain days instead of taking a lunch break, or posting the holidays of different religions on the company calendar.

The result: people feel respected and valued and able to practice their faith, and employee loyalty is often enhanced. An important number: according to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), the number of religious discrimination complaints and settlement amounts increased by more than 50% over the past 20 years.

EXPLANATION — CAPABILITIES/DISABILITIES
  • The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990 does two things: it prohibits discrimination against persons with disabilities, and it requires employers to make reasonable accommodations so these people can perform their jobs effectively.
  • Few people would argue with the goal of the law, but applying it brings challenges. The main one: some people hesitate to reveal their disabilities to employers, and so they miss the accommodations they need. So the key challenge for the manager: to promote an environment in which employees who need accommodation feel comfortable disclosing their needs.
  • That is why the manager often must educate both himself and his employees about disabilities.
  • A number: the unemployment rate for workers with a disability is twice that of workers without disabilities.
  • But many companies are committed to hiring them, and they see diversity and inclusion as two strategies vital to their success. In 2022 the American Association of People with Disabilities and Disability:IN recognized 241 companies with their “Best Places to Work” award, including Airbnb, Best Buy, Hilton, Starbucks, and Target. These companies scored 100% on the Disability Equality Index = a benchmarking tool that gives organizations an objective score on their disability and inclusion policies and practices.
EXPLANATION — SOCIOECONOMIC BACKGROUND

From a management point of view: socioeconomic diversity (and especially diversity in income levels) requires the manager to be sensitive and responsive to the needs and concerns of people who might not be as wealthy as others.

The story in numbers: in the middle to late 1990s, U.S. welfare reform pushed single mothers and others receiving public assistance to join or return to the workforce. With a strong economy, the result was record declines in the number of families, households, and children living below the poverty level (according to the 2000 census).

  • When the child tax credits ended at the end of 2021, more than 3.7 million children slipped back into poverty in early 2022.
  • How is the poverty line set? The Census Bureau relies on predetermined threshold income figures based on family size and composition, adjusted every year for inflation. Families whose income falls below the threshold level are considered poor. Example: in 2022 a family of four was considered poor if its yearly income fell below $27,750.
  • When workers earn less than $15 per hour, it is often difficult, if not impossible, to meet their families' needs. And more families face the challenge of finding suitable child care.
  • During COVID-19: companies sent employees home to work, and schools closed for long periods with children learning remotely. In a survey of working parents, nearly 20% had to leave work or reduce their hours solely because of the lack of child care. More than 2 million women are estimated to have left the labor force to care for their children, and many went back in 2022 as the pandemic eased, schools reopened, and high inflation persisted.

What does the manager do? (two points)

  1. He is sensitive and responsive to the needs of workers who may be less fortunate than himself in: income and financial resources, child care and older adult care options, housing opportunities, and sources of social and family support.
  2. Equally important: he gives them opportunities to learn, advance, and make meaningful contributions to their organizations while improving their economic well-being.

EXPLANATION — OTHER KINDS OF DIVERSITY

There are other important kinds in organizations; managers must deal with them well, and they are all potential sources of unfair treatment:

  • Backgrounds and experiences: organizations and teams need members with diverse backgrounds and experiences. The clearest example is the prevalence of cross-functional teams in organizations — teams whose members come from different departments such as marketing, production, finance, and sales (teams are covered in Chapter 15). For example, a team that develops and introduces a new product often needs the expertise of people not only from research and design and engineering, but also from marketing, sales, production, and finance.
  • Physical appearance: employees differ in how they look, and this has no bearing on their job performance — unless the job itself is one where appearance plays a role, such as modeling. Even so, sometimes these physical sources of diversity affect advancement rates and salaries.
  • The numbers: in a recent survey, when 500 hiring professionals were shown a picture of a larger-bodied woman, only 15.6% said they would hire her. In another study, biased workers saw larger-bodied people as less competent.

The conclusion: the manager must make sure all employees are treated fairly, regardless of their physical appearance.

EXPLANATION — THE CLOSING IDEA OF LO5-1

The book ends this section by joining everything together. The increasing diversity of the environment (customers, suppliers, society) increases the diversity of the workforce, and that, in turn, increases the challenges managers face in managing diversity effectively. Each kind of diversity you just studied brings its own set of issues that managers must understand before they can respond to them well — and this is not always simple, as many informed managers have discovered.

Here is the point of the book: research on how different groups are currently treated and on the unconscious biases that might adversely affect them is vital, because it helps managers become aware of the many subtle ways in which diverse employee groups can come to be treated unfairly over time. You will study bias, stereotypes, and overt discrimination in lesson 3, and the steps managers can take in lesson 4.

EXAM BOX

The guidelines require that employers make reasonable accommodations for religious practices, such as observances of holidays, as long as doing so does not entail major costs or hardships.

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990 prohibits discrimination against persons with disabilities and requires that employers make reasonable accommodations to enable these people to effectively perform their jobs. A key challenge for managers is to promote an environment in which employees who need accommodation feel comfortable disclosing their needs.

The term socioeconomic background typically refers to a combination of social class and income-related factors. From a management perspective, socioeconomic diversity (and, in particular, diversity in income levels) requires that managers be sensitive and responsive to the needs and concerns of individuals who might not be as wealthy as others.

In 2015, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission pronounced that workplace discrimination on the grounds of sexual orientation is illegal, according to federal law.

Other kinds of diversity are important in organizations, are critical for managers to deal with effectively, and are potential sources of unfair treatment.

Employees differ from each other in physical appearance, and it has no bearing on their job performance unless they have jobs in which physical appearance plays a role, such as modeling.

TRAP
  • Reasonable accommodations come twice: for religion (the White House guidelines of 1997, as long as it does not entail major costs or hardships) and for disability (the ADA, 1990). Check which one the question asks about.
  • The 1997 guidelines are guidelines, not a law, and technically only for federal offices — large corporations rely on them. They are not a row in Table 5.1.
  • The ADA has two parts: it prohibits + it requires accommodations. And the challenge the book names: the employee hesitates to reveal his disability — so the manager creates an environment where disclosing feels comfortable.
  • The Disability Equality Index (disability, 241 companies) ≠ the Corporate Equality Index (LGBTQ, 842 against 13 two decades ago, four components). The exam mixes up the two indexes.
  • Socioeconomic background = social class + income-related factors — not education and not race. And poverty: 2018 = 13.9% · 2020 = 9.1% (the lowest, because of government help) · 2021 = 12.8%.
  • Sexual orientation: 2015 = an EEOC pronouncement (not a law in the table) · 7.1% in 2021 = double 2012.
  • Physical appearance: the only exception in the book is modeling · 500 professionals and only 15.6%.
  • The closing chain: a more diverse environment → a more diverse workforce → more challenges for managers. Research on unconscious biases matters because it shows the subtle ways groups are treated unfairly over time — an option saying unfair treatment is always open and obvious is the opposite of the book.

EXAM BOX

Equal Pay Act (1963): Requires that all employees, regardless of gender, be paid equally if they are performing equal work.

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964): Prohibits discrimination in employment decisions on the basis of race, religion, sex, color, or national origin; covers a wide range of employment decisions, including hiring, firing, pay, promotion, and working conditions.

Age Discrimination in Employment Act (1967): Prohibits discrimination against workers over the age of 40 and restricts mandatory retirement.

Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978): Prohibits discrimination against women in employment decisions on the basis of pregnancy, childbirth, and related medical decisions.

Americans with Disabilities Act (1990): Prohibits discrimination against individuals with a disability in employment decisions and requires that employers make accommodations for workers with a disability to enable them to perform their jobs.

Civil Rights Act (1991): Prohibits discrimination (as does Title VII) and allows for the awarding of punitive and compensatory damages, in addition to back pay, in cases of intentional discrimination.

Family and Medical Leave Act (1993): Requires that employers provide 12 weeks of unpaid leave for medical and family reasons, including paternity and illness of a family member.

Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act (1994): Protects the civilian employment of active and reserve military personnel called to active duty; prohibits discrimination on the basis of military status and military service obligations; also provides certain reemployment rights following military service.

TRAP
  • 1963 = Equal Pay Act (pay only, regardless of gender) against 1964 = Title VII (discrimination in all employment decisions). Do not reverse them.
  • Title VII has five bases: race, religion, sex, color, national origin. Age, disability, and sexual orientation are not among them — a choice that says Title VII prohibits age discrimination is wrong (age = ADEA, 1967).
  • Civil Rights Act 1964 against 1991: the difference is that 1991 adds punitive and compensatory damages on top of back pay in cases of intentional discrimination.
  • ADEA = workers over 40 exactly (not 50 and not 65), and it restricts mandatory retirement.
  • FMLA = 12 weeks, unpaid — not paid, and it includes paternity (so fathers too) and the illness of a family member.
  • ADA 1990 = prohibits and requires accommodations. Pregnancy = 1978. And the last law is 1994, for the military.
  • The count is eight. The 1997 White House guidelines and the EEOC's 2015 pronouncement are not rows in the table.

2. A company refuses to hire qualified applicants who are over 50 years old and forces all employees to retire at 60. According to Table 5.1, the company is most likely violating the:
  1. Equal Pay Act (1963)
  2. Age Discrimination in Employment Act (1967)
  3. Family and Medical Leave Act (1993)
  4. Pregnancy Discrimination Act (1978)
Answer

B. The ADEA of 1967 prohibits discrimination against workers over the age of 40 and restricts mandatory retirement — and the scenario has both. The trap in A: the Equal Pay Act is about equal pay regardless of gender, not age. C is a trap too: the FMLA is about 12 weeks of unpaid leave. And D is about pregnancy and childbirth. Notice that the number 50 in the question changes nothing — the protection starts over 40.

3. Which law prohibits discrimination as Title VII does but also allows for the awarding of punitive and compensatory damages, in addition to back pay, in cases of intentional discrimination?
  1. Equal Pay Act (1963)
  2. Americans with Disabilities Act (1990)
  3. Civil Rights Act (1991)
  4. Uniformed Services Employment and Reemployment Rights Act (1994)
Answer

C. This is the description of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, word for word, in Table 5.1: it prohibits discrimination (as does Title VII) and adds punitive and compensatory damages on top of back pay in cases of intentional discrimination. Be careful: Title VII itself (1964) does not have the damages — they are what the 1991 law adds. B is a trap: the ADA prohibits discrimination against people with a disability and requires accommodations; it has nothing to do with damages. And A and D are about pay regardless of gender and about the military.

4. Maria is a top-rated manager. She can clearly see the vice president positions above her, yet she and other women and minorities in her company keep getting passed over because of invisible barriers that no written policy mentions. This situation is best described as:
  1. The gender wage gap
  2. The glass ceiling
  3. Pay secrecy
  4. Reasonable accommodation
Answer

B. The glass ceiling = a metaphor alluding to the invisible barriers that prevent minorities and women from being promoted to top corporate positions — you see the position but cannot reach it, and the barrier cannot be seen. The trap in A: the gender wage gap is about pay ($968 against $1,166), not about promotion to top positions. C is a trap too: pay secrecy is the hiding of salaries that reduces women's bargaining power. And D is about accommodations for religion or disability.

5. Which of the following is NOT one of the reasons the text gives for why diversity is such a pressing concern for managers and organizations?
  1. There is a strong ethical imperative that minorities and other underrepresented groups receive equal opportunities, and unfair treatment is also illegal.
  2. Effectively managing diversity can improve organizational effectiveness and help an organization gain a competitive advantage.
  3. Simply having a diverse workforce guarantees higher performance.
  4. The glass ceiling is beginning to crack, though much work still needs to be done.
Answer

C. The book says exactly the opposite: simply having a diverse workforce may not guarantee higher performance — diversity raises performance only in specific situations, such as teams. The trap in A, B, and D: these are really three of the four reasons (the fourth: unfair treatment continues because of biases, stereotypes, and overt discrimination). And the question uses NOT, so look for the sentence that exaggerates with the word guarantees.
