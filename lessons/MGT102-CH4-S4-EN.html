Chapter 4 — Approaches to Social Responsibility

MGT 102 · Chapter 4 · Section 4 of 4: Approaches to Social Responsibility
Objective LO4-6: Distinguish among the four main approaches toward social responsibility that a company can take.

The last lesson of the chapter: a ladder with four steps — learn its order like your own name

In the previous lesson you saw that a company's ethics come from four sources: societal, occupational, individual, organizational. Now we see what comes out of them: the company's stance on social responsibility — how the company sees its duty toward its stakeholders and toward society as a whole.

The heart of the lesson is Figure 4.6: four approaches on a ladder from the lowest responsibility to the highest — obstructionist → defensive → accommodative → proactive. The exam likes to give you a company scenario and ask which approach it is, or to flip the order on you. After that you see why a company should be responsible at all (reputation and more), and how culture and the ethics officer turn ethics into a daily habit, not just a piece of paper.

And the numbers, as always in this chapter: 4 approaches, 9 forms in Table 4.3, 3 reasons to be responsible (the book numbers two and brings the third with Moreover — we count it as the third), and 3 responsibilities for the ethics officer. Take the English definitions exactly as they are from the EXAM BOX.

Social Responsibility
A company's ethics decide its stance — and the definition has three verbs
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Social responsibility = how a company's managers and employees see their duty to make decisions that protect, enhance, and promote the good of stakeholders and society as a whole.
🧠
A company sold a faulty batch by mistake. Announce it and pull it back, or hide it? That answer is its stance.
EXPLANATION — WHERE DOES THE STANCE COME FROM?

The book draws a chain you must memorize: a company's ethics are the result of the differences in the four sources, and the company's ethics in turn determine its stance on social responsibility.

The four sources (societal · organizational · occupational · individual) → the company's ethics → its stance on social responsibility

As the book said before: when no laws specify how a company should act toward stakeholders, managers must decide the right, ethical, and socially responsible thing to do. And because business ethics differ from company to company, companies reach different positions on their responsibility toward stakeholders.

EXPLANATION — DECISIONS THAT SHOW A COMPANY'S STANCE

The book says many decisions signal what a company believes about its obligations. Its three examples:

  1. the decision to spend money on training and educating employees — investing in them.
  2. the decision to minimize or avoid layoffs whenever possible.
  3. the decision to act promptly and warn customers when a batch of defective merchandise has been accidentally sold.

Companies that try to hide problems like these = show little regard for social responsibility. Here are the book's examples side by side:

❌ Hiding and delaying✅ A voluntary recall
GM and Ford tried in the past to hide that several of their vehicles had defects that made them dangerous to drive. They were penalized with hundreds of millions of dollars in damages, and today they move more quickly to recall vehicles and fix problems.

Mary Barra, CEO of General Motors, admitted that the company did not react fast enough when a fault was found with an ignition switch — the recall eventually reached more than 10 million cars worldwide.		 Fitbit voluntarily recalled its activity tracking wrist band, the Fitbit Force, because of skin rash issues. It offered to send customers a return kit, and promised a reimbursement check or an exchange within two to six weeks of receipt.

The rule the book draws: the way a company announces business problems or admits its mistakes gives strong clues about its stance on social responsibility.

EXAM BOX

A company's ethics are the result of differences in societal, organizational, occupational, and individual ethics. In turn, a company's ethics determine its stance on social responsibility. A company's stance on social responsibility is the way its managers and employees view their duty or obligation to make decisions that protect, enhance, and promote the welfare and well-being of stakeholders and society as a whole.

Companies that try to hide such problems show little regard for social responsibility. The way a company announces business problems or admits its mistakes provides strong clues about its stance on social responsibility.

TRAP
  • The definition is about stakeholders and society as a whole — all stakeholders and society, not only the shareholders. And the three verbs are protect, enhance, and promote. A choice that says “maximize shareholder profit” is wrong.
  • The direction of the arrow: the four sources → the company's ethics → its stance. A choice that says the stance decides the sources is reversed.
  • Table 4.3 has 9 forms. A NOT question brings you the opposite of a point: “moves operations abroad to save money” (the table says keep them), “invests in countries with poor human rights records” (the table says decline), “closes the factory” (the table says keep it open).
  • Fitbit = a voluntary recall = a good example. GM and Ford = hiding in the past = a bad example. And careful: GM comes back later as an example of the accommodative approach — the same company in two places.

Four Approaches: Obstructionist and Defensive
The first two steps of the ladder — one breaks the law and hides it, one stops at the edge of the law
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The ladder has four steps from the lowest to the highest. Obstructionist = acts unethically and illegally and hides it. Defensive = obeys the law to the letter and does nothing above it.
🧠
Two students in an exam: one cheats and hides his notes (obstructionist); one never cheats but uses every gap in the rules for himself (defensive).
EXPLANATION — THE WHOLE LADDER

The book says: the strength of companies' commitment to social responsibility can range from low to high, and on that range sit four approaches in order.

Figure 4.6 — Four Approaches to Social Responsibility

This is Figure 4.6 in the book. Four boxes side by side above an arrow called Social Responsibility: the left end says Low social responsibility and the right end says High social responsibility. How to read it, from left to right: Obstructionist approach → Defensive approach → Accommodative approach → Proactive approach — each box has more responsibility than the one before it. So this is a graded ladder, not four separate types. What they ask about it: the order from lowest to highest, which approach is higher or lower than another, or they describe a company and ask where it sits on the ladder. Memorize the first letters: O – D – A – P.

EXPLANATION — 1. Obstructionist approach (the lowest)
The book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Companies and their managers choose not to behave in a socially responsible way and instead behave unethically and illegally. companies and their managers choose not to act in a socially responsible way, and instead they act unethically and illegally.

The text adds: they do all they can to prevent knowledge of their behavior from reaching the other stakeholders and society. So the mark of this approach = hiding. Most of the book's examples are about hiding (and Enron: managers stopped employees selling while they sold their own shares):

EXPLANATION — 2. Defensive approach
The book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Companies and their managers behave ethically to the degree that they stay within the law and strictly abide by legal requirements. they behave ethically only as far as the law goes: they stay inside the law and abide strictly by legal requirements.
  • The book says it shows at least some commitment to ethical behavior — so it is better than obstructionist.
  • But it makes no attempt to practice social responsibility beyond what the law dictates, and because of that it can and often does act unethically. Legal and unethical at the same time.

The book's examples:

  • Computer Associates, World Com, and Merrill Lynch — gave their managers large stock options and bonuses even as company performance was declining rapidly.
  • Managers who sell their shares before the other shareholders because they know the company's performance is about to fall. Acting on inside information is illegal, but it is often hard to prove, because top managers have wide latitude about when they sell their shares.
  • The founders of most tech companies took advantage of this legal loophole to sell billions of dollars of shares before their prices collapsed.

Bottom line: when these managers make ethical decisions, they put their own interests first and commonly harm other stakeholders.

EXAM BOX

The strength of companies' commitment to social responsibility can range from low to high (see Figure 4.6). At the low end of the range is an obstructionist approach, in which companies and their managers choose not to behave in a socially responsible way. Instead they behave unethically and often illegally and do all they can to prevent knowledge of their behavior from reaching other organizational stakeholders and society at large.

A defensive approach indicates at least some commitment to ethical behavior. Defensive companies and managers stay within the law and abide strictly by legal requirements but make no attempt to exercise social responsibility beyond what the law dictates; thus, they can and often do act unethically. When making ethical decisions, such managers put their own interests first and commonly harm other stakeholders.

TRAP
  • The order is O → D → A → P from lowest to highest. If a choice starts with proactive, it is running from highest to lowest — read the question: does it want lowest to highest or the reverse? And in the figure, Low is on the left and High on the right.
  • Obstructionist is the only one the book calls illegal (illegally), and the word that marks it is hiding (prevent knowledge of their behavior from reaching). The margin definition says unethically and illegally, and the text says often illegally.
  • Defensive = legal but can be unethical. This is a direct link to lesson 1: legal is not always ethical. A choice that says defensive companies always act ethically is wrong. And one that says no commitment at all is wrong too — the book said at least some commitment.
  • For a defensive company, the law is the ceiling, not the floor: it stops there and does nothing extra.
  • Do not swap the examples: Manville · tobacco · Lehman Brothers · Enron · Arthur Andersen = obstructionist. Computer Associates · World Com · Merrill Lynch and selling shares before the shareholders = defensive.

Four Approaches: Accommodative and Proactive
The last two steps — the difference is reacting versus taking the first step
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Accommodative = balances stakeholders as the need arises (a reaction). Proactive = goes out of its way to learn what stakeholders need, and uses the organization's resources for all of them (it takes the first step).
🧠
One neighbor helps when you knock and ask (accommodative); another knocks on your door first and asks what you need (proactive).
EXPLANATION — 3. Accommodative approach
The book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Companies and their managers behave legally and ethically and try to balance the interests of different stakeholders as the need arises. companies and their managers act legally and ethically, and try to balance the interests of different stakeholders as the need arises.
  • It acknowledges the need to support social responsibility.
  • It balances so that the claims of shareholders are seen in relation to the claims of other stakeholders — not above them.
  • Managers here want choices that are reasonable in the eyes of society, and they want to do the right thing.
  • This is the approach of the typical large U.S. company, because it has the most to lose from unethical or illegal behavior.
  • The book's rule: the older and more reputable a company, the more likely its managers are to curb attempts by their employees to act unethically.
  • Examples: GM, Intel, 3M, Dell — they seek every way to build their competitive advantage, and still they rein in managers from unethical or illegal behavior, because they know the grave consequences for future profitability.
  • But sometimes they fail: in 2013 SAC Capital Advisors (among others) agreed to pay $1.8 billion and plead guilty to criminal insider trading charges — the biggest insider trading settlement in history to date.
EXPLANATION — 4. Proactive approach (the highest)
The book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Companies and their managers actively embrace socially responsible behavior, going out of their way to learn about the needs of different stakeholder groups and using organizational resources to promote the interests of all stakeholders. companies and their managers actively embrace socially responsible behavior, going out of their way to learn what different stakeholder groups need, and use the organization's resources to serve the interests of all stakeholders.

The text makes it clear: not only shareholders, but employees and communities too. The main example is Nucor (a U.S. steelmaker) — learn the sequence:

YearWhat happened
1977the visionary CEO Ken Iverson announced that Nucor had never laid off one employee in its history, and even though a major recession was raging, it did not plan to start now.
2009CEO Daniel R. DiMicco announced that, again, there would be no layoffs — even though its mills were running at only 50% of capacity (95% just months earlier), because customers had slashed orders in the recession. Rivals laid off thousands, and Nucor stayed loyal to its employees.
But…no layoffs, but managers and employees took major cuts in pay and bonuses to weather the storm together, as they always had, and they searched for ways to reduce operating costs so everyone would benefit when the economy recovered.
2012the sacrifice paid off: Nucor was doing well again.
2022it paid a cash dividend to shareholders for the 198th consecutive quarter.

The causes proactive companies are often at the forefront of — the book separates them with semicolons into four phrases:

  1. A pollution-free environment.
  2. Recycling and conservation of resources.
  3. The minimization or elimination of the use of animals in drug and cosmetics testing.
  4. The reduction of crime, illiteracy, and poverty (three issues in one item).

Companies with reputations for being proactive in supporting stakeholders such as their suppliers or the communities they work in: McDonald's, Google, REI, Whole Foods, Target.

EXPLANATION — the Focus on DEI&B box: Bitty & Beau's Commitment to Human Rights

This box is a detailed example of a proactive company in employee diversity. Its main points:

  • Bitty & Beau's Coffee calls itself “a human rights movement disguised as a coffee shop”.
  • The first location, in Wilmington, North Carolina, employed 19 people with intellectual and developmental disabilities. Six years later: 400 employees in 23 franchises in 12 states, plus several shops inside companies' headquarters.
  • The owners, Amy and Ben Wright (husband and wife), started for personal reasons: of their four children, one has autism and two — Beau and Bitty — have Down syndrome. They saw an 80% unemployment rate among people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, and decided to change that number by starting a business.
  • Their vision: “a path for people with disabilities to become more valued, accepted and included in every community.”
  • From the beginning they sought profitability — and say they achieved it. So in this book, social responsibility is not against profit.
  • The business advantage: in a tight labor market, people with disabilities are an underused source of talent, so job applicants actively look for the company, and turnover is almost nonexistent because the workers are dedicated and enthusiastic.
  • Work design: employees use a deck of playing cards for each order and hand a card to the customer — calling out the card's number and suit is easier than reading customers' names.
  • The impact: Kyle (who has autism) found a sense of community at work and became outgoing, and Sydney (who has Down syndrome) values the dignity of earning a paycheck. The company pays at least minimum wage and gives employee benefits.
EXAM BOX

An accommodative approach acknowledges the need to support social responsibility. Accommodative companies and managers agree that organizational members ought to behave legally and ethically, and they try to balance the interests of different stakeholders so the claims of shareholders are seen in relation to the claims of other stakeholders. This approach is the one taken by the typical large U.S. company, which has the most to lose from unethical or illegal behavior.

Companies and managers taking a proactive approach actively embrace the need to behave in socially responsible ways. They go out of their way to learn about the needs of different stakeholder groups and are willing to use organizational resources to promote the interests not only of shareholders, but also of the other stakeholders such as their employees and communities.

TRAP
  • The biggest trap between the last two steps: as the need arises = accommodative (it responds when a need comes up), going out of their way and actively embrace = proactive (it takes the first step). These words are the difference.
  • The typical large U.S. company = accommodative, not proactive.
  • Proactive serves all stakeholders. A choice that says only shareholders is wrong.
  • Nucor did not lay anyone off, but it cut pay and bonuses — a choice that says “with no sacrifice at all” is wrong. The numbers: 1977 (Iverson) · 2009 (DiMicco, 50% against 95%) · 2012 doing well again · 2022 the 198th quarter.
  • SAC Capital Advisors appears with the accommodative approach = an example that large companies sometimes fail — not an example of the obstructionist approach.
  • Bitty & Beau's = proactive, and it sought profit and achieved it. “Social responsibility means losing money” is wrong.
  • The causes of proactive companies: four phrases, and the fourth holds three issues (crime, illiteracy, poverty). Know both counts.

Why Be Socially Responsible?
Three reasons — and the first one is reputation, with a new definition
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Social responsibility is not only “the right thing” — the book says it is the economically right thing to do: reputation → more business and easier resources → profits.
🧠
A coffee shop everyone trusts: customers come back, suppliers give it the best terms, and good workers want to work there.
EXPLANATION — THE THREE REASONS

The book says many advantages come when a company acts in a socially responsible way, and it numbers only two of them (First and A second major reason); the third comes with Moreover — we count it as the third one to make it easy to remember:

#ReasonDetails
1Reputation Showing social responsibility helps a company build a good reputation. Reputation here = the trust, goodwill, and confidence others have in a company that lead them to want to do business with it — so people want to deal with it.
The rewards of a good reputation: increased business and improved ability to obtain resources from stakeholders. So reputation raises profitability and builds shareholder wealth.
2Costs in a capitalist system In a capitalist system, companies as well as the government have to bear the costs of protecting stakeholders, providing health care and income, paying taxes, and so on. So if all companies in a society act responsibly, the quality of life as a whole increases.
3Companies shape society's values How companies treat their employees decides many of a society's values and norms and the ethics of its citizens. It has been suggested that if all organizations adopted a caring approach and agreed that their responsibility is to promote the interests of their employees, a climate of caring would pervade the wider society.

The countries experts point to as places where organizations are highly socially responsible — five countries: Japan, Sweden, Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland. The result there:

  • crime, poverty, and unemployment are relatively low.
  • literacy rates are relatively high.
  • sociocultural values promote harmony between different groups of people.

Bottom line: business activity affects every part of people's lives, so how business behaves toward stakeholders decides how stakeholders behave toward business. And the book's Summary and Review at the end of the chapter puts it like this: the rewards of acting in a socially responsible way = a good reputation + the support of all stakeholders → and so superior company performance.

EXAM BOX

Several advantages result when companies and their managers behave in a socially responsible manner. First, demonstrating its social responsibility helps a company build a good reputation. Reputation is the trust, goodwill, and confidence others have in a company that lead them to want to do business with it. The rewards for a good company reputation are increased business and improved ability to obtain resources from stakeholders.

A second major reason for companies to act responsibly toward employees, customers, and society is that, in a capitalist system, companies as well as the government, have to bear the costs of protecting their stakeholders, providing health care and income, paying taxes, and so on. So if all companies in a society act responsibly, the quality of life as a whole increases.

The rewards from behaving in a socially responsible way are a good reputation, the support of all organizational stakeholders, and thus superior company performance.

TRAP
  • The chapter has two definitions of reputation: lesson 2 = the esteem or high repute that people or organizations gain when they behave ethically, and here = the trust, goodwill, and confidence others have in a company that lead them to want to do business with it. If the choice has want to do business with it, it is this lesson's definition.
  • The rewards of reputation are two: increased business and improved ability to obtain resources from stakeholders.
  • The second reason: companies as well as the government bear the costs — not the government alone.
  • The countries: five countries where “organizations are highly socially responsible” (Japan, Sweden, Germany, the Netherlands, Switzerland) are not the four “most ethical” countries of lesson 3 (Denmark, Iceland, Sweden, Switzerland). The two lists share Sweden and Switzerland only — do not mix them.
  • The book says social responsibility is the economically right thing to do — a choice that says it always hurts profits is wrong.

Organizational Culture and the Ethics Officer
The code guides the decision; the culture turns ethics into a habit
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A code of ethics guides the decision when an ethical question comes up, but a manager can go one step further: make ethical values part of the organization's culture — by being a role model, and with an ethics officer.
🧠
A match referee with authority over the whole field: any player from any team can report a foul without fearing his own coach.
EXPLANATION — CULTURE AND ROLE MODELS
  • The Southwest Airlines example: Herb Kelleher and Coleen Barrett built a culture where employee well-being is a main priority. That turns into values and norms that say: layoffs should be avoided, and employees should share in the profits. And Google, UPS, Toyota are among the companies that espouse similar values.
  • When ethical values and norms become part of the culture, they help members resist self-interested action, because they feel they are part of something bigger than themselves.
  • The manager as an ethical role model: employees naturally look to people in authority for leadership — just as citizens look to their political leaders — and the manager's behavior is scrutinized by employees.
  • If top managers are seen as self-interested and not ethical, employees are not likely to behave ethically. They think: if it is all right for the boss, it is all right for us. For employees this can mean slacking off, reducing customer support, and doing nothing to help their company.
  • That is why the actions of top managers such as CEOs and the president of the United States are checked so closely: their actions represent the values of their organizations — and in the case of the president, the values of the nation.
EXPLANATION — the ethics officer and the ethics committee

The manager can also give a visible means of support to an ethical culture. And more and more organizations are creating the role of ethics officer to monitor their ethical practices and procedures.

TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Ethics officer
مسؤول الأخلاقيات		 A manager responsible for communicating and teaching ethical standards to all employees and monitoring their conformity to those standards. a manager whose job is to explain and teach ethical standards to all employees, and to watch their conformity to them.

His three responsibilities, as the text lists them:

  1. Communicating ethical standards to all employees.
  2. Designing systems to monitor employees' conformity to those standards.
  3. Teaching managers and employees at all levels of the organization how to respond to ethical dilemmas appropriately.
  • Why is he important? Because he typically has organizationwide authority, so any member from any department can report unethical behavior by his managers or coworkers without fear of retribution. This makes it easier for everyone to behave ethically.
  • He also gives guidance when members are not sure whether an action is ethical.
  • Some organizations have an organizationwide ethics committee: it gives guidance on ethical issues and helps write and update the company code of ethics.

The ending: ethical organizational cultures encourage members to behave in a socially responsible way. The company that epitomizes the ethical, socially responsible firm is Johnson & Johnson: its values and norms, along with its credo (Figure 4.5 in lesson 3), have guided its managers to the right decision in difficult situations for decades.

EXAM BOX

When ethical values and norms such as these are part of an organization's culture, they help organizational members resist self-interested action because they recognize that they are part of something bigger than themselves.

Increasingly, organizations are creating the role of ethics officer, to monitor their ethical practices and procedures. The ethics officer is responsible for communicating ethical standards to all employees, designing systems to monitor employees' conformity to those standards, and teaching managers and employees at all levels of the organization how to respond to ethical dilemmas appropriately. Because the ethics officer typically has organizationwide authority, organizational members in any department can communicate instances of unethical behavior by their managers or coworkers without fear of retribution.

Some organizations have an organizationwide ethics committee to provide guidance on ethical issues and help write and update the company code of ethics.

TRAP
  • Ethics officer = a person (a manager). Ethics committee = a committee. In the text, the one that helps write and update the code of ethics is the committee, not the officer.
  • The ethics officer has three responsibilities: communicate · design monitoring systems · teach how to handle dilemmas. A choice that says “punishes employees” or “writes the laws” is not one of them.
  • Why can an employee report without fear? Because the officer has organizationwide authority — memorize the word.
  • If top managers are unethical, the result is that employees are not likely to behave ethically — not “they are not affected”.
  • Southwest = Herb Kelleher and Coleen Barrett: avoid layoffs + share the profits. Do not mix it with Nucor (the proactive example: no layoffs, but pay cuts).

1. A company stays within the law and abides strictly by legal requirements, but makes no attempt to exercise social responsibility beyond what the law dictates. Its top managers sold their shares just before the company announced poor results. This company's stance is best described as the:
  1. Obstructionist approach
  2. Defensive approach
  3. Accommodative approach
  4. Proactive approach
Answer

B. “Stays within the law + nothing beyond what the law dictates” is the description of the defensive approach word for word, and selling shares before the other shareholders is the book's own example of defensive managers (acting on inside information is illegal but hard to prove). The trap in A: the obstructionist approach breaks the law and hides its behavior, and this company stays inside the law. C is a trap too: the accommodative approach is legal and ethical and balances stakeholders — here the managers put their own interests first. And D is the farthest from the truth: the proactive approach goes out of its way for all stakeholders.

Watch B in particular: if you read fast you may think it says “invest” — but it says decline = refuse.

4. During a recession, a steelmaker's mills operate at only 50% of capacity, yet its CEO announces there will be no layoffs. Managers and employees take cuts in pay and bonuses to weather the storm together. The company goes out of its way to promote the interests of employees and communities, not only shareholders. This company is taking which approach?
  1. Defensive approach
  2. Accommodative approach
  3. Proactive approach
  4. Obstructionist approach
Answer

C. This is the book's Nucor example (2009, 50% of capacity, no layoffs, with cuts in pay and bonuses), and goes out of its way + not only shareholders = the definition of the proactive approach. The trap in B: the accommodative approach balances stakeholders as the need arises — a reaction, not taking the first step. A is a trap: the defensive approach does nothing beyond the law, and no law stops layoffs.

5. Employees in any department of a company can report unethical behavior by their managers or coworkers without fear of retribution, because the person they report to typically has organizationwide authority and is responsible for communicating ethical standards and monitoring employees' conformity to them. This person is best described as:
  1. An ethics committee
  2. An ethics officer
  3. The board of directors
  4. An ethical role model
Answer

B. Organizationwide authority + without fear of retribution + communicating standards and monitoring conformity = exactly the ethics officer, and he is a manager. The trap in A: the ethics committee is a committee, not a person, and its job is guidance and helping write and update the code of ethics. D is a trap: every manager is an ethical role model because employees copy him, but that is not a formal role with authority and monitoring. And C: the board of directors watches top managers (lesson 3); it does not take reports from employees.
