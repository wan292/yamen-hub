The last lesson of the chapter: from the ledger to the financial statements — and how much the company depends on debt

In the previous lesson you recorded FastForward's 16 transactions in the journal and posted them to the ledger. Now, Yamen, we do three things: we collect all the balances on one page called the trial balance and check that debits = credits; then we prepare the three financial statements from it, in order (P1); and finally we measure the risk of debt with one ratio called the debt ratio (A2).

Preparing a trial balance — three steps:

  1. List each account title and its amount (from the ledger) in the trial balance.
  2. Compute the total of the debit balances and the total of the credit balances, separately.
  3. Verify (prove) that the total of the debit balances = the total of the credit balances.

And how do you know which column each balance goes in? From the normal balance that you learned in lesson 2:

See them in the table above: $ 4,275 at the top of the debit column, $ 6,200 at the top of the credit column, and $45,300 in the totals.

EXAM BOX

A trial balance is a list of all ledger accounts and their balances at a point in time. It is not a financial statement but a tool for checking equality of debits and credits in the ledger.

Preparing a trial balance has three steps.

  1. List each account title and its amount (from the ledger) in the trial balance.
  2. Compute the total of debit balances and the total of credit balances.
  3. Verify (prove) total debit balances equal total credit balances.

Dollar signs are not used in journals and ledgers. They do appear in financial statements and other reports such as trial balances. We usually put dollar signs beside only the first and last numbers in a column.

TRAP
  • A trial balance is not a financial statement. Any choice that says it is a financial statement, or puts it with the statements, is wrong. It is only a checking tool.
  • Its heading has the date of one moment (December 31, 2021), like the balance sheet — not For Month Ended.
  • Dividends go in the debit column — not credit. And Unearned consulting revenue goes in credit because it is a liability, not revenue, even though its name has the word revenue.
  • The old Retained earnings (from the year before) enters the trial balance in credit — like Accel.
  • Dollar signs: no in journals and ledgers, yes in the trial balance and the statements — and only beside the first and last numbers in a column.
  • Your doctor's handouts may use a sole proprietorship: Owner's Capital instead of Common Stock, and Owner's Withdrawals (or Drawing) instead of Dividends. Same idea in a different business form, and the same rules: capital is on the credit side like common stock, and withdrawals are on the debit side like dividends.

Trial Balance Errors and Searching for Them
A balanced trial balance is not a clean bill of health — and an unbalanced one, you search it backwards
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
If the trial balance does not balance, there is surely an error, and you search for it in reverse order: from the trial balance back to the journal. And if it does balance? That does not mean there is no error — some errors never show, and the book gives two examples.
🧠
A grocery scale: you weigh a kilo of tomatoes but write onions — the weight is right, but the bill is wrong.
EXPLANATION — WHAT A BALANCED TRIAL BALANCE DOES NOT PROVE

The two totals are equal in Exhibit 2.14. But the book warns: equal totals do not guarantee that no errors were made. The book gives two examples of errors that the trial balance does not catch (examples, not the full list):

#The errorWhy does the trial balance still balance?
1A debit or credit of a correct amount is made to a wrong account The amount went into the right column with the right number, but on the line of another account — the column total did not change.
2Equal debits and credits of an incorrect amount are entered The same mistake went into both sides, so both sides went up or down by the same amount.

An extra point from me (not from the book's text here, but the same logic as number 2): an entry that was never recorded, a whole entry that was never posted, or an entry that was posted twice — debits and credits are affected by the same amount, so the trial balance comes out balanced and the error is still there. The book uses this idea as a wrong choice in its exercises (the last choice in QS 2-12: an entry that was never posted → it does not break the balance).

EXPLANATION — SEARCHING FOR ERRORS: SIX STEPS BACKWARDS

If the trial balance does not balance (its columns are not equal), you must find the error and correct it. An efficient way is to check the journalizing, the posting and the trial balance preparation in reverse order — you start from the last thing you did and go back:

StepWhat you do
1Check that the trial balance columns are added correctly.
2Check that the account balances are accurately copied from the ledger to the trial balance.
3See whether a debit balance is mistakenly listed in the credit column (or the opposite).
🔑 The clue: the difference between total debits and total credits = twice the balance of the wrong account.
4Recompute each account balance in the ledger.
5Check that each journal entry is properly posted.
6Check that the original journal entry has equal debits and credits.

At this point, the errors should be uncovered.

Why “twice” in step 3? Because the balance was taken out of its right column (so that column went down by its amount) and put into the other column (so that one went up by its amount) — so the difference became the amount two times. In other words: divide the difference by 2 and look for an account with that balance.

THE BOOK'S EXAMPLE — A credit to Unearned Revenue posted to Revenue

The question: if a credit that should go to the Unearned Revenue account (a liability) was posted incorrectly to the Revenue account in the ledger, does the ledger still balance?

AccountWhat should happenWhat happenedThe effect
Unearned Revenue (liability)goes up with a creditdid not go up liabilities are understated (lower than the truth)
Revenuenot affectedwent up with a credit income is overstated (higher than the truth) → and equity is overstated too

The answer: the ledger still balances — the credit was recorded with its correct amount, only in the wrong account (error number 1 above). But liabilities are understated, equity is overstated, and income is overstated.

EXAM BOX

Equality of these two totals does not guarantee that no errors were made. For example, the column totals will be equal when a debit or credit of a correct amount is made to a wrong account. Another error not identified with a trial balance is when equal debits and credits of an incorrect amount are entered.

An efficient way to search for an error is to check the journalizing, posting, and trial balance preparation in reverse order. Step 3 is to see whether a debit (or credit) balance is mistakenly listed in the trial balance as a credit (or debit). A clue to this error is when the difference between total debits and total credits equals twice the amount of the incorrect account balance.

Example: If a credit to Unearned Revenue was incorrectly posted to the Revenue ledger account, would the ledger still balance? Answer: The ledger would balance, but liabilities would be understated, equity would be overstated, and income would be overstated.

TRAP
  • “The trial balance balances, so there are no errors” = wrong. The book's two examples of errors that do not show: a correct amount in a wrong account, and equal debits and credits of a wrong amount (this includes an entry never recorded or posted twice).
  • What breaks the balance: something that hits one side only — a wrong addition, a balance copied wrong from the ledger, a balance in the wrong column, a debit posted without its credit, or an original entry whose debits do not equal its credits.
  • The order is reverse: the first step = adding the trial balance columns, and the last = the original journal entry. A choice that starts from the journal is the opposite of the book.
  • The difference = twice the amount when a balance is in the wrong column — not the same amount. Do not look for an account whose balance = the whole difference.
  • The Unearned Revenue example: the ledger balances, liabilities are understated, equity and income are overstated. The exam flips the directions — memorize them as they are.

EXPLANATION — THE REPORTING PERIOD AND THE FISCAL YEAR

A reporting period of one year (annual) is common, and so are semiannual, quarterly and monthly periods — so four common lengths.

  • The reporting period that is one year long is called the accounting year or fiscal year.
  • Businesses whose accounting year begins on January 1 and ends on December 31 are called calendar-year companies.
EXAM BOX

A balance sheet reports an organization’s financial position at a point in time. The income statement, statement of retained earnings, and statement of cash flows report financial performance over a period of time. The three statements in the middle column of Exhibit 2.15 explain how financial position changes from the beginning to the end of a reporting period.

A one-year (annual) reporting period is common, as are semiannual, quarterly, and monthly periods. The one-year reporting period is called the accounting, or fiscal, year. Businesses whose accounting year begins on January 1 and ends on December 31 are called calendar-year companies.

TRAP
  • Only one is a point in time: the balance sheet. The other three (income, retained earnings, cash flows) are a period. Any choice that says the balance sheet is “over a period of time” is wrong.
  • The statements that explain the change are the three in the middle — not the beginning or the ending balance sheet.
  • Fiscal year = accounting year = a reporting period one year long. And a calendar-year company = its year runs from January 1 to December 31 exactly. A company whose year runs, for example, from July to June has a fiscal year, but it is not a calendar-year company.
  • The common periods are four: annual, semiannual, quarterly, monthly.

EXAM BOX

An income statement reports revenues earned minus expenses incurred over a period of time. Owner investments and dividends are not part of income.

The statement of retained earnings reports how retained earnings changes over the reporting period. The beginning balance in the statement of retained earnings is rarely zero, except in the first period of operations.

The balance sheet reports the financial position of a company at a point in time. This presentation of the balance sheet is called the account form: assets on the left and liabilities and equity on the right. Another presentation is the report form: assets on top, followed by liabilities and then equity. Either presentation is acceptable.

An income statement is also called an earnings statement, a statement of operations, or a P&L (profit and loss) statement. A balance sheet is also called a statement of financial position.

A statement’s heading lists the 3 W’s: Who—name of organization, What—name of statement, When—point in time or period of time.

TRAP
  • The order is a must: income → retained earnings → balance sheet. You cannot do the second before you know net income, or the third before you know the ending retained earnings.
  • Dividends are subtracted in the statement of retained earnings — not in the income statement. If you subtract them from income you get 3,195 instead of 3,395 — and this is a ready-made trap number in the choices. And the owners' investment (30,000) is not income either.
  • The number that moves to the balance sheet is the ending retained earnings balance, 3,195 — not net income 3,395.
  • Unearned consulting revenue (3,000) goes to the balance sheet as a liability — not to the income statement.
  • Accounts receivable has a balance of 0 in the trial balance, so it does not appear in the balance sheet's assets. Do not look for it.
  • Account form = left and right, report form = top and bottom, and both are acceptable. A choice that says “only one is allowed” is wrong.
  • P&L / earnings statement / statement of operations = the income statement. Statement of financial position = the balance sheet. Do not mix them up.

Debt Ratio
How much of the company's assets is financed by debt?
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Debt ratio = Total liabilities ÷ Total assets. The bigger it is = the more the company is financed by debt = higher financial leverage = higher risk.
🧠
Your house, mostly a bank loan: miss one installment and you are in trouble — the bigger the bank's share, the bigger your risk.
EXPLANATION — FINANCING AND FINANCIAL LEVERAGE

It is important to assess the risk that a company fails to pay its debts. And companies finance their assets in only two ways: liabilities or equity.

  • A company that finances a relatively large portion of its assets with liabilities is said to have higher financial leverage.
  • Why does higher leverage mean higher risk? Because the company must repay its liabilities, and they often need regular interest payments — equity financing does not need this.
  • One measure of the risk associated with liabilities is the debt ratio — Exhibit 2.17.
Debt ratio = Total liabilities ÷ Total assets
Exhibit 2.17 — Debt Ratio

So the ratio tells you: what share of the assets comes from debt. The top of the fraction is all the liabilities, and the bottom is all the assets.

The book's analysis: since Costco's ratio is higher than Walmart's every year, this suggests a slightly higher-than-average risk from financial leverage.

So, is financial leverage good or bad? The book's answer: if Costco makes more money with this debt than it pays the lenders, then it is borrowing money to make more money — and that is success. But using debt can turn unprofitable quickly if the return from that money drops below the rate it pays the lenders.

DECISION MAKER — The investor and Converse

The situation: you are thinking about buying stock in Converse. You computed its debt ratio for 2019, 2020 and 2021 as 0.35, 0.74 and 0.94, respectively. The industry average is 0.40. Is Converse a low-risk investment? And has the risk changed?

The book's answer:

  • The ratio suggests that Converse's stock is riskier than normal, and that this risk is rising (0.35 → 0.74 → 0.94).
  • The industry average of 0.40 supports this conclusion, and the book says the 2021 ratio (0.94) is twice the industry norm.
  • A debt ratio that is approaching 1.0 indicates little to no equity.
EXAM BOX

It is important to assess a company’s risk of failing to pay its debts. Companies finance their assets with either liabilities or equity. A company that finances a relatively large portion of its assets with liabilities is said to have higher financial leverage. Higher financial leverage means greater risk because liabilities must be repaid and often require regular interest payments (equity financing does not). One measure of the risk associated with liabilities is the debt ratio, as defined in Exhibit 2.17.

Debt ratio = Total liabilities / Total assets

If Costco is making more money with this debt than it is paying the lenders, then it is successfully borrowing money to make more money. A company’s use of debt can turn unprofitable quickly if its return from that money drops below the rate it is paying lenders.

Also, a debt ratio approaching 1.0 indicates little to no equity.

TRAP
  • The top is Total liabilities and the bottom is Total assets. The choices give you liabilities ÷ equity, equity ÷ assets, or the ratio upside down (assets ÷ liabilities) — all wrong.
  • A higher ratio = higher financial leverage = higher risk. Not “better” and not “safer”.
  • Leverage is not always bad: if the return from the debt is bigger than what you pay the lenders = you borrow to earn more. And if the return drops below the lenders' rate → the debt turns unprofitable quickly.
  • The ratio alone is not enough — compare it with a competitor or with the industry average (Costco against Walmart, and Converse against 0.40).
  • Reading Exhibit 2.18: Costco's ratio went down from 0.70 (2 years ago) to 0.66 (current year), and Walmart's went up from 0.59 to 0.64. The first column = the current year — do not read it backwards.
  • Close to 1.0 = almost all the assets are financed by debt = little to no equity.

1. A trial balance shows total debits of $48,600 and total credits of $49,400. Which error could explain this difference?
  1. An $800 cash purchase of supplies was never journalized.
  2. A $400 payment of rent was debited to Salaries Expense instead of Rent Expense.
  3. A $400 debit balance in Prepaid Insurance was listed in the Credit column of the trial balance.
  4. An $800 collection from a customer was credited to Revenue instead of Accounts Receivable.
Answer

C. The difference is 49,400 − 48,600 = 800 = twice 400 — and this is the clue of step 3: a debit balance was written in the credit column. When the 400 was taken out of the debits and put into the credits, debits went down by 400 and credits went up by 400 → a difference of 800. The trap in A: an entry that was never recorded leaves debits and credits short by the same amount, so the trial balance still balances. B and D are a correct amount in a wrong account — the error the trial balance cannot catch at all; they tempt you because they have 800 or 400 in them.

2. Using FastForward's December 31, 2021 balance sheet in Exhibit 2.16 (total liabilities $9,200; total equity $33,195; total assets $42,395), the debt ratio is closest to:
  1. 0.22
  2. 0.28
  3. 0.78
  4. 4.61
Answer

A. 9,200 ÷ 42,395 = 0.217 ≈ 0.22. The trap in B: you divided liabilities by equity (9,200 ÷ 33,195 = 0.28) instead of assets. C = equity ÷ assets (0.78) — that is the owners' share, not the debt. And D = the ratio upside down (42,395 ÷ 9,200 = 4.61). The rule: all the liabilities on top, and all the assets at the bottom.

3. Accel's trial balance (Need-To-Know 2-4) shows Retained earnings, Dec. 31, prior year $26,000; Services revenue $41,000; Rent expense $7,000; Wages expense $21,000; and Dividends $12,500. Accel's retained earnings at December 31 of the current year is:
  1. $39,000
  2. $13,000
  3. $500
  4. $26,500
Answer

D. First, net income: 41,000 − 7,000 − 21,000 = 13,000. Then the statement of retained earnings: 26,000 + 13,000 − 12,500 = 26,500. The trap in A: you forgot to subtract the dividends (39,000). B = net income only — you forgot the beginning balance and the dividends. And C = net income minus dividends without the beginning balance of 26,000 (500).

4. A credit to Unearned Revenue was incorrectly posted to the Revenue ledger account. Which statement is correct?
  1. The trial balance will not balance.
  2. The ledger still balances, but liabilities are understated and equity and income are overstated.
  3. The ledger still balances, but liabilities are overstated and income is understated.
  4. The ledger still balances, and the financial statements are not affected.
Answer

B. This is the book's example word for word: the credit was recorded with its correct amount but in the wrong account, so the ledger balances. Unearned Revenue (a liability) did not go up → liabilities are understated. Revenue went up when it should not → income and equity are overstated. The trap in A: you think every error breaks the balance. C flips the directions. And D is a trap: a balanced ledger does not mean the statements are correct.

5. Which of the following statements is correct?
  1. A trial balance is a financial statement prepared for a period of time.
  2. Dividends are reported as an expense on the income statement.
  3. A balance sheet reports financial position at a point in time, while the income statement reports performance over a period of time.
  4. Only the account form of the balance sheet is acceptable.
Answer

C. The balance sheet is at a point in time, and the income statement is over a period of time. The trap in A: a trial balance is not a financial statement — it is a tool to check that debits equal credits, and it is at a point in time. B: dividends are not part of income; they are subtracted in the statement of retained earnings. And D: the account form and the report form are both acceptable.
