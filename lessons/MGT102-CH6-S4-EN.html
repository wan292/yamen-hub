National Culture and Values
Globalization brought countries closer, but values and norms still set them apart
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
National culture = the values, norms, knowledge, beliefs, laws, and customs that unite the citizens of a country. Its first building block is values: what a society sees as good, right, desirable, or beautiful — or their opposites.
🧠
Values are a society's inner compass: they say what is “good” and “right”, and people will fight for them.
EXPLANATION — WHY ARE CULTURES STILL DIFFERENT WITH GLOBALIZATION?
  • There is evidence that countries are becoming more similar because of globalization, and that the world may become “a global village”. But the cultures of different countries still vary widely because of vital differences in their values, norms, and attitudes.
  • In lesson 2 you took the short margin definition of national culture (under the sociocultural forces). Here the book widens it: national culture includes values, norms, knowledge, beliefs, moral principles, laws, customs, and other practices — that unite the citizens of a country.
  • How does it work? (1) It shapes individual behavior: it says what behavior is appropriate and inappropriate and how you deal with others. (2) People learn it in their everyday lives by interacting with those around them. (3) This learning starts at an early age and continues throughout their lives.

EXPLANATION — WHAT MUST YOU KNOW ABOUT VALUES?
  • The basic building blocks of national culture are only two: values and norms.
  • Values give the basic underpinnings for ideas such as individual freedom, democracy, truth, justice, honesty, loyalty, social obligation, collective responsibility, gender roles, love, sex, and marriage.
  • Values are more than just abstract ideas: they carry great emotional significance — people argue, fight, and even die over values such as freedom or dignity.
  • Values are deeply embedded in society, but they are not static: they change over time, and the change is often a slow and painful process. The book's example: former communist states such as Georgia, Hungary, and Romania moved from values of state control to values of individual freedoms.
  • When countries go through a big change in their values, social turmoil often follows — as is happening today in Asia, South America, and the Middle East.
EXAM BOX

Despite evidence that countries are becoming more similar because of globalization and that the world may become “a global village,” the cultures of different countries still vary widely because of vital differences in their values, norms, and attitudes. As noted earlier, national culture includes the values, norms, knowledge, beliefs, moral principles, laws, customs, and other practices that unite the citizens of a country. National culture shapes individual behavior by specifying appropriate and inappropriate behavior and interaction with others.

The basic building blocks of national culture are values and norms. Values are beliefs about what a society considers to be good, right, desirable, or beautiful—or their opposites. Values are more than merely abstract concepts; they are invested with considerable emotional significance. Although deeply embedded in society, values are not static; they change over time, but change is often the result of a slow and painful process.

TRAP
  • Values = ideas and beliefs about what is good and right (what we believe). Norms = rules of behavior (what we do). A choice that says unwritten, informal codes of conduct is the definition of norms, not values.
  • The four words in the definition of values: good, right, desirable, or beautiful. If you see legal or profitable instead = wrong.
  • Values change, but slowly and painfully. The choice “values are fixed and never change” is wrong, and “they change quickly and easily” is wrong too.
  • Globalization did not make cultures the same: still vary widely. A choice that says “globalization made national cultures similar, so they no longer affect management” = the opposite of the book.
  • The basic building blocks are only two: values and norms. Laws and customs are parts of culture in the wider definition, not the basic building blocks.

Norms: Mores and Folkways
Breaking one kind sends you to jail; breaking the other only makes people think you have bad manners
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Norms = unwritten, informal rules of behavior that tell you how to act in a particular situation. Two kinds play a major role in national culture: mores (central — breaking them brings serious retribution, and they have often become law) and folkways (everyday routine — breaking them only makes you look “strange” or “ill-mannered”).
🧠
Theft = mores (police and punishment). Coming to a formal meeting in house clothes = folkways (strange looks, and that is it).

EXAM BOX

Norms are unwritten, informal codes of conduct that prescribe appropriate behavior in particular situations and are considered important by most members of a group or organization. They shape the behavior of people toward one another. Two types of norms play a major role in national culture: mores and folkways.

Mores are norms that are considered to be of central importance to the functioning of society and to social life. Accordingly, the violation of mores brings serious retribution. Mores include proscriptions against murder, theft, adultery, and incest. In many societies, mores have been enacted into law.

Folkways are the routine social conventions of everyday life. They concern customs and practices such as dressing appropriately for particular situations, having good social manners, eating with the correct utensils, and engaging in neighborly behavior. Although folkways define how people are expected to behave, violation of folkways is not a serious or moral matter. People who violate folkways are often thought to be eccentric or ill-mannered, but they are not usually considered immoral or wicked.

TRAP
  • They give you an example and ask which kind it is: murder / theft / adultery → mores. Dress / manners / eating utensils / neighbors → folkways. The words routine … everyday life = folkways, and the words central to the functioning of society = mores.
  • Someone who breaks folkways = eccentric or ill-mannered, not immoral or wicked. And serious retribution is for mores only.
  • Only two kinds of norms play a major role in national culture: mores and folkways. And values are not a kind of norm — they are the other building block.
  • The alcohol example in Saudi Arabia = mores (not folkways), because its punishment is prison or death. And the point the book wants: mores differ from society to society.
  • The stranger: the first time he is excused, and if he repeats it, he is censured. A choice that says “the stranger is always excused” or “he is punished from the first time” = wrong.

EXAM BOX

As a psychologist for IBM, Hofstede collected data on employee values and norms from more than 100,000 IBM employees in 64 countries. Based on his research, Hofstede developed five dimensions along which national cultures can be placed.

Individualism is a worldview that values individual freedom and self-expression and adherence to the principle that people should be judged by their individual achievements rather than by their social background. In contrast, collectivism is a worldview that values subordination of the individual to the goals of the group and adherence to the principle that people should be judged by their contribution to the group.

By power distance, Hofstede meant the degree to which societies accept the idea that inequalities in the power and well-being of their citizens are due to differences in individuals’ physical and intellectual capabilities and heritage. Societies in which inequalities are allowed to persist or grow over time have high power distance. In contrast, in societies with low power distance, large inequalities between citizens are not allowed to develop.

Advanced Western countries such as the United States, Germany, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom have relatively low power distance and high individualism. Economically poor Latin American countries such as Guatemala and Panama, and Asian countries such as Malaysia and the Philippines, have high power distance and low individualism.

TRAP
  • Individualism = you are judged by your individual achievement rather than … social background. Collectivism = subordination of the individual + you are judged by your contribution to the group. If a choice says “people are judged by their social background”, this is what individualism rejects — not the definition of collectivism.
  • Japan = collectivist, and still not communist. And the difference between Japanese and American management comes from individualism versus collectivism.
  • High power distance = society accepts inequality and lets it grow. Low = it does not let it grow (taxes and welfare). A choice that links “taxation and welfare programs” to high = upside down.
  • The two dimensions go together in the book's examples: rich Western countries = low power distance + high individualism; Guatemala, Panama, Malaysia, the Philippines = high power distance + low individualism. Do not flip them.
  • Hofstede's numbers: 100,000+ employees, 64 countries, 5 dimensions, all from IBM, and he is a psychologist. Do not mix them with GLOBE's numbers (62 countries, 17,000 managers, 9 dimensions).

EXAM BOX

Societies that have an achievement orientation value assertiveness, performance, success, competition, and results. Societies that have a nurturing orientation value the quality of life, warm personal relationships, and services and care for the weak. Japan and the United States tend to be achievement-oriented; the Netherlands, Sweden, and Denmark are more nurturing-oriented.

Societies as well as individuals differ in their tolerance for uncertainty and risk. Societies low on uncertainty avoidance (such as the United States and Hong Kong) are easygoing, value diversity, and tolerate differences in personal beliefs and actions. Societies high on uncertainty avoidance (such as Japan and France) are more rigid and skeptical about people whose behaviors or beliefs differ from the norm. In these societies, conformity to the values of the social and work groups to which a person belongs is the norm, and structured situations are preferred because they provide a sense of security.

A national culture with a long-term orientation rests on values such as thrift (saving) and persistence in achieving goals. A national culture with a short-term orientation is concerned with maintaining personal stability or happiness and living for the present.

TRAP
  • High uncertainty avoidance = society cannot tolerate ambiguity (rigid, conformity, wants structured situations). Low = easygoing and accepts differences. Be careful: the margin definition says willing to tolerate uncertainty and risk — the name says “avoidance” and the definition talks about “tolerance”, so high = less tolerance. (The class notes write it as uncomfortable with uncertainty and risk and prefers structured situations — and this describes the high pole.)
  • Japan shows up in more than one dimension: collectivist + achievement + high uncertainty avoidance. The United States: individualism + low power distance + achievement + low uncertainty avoidance + short-term. Hong Kong: low uncertainty avoidance + long-term. France: high uncertainty avoidance + short-term. Read which dimension the question asks about before you pick the country.
  • Nurturing = quality of life + warm relationships + care for the weak. Achievement = assertiveness + performance + success + competition (+ results in the main text). If you see quality of life = nurturing. (The class notes write care for others, but the book says care for the weak.)
  • Long-term = saving and persistence (Taiwan, Hong Kong). Short-term = personal stability or happiness + living for the present (they spend more). The choice “long-term focuses on personal stability” = upside down.
  • The count is exactly 5 dimensions, and the last one is long-term vs. short-term. Gender egalitarianism, future orientation, and performance orientation are not Hofstede's — they are GLOBE's.

EXAM BOX

Hofstede’s research has inspired other major international research projects, including the GLOBE Project, which extends Hofstede’s work by looking at additional cultural dimensions. Conceived in the early 1990s by Professor Robert J. House of the University of Pennsylvania, the GLOBE (Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness) Project is an ongoing international research endeavor involving more than 200 researchers who have collected data from more than 17,000 managers in 62 countries. The GLOBE Project looks at nine cultural dimensions:

Institutional collectivism: The degree to which organizational and societal practices encourage and reward collective distribution of resources and collective action. In-group collectivism: The degree to which individuals express pride, loyalty, and cohesiveness in their organizations or families.

Power distance: The extent to which the community accepts and endorses authority, unequal distribution of power, and status privileges. Uncertainty avoidance: The extent to which a society or organization uses rules, regulations, and procedures to alleviate the unpredictability of future events.

Based on the data, countries receive an average score on the nine cultural dimensions. For example, Russia scores high on power distance, Singapore scores low on humane orientation, and China scores high on gender differentiation. Managers can also use GLOBE data to cluster countries based on similar cultural values, which would give people working in foreign countries a research-based starting point on how individuals from these cultural clusters are likely to behave.

TRAP
  • Power distance and uncertainty avoidance are in both models with different definitions. If the question says according to the GLOBE Project and gives rules, regulations, and procedures = GLOBE's uncertainty avoidance; if it gives accepts and endorses authority … status privileges = GLOBE's power distance.
  • Institutional collectivism = collective distribution of resources and collective action (practices of the organization and society). In-group collectivism = pride, loyalty, and cohesiveness in your organization or family. The word families or pride, loyalty = in-group.
  • Humane orientation (GLOBE) = fair, altruistic, generous, caring, kind. Do not mix it with Hofstede's nurturing orientation (quality of life and care for the weak).
  • Future orientation (planning, investing, delaying gratification) = GLOBE; long-term orientation (saving and persistence) = Hofstede. And performance orientation (GLOBE) is not the same as achievement orientation (Hofstede).
  • Assertiveness in GLOBE = confrontational and aggressive — a separate dimension. The same word appears inside Hofstede's definition of achievement orientation, so do not be fooled.
  • The numbers: 9 dimensions, 62 countries, 17,000+ managers, 200+ researchers, Robert J. House, early 1990s. And GLOBE extends Hofstede's work — it does not cancel it or oppose it.

National Culture and Global Management
What works in your country can backfire in another — fit your management to the culture
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
A management practice that is effective in one country might be troublesome in another — so the manager must tailor it to the local culture, respect its values and norms, and benefit from a culturally diverse management team.
🧠
A U.S. manager plans a packed workday for his Saudi guests… on a Friday. A perfect plan on paper, a cultural mistake in real life.
EXPLANATION — THE STORY OF GE AND TUNGSRAM (MEMORIZE THE ORDER)
  1. General Electric bought Tungsram — a Hungarian lighting products company — for $150 million.
  2. Why was GE attracted to it? Three reasons: it was seen as one of Hungary's best companies, Hungary's low wage rates, and the chance to use it as a base to export lighting products to Western Europe.
  3. GE transferred some of its best managers there and hoped it would soon become a leader in Europe — but many problems arose.
  4. Major misunderstandings: the Americans complained that the Hungarians were lazy, and the Hungarians thought the Americans were pushy.
  5. The Americans wanted strong sales and marketing functions that would pamper customers — but in the former command economy, sales and marketing were not needed at all.
  6. The Hungarians expected Western-style wages — and GE had gone there in the first place for the low wages.
  7. Losses mounted, and GE managers admitted that — because of differences in basic attitudes between countries — they had underestimated the difficulties of turning the company around.
  8. The end: the problems were eventually solved, and the higher efficiency made GE a major player in the European lighting market for more than a decade, until it sold its overseas lighting division in 2018.
EXPLANATION — TAILOR YOUR PRACTICES AND RESPECT VALUES AND NORMS
  • Management practices often must be tailored to the cultural context in which the organization works. What works in the U.S. might not work in Japan, Hungary, or Mexico.
  • Example: U.S.-style pay-for-performance systems that focus on the performance of the individual might not work well in Japan, because the value that gets the focus there is individual performance in pursuit of group goals — link it to individualism vs. collectivism in the third concept.
  • A manager dealing with people from another country must be sensitive to their value systems and norms and behave accordingly. The book's example: Friday is the Islamic Sabbath, so it would be impolite and inappropriate for a U.S. manager to schedule a busy day of activities for Saudi managers on a Friday.
  • (From the end-of-chapter exercises — Managing Ethically: Home Depot entered China in 2006 and left after 6 years, because the “do it yourself” (DIY) idea did not work: labor is cheap, so people hire someone to do the work, and most housing is apartments and towers.)
EXPLANATION — THE CULTURALLY DIVERSE TEAM AND THE IBM EXAMPLE
  • A culturally diverse management team can be a source of strength for an organization in the global marketplace. Compared to culturally homogeneous teams, organizations with managers from many cultures understand better how national cultures differ, and they tailor their management systems and behaviors to these differences.
  • An advantage many Western companies have over their Japanese competitors: a greater willingness to create global teams of employees from different countries, who share their cultural experiences and knowledge to give service customized to the needs of companies in different countries.

The IBM example in order:

  1. Technology services = more than half of IBM's yearly revenues, so it looks for ways to use its talented employees to lower costs and offer unique, specialized services its competitors cannot.
  2. In the 2000s it created competency centers around the world, staffed by employees who share the same technology skill, and most of IBM's employees work in these centers, in the countries where IBM has the most clients and does the most business.
  3. The challenge: how to use these experts efficiently? The solution: sophisticated software that forms self-managed teams with the best mix of skills for a specific client's problem.
  4. Programmers analyze the skills and experience of 80,000 global employees and enter them into the program → then they analyze and code the client's problem → the program matches them and gives a “best fit” list → a senior manager at IBM narrows the list and decides the final team.
  5. Members assemble from wherever they are in the world as fast as possible and work on the problem. So IBM has an ever-changing set of global teams, and each employee is placed in their “most highly valued use” — the team where their skills best increase efficiency and effectiveness.
EXAM BOX

Differences among national cultures have important implications for managers. First, because of cultural differences, management practices that are effective in one country might be troublesome in another.

Often management practices must be tailored to suit the cultural contexts within which an organization operates. An approach effective in the United States might not work in Japan, Hungary, or Mexico because of differences in national culture. For example, U.S.-style pay-for-performance systems that emphasize the performance of individuals might not work well in Japan, where individual performance in pursuit of group goals is the value that receives emphasis.

Managers doing business with people from another country must be sensitive to the value systems and norms of that country and behave accordingly. For example, Friday is the Islamic Sabbath. Thus, it would be impolite and inappropriate for a U.S. manager to schedule a busy day of activities for Saudi Arabian managers on a Friday.

A culturally diverse management team can be a source of strength for an organization participating in the global marketplace.

TRAP
  • The book says a practice effective in one country might cause problems in another — so the solution is to tailor it, not to move it as it is. The choice “successful management practices work in all countries” = wrong.
  • GE/Tungsram: the Americans said the Hungarians were lazy, and the Hungarians said the Americans were pushy — do not flip them. The price was $150 million, and the story ended in success (a major player for more than a decade until 2018) — not a final failure.
  • Individual pay-for-performance might not work in Japan because it is collectivist — not because it is nurturing (Japan is achievement-oriented in Hofstede).
  • The Friday example is about sensitivity to the values and norms of the other country — and it is the Islamic Sabbath.
  • A culturally diverse team = a source of strength, not a source of problems. And the advantage Western companies have over Japanese ones = greater willingness to form global teams — not the other way around.
  • IBM: competency centers = employees with the same technology skill; the software gives a best fit list, but a senior manager decides the team; the teams are self-managed; and the number is 80,000 employees.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 1, go back to the mores against folkways table in the second concept; if you miss question 3 or 4, go back to the Hofstede against GLOBE table in the fifth concept.

1. During a business trip abroad, a manager dresses too casually for a formal dinner and eats with the wrong utensils. His hosts think he is ill-mannered, but nobody considers him immoral, and they excuse him because he is a stranger. The behaviors he violated are best described as:
  1. values
  2. mores
  3. folkways
  4. laws
Answer

C. Folkways = the routine social conventions of everyday life, and dressing appropriately and eating with the correct utensils are the book's own examples. The person who breaks them is seen as eccentric or ill-mannered but not immoral or wicked, and a stranger is excused the first time. The trap in B: mores = central norms whose violation brings serious retribution (murder, theft). A: values = ideas about what is good and right, not rules of behavior. And D: it is mores, not folkways, that become laws.

2. In Country X, professionally successful workers amass wealth and pass it on to their children, and the gap between rich and poor keeps growing over time. According to Hofstede, Country X is best described as having:
  1. low power distance
  2. high power distance
  3. high uncertainty avoidance
  4. a nurturing orientation
Answer

B. The book says: Societies in which inequalities are allowed to persist or grow over time have high power distance, and successful people there amass wealth and pass it on to their children, so the gap grows. The trap in A: in low power distance, the government uses taxation and welfare programs so that a big gap does not form — the opposite of this scenario. C is about tolerating ambiguity and risk, not wealth. And D: nurturing = quality of life and care for the weak — far from a society that lets the poor fall behind.

3. Which of the following is NOT one of the five dimensions of national culture in Hofstede's model?
  1. Gender egalitarianism
  2. Uncertainty avoidance
  3. Achievement versus nurturing orientation
  4. Long-term versus short-term orientation
Answer

A. Gender egalitarianism is one of GLOBE's nine dimensions, not Hofstede's. Hofstede's five are: individualism vs. collectivism, power distance, achievement vs. nurturing, uncertainty avoidance, and long-term vs. short-term. So B, C, and D are all real Hofstede dimensions — and the trap here is that uncertainty avoidance is in both models, so you might think it belongs to GLOBE only. The question uses NOT, so do not pick the first dimension you recognize.

4. According to the GLOBE Project, the degree to which individuals express pride, loyalty, and cohesiveness in their organizations or families is called:
  1. institutional collectivism
  2. humane orientation
  3. collectivism
  4. in-group collectivism
Answer

D. This is the definition of in-group collectivism word for word — the key words are pride, loyalty, cohesiveness and families. The trap in A: institutional collectivism = collective distribution of resources and collective action at the level of organizational and societal practices. B: humane orientation = fair, altruistic, generous, and kind. And C: collectivism alone is one pole of a Hofstede dimension (the individual under the group's goals), and the question said according to the GLOBE Project.

5. A U.S. company acquires a factory in Japan and introduces a U.S.-style pay-for-performance system that rewards each worker only for individual results. The system works poorly. Based on the chapter, the best explanation is that:
  1. Japan has a nurturing orientation, so workers do not care about performance
  2. Japanese culture values collectivism, so individual performance in pursuit of group goals is what receives emphasis
  3. Japan is low on uncertainty avoidance, so workers dislike structured pay systems
  4. Japan has a short-term orientation, so workers prefer spending to saving
Answer

B. The book says U.S. pay-for-performance systems that focus on individuals might not work well in Japan, where individual performance in pursuit of group goals is the value that receives emphasis — because Japan is collectivist. The trap in A: in Hofstede, Japan is achievement-oriented, not nurturing. C is upside down: Japan is high on uncertainty avoidance. And D: short-term in the book = the United States and France, not Japan — and it has nothing to do with the pay system anyway.
