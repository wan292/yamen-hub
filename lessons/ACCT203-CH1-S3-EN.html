Chapter 1 — Business Transactions and the Accounting Equation

ACCT 203 · Chapter 1 · Section 3 of 4: Business Transactions and Accounting
Objective A1: Define and interpret the accounting equation and each of its components.

This is the most important lesson in the course

Everything after it — journal entries, the ledger, the trial balance, the financial statements — is built on one equation. If you understand it now, the rest of the chapter is easy.

Assets · Liabilities · Equity
The three components of the equation
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Accounting shows only two things: what the company owns, and who has a claim on it. That is all.
🧠
Picture a 1M house: 600k bank loan, 400k your cash. House = asset, loan = liability, your cash = equity.
EXPLANATION

Accounting shows two sides of a company. One side is the resources the company owns. The other side is the claims on those resources.

Claims are of two kinds. Non-owner claims are liabilities. Owner claims are equity. Together, the two are the source of the money that bought the assets.

ComponentDefinitionExamples from the book
Assets Resources a company owns or controls. They are expected to yield future benefits. cash · supplies · equipment · land · accounts receivable
Liabilities Creditors' claims on assets. They are company obligations to provide assets, products, or services to others. wages payable · accounts payable · notes (loans) payable · taxes payable
Equity The owner's claim on assets. It equals assets minus liabilities. also called net assets or residual equity

Two words you must know:

  • Receivable = an asset that promises a future inflow. A company that gives a service or a product on credit gets an account receivable from the customer.
  • Payable = a liability that promises a future outflow.

Note from the book: the phrases on credit and on account both mean that the cash is received or paid at a later date.

EXAM BOX

Assets are resources a company owns or controls. These resources are expected to yield future benefits. Liabilities are creditors' claims on assets; these claims are company obligations to provide assets, products, or services to others. Equity is the owner's claim on assets and is equal to assets minus liabilities. Equity is also called net assets or residual equity.

TRAP
  • Assets do not mean "owns" only. The book says owns or controls. The word controls shows up in the answer choices.
  • A receivable is an asset (money coming in). A payable is a liability (money going out). Mixing the two is the most common mistake in this section.
  • Equity = assets minus liabilities. Not plus.
⚖️
The Accounting Equation
Memorize it now — do not continue before you know it by heart
🧠
Picture a scale: assets on one pan, liabilities plus equity on the other. Whenever you look, both pans are level.
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
EXPLANATION

This relation applies to all transactions and events. It applies to all companies and organizations. And it applies at all points in time. There is no exception.

You can also rearrange it. Example from the book: Assets − Liabilities = Equity

EXPLANATION — THE EXPANDED EQUATION

We can break equity into four parts. This gives us the expanded accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings
= Liabilities + Common Stock − Dividends + Revenues − Expenses

Contributed capital is the money the owners contributed to the company. Retained earnings is the income the company retained instead of paying it out.

Equity increases from owner investments, also called stock issuances, and from revenues. Equity decreases from dividends and from expenses.

PartSignDefinition
Common Stock
part of contributed capital		 + Inflows of cash and net assets from shareholders in exchange for stock. Stock reflects ownership in the company.
Dividends
from retained earnings		 − Outflows of cash and other assets to shareholders. They decrease equity.
Revenues + They increase equity (through net income) from selling products and services to customers. Examples: product sales, consulting services, facility rentals, commissions.
Expenses − They decrease equity (through net income) from the costs of providing products and services. Examples: employee time, supplies used, advertising, utilities, insurance.
EXAM BOX

The accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. It applies to all transactions and events, to all companies and organizations, and to all points in time. The expanded accounting equation is:

Assets = Liabilities + Common Stock − Dividends + Revenues − Expenses

Equity increases from owner investments (stock issuances) and from revenues; it decreases from dividends and from expenses.

TRAP
  • The signs are the question. Common Stock and Revenues carry a plus. Dividends and Expenses carry a minus. Any choice that reverses one sign is wrong.
  • Dividends are not an expense. This is the biggest trap in the chapter. A dividend is a distribution to the owners. It decreases equity, but it is not an expense and it never enters net income.
  • Contributed Capital = Common Stock. Retained Earnings = Revenues − Expenses − Dividends.
  • The equation holds at every point in time. Any choice saying it holds only at the end of the year is wrong.

Transactions and Events
What actually moves the equation
🧠
Picture a handshake between two shops: external. A worker opening his own shop's pens: internal. A fire, no handshake: event.
EXPLANATION
TypeDefinitionExample from the book
External transactions An exchange of value between two entities. They always cause a change in the accounting equation. Apple sells an AppleCare Protection Plan
Internal transactions An exchange inside the same entity. It may or may not affect the equation. Target uses its own supplies — recorded as an expense when used
Events Happenings that affect the equation and can be measured reliably. market value changes in some assets and liabilities · a fire that damages assets and creates losses

The book uses the equation to analyze 11 transactions and events for FastForward. FastForward is a new consulting company, so it is a service company. The analysis covers its first month of operations.

The golden rule: after every transaction and every event, assets always equal liabilities plus equity. If the two sides are not equal, your analysis has a mistake.

EXAMPLE 1 — INVESTMENT BY OWNER

On December 1, Chas Taylor started a consulting company called FastForward. He set it up as a corporation. He invested $30,000 cash in the new company in return for its common stock. He put the cash in a bank account in the company's name.

After the transaction: cash (an asset) = $30,000, and equity = $30,000. The increase came from the owner's investment, a stock issuance. It is reported under Common Stock.

Transaction 1
Assets=Liabilities+Equity
Cash=+Common Stock
(1)+ $30,000= + $30,000Owner investment

Notice: both sides went up by the same amount, so the equation remains balanced — 30,000 = 0 + 30,000.

TRAP
  • External = between two entities. Internal = inside one entity.
  • Internal transactions may not affect the equation. The book says may or may not affect. Any choice that is sure about one side is wrong.
  • An event must be reliably measurable before the company can record it.

Test yourself

Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 2 or 3, go back to the table above.

1. A company has assets of $180,000 and liabilities of $75,000. Its equity is:
  1. $255,000
  2. $105,000
  3. $75,000
  4. Cannot be determined
Answer

B. Equity = Assets − Liabilities = 180,000 − 75,000 = 105,000. Choice A adds instead of subtracting, and that is the trap. Choice D says the answer cannot be determined, but the equation always gives you the third number when you have the other two.

2. Which of the following decreases equity?
  1. Revenues
  2. Common stock issued to shareholders
  3. Dividends
  4. Accounts receivable collected
Answer

C. In the expanded equation, Common Stock and Revenues carry a plus, while Dividends and Expenses carry a minus. Choice D is a receivable that was collected. That only changes the form of the assets, from a receivable into cash, so equity does not move at all.

3. Which statement about dividends is correct?
  1. Dividends are an expense and reduce net income
  2. Dividends reduce equity but are not an expense
  3. Dividends increase equity because they are paid to owners
  4. Dividends have no effect on the accounting equation
Answer

B. Dividends decrease equity, but they are not an expense and they do not enter the calculation of net income. Choice A is the most famous trap in the whole chapter.

4. Target uses some of its own supplies, which are then reported as expenses. This is best described as:
  1. An external transaction
  2. An internal transaction
  3. An event
  4. Not a transaction at all
Answer

B. The exchange happens inside the same entity, so there is no second party. This is the book's own example of an internal transaction.

5. An owner invests $50,000 cash in exchange for common stock. The effect on the accounting equation is:
  1. Assets increase $50,000; liabilities increase $50,000
  2. Assets increase $50,000; equity increases $50,000
  3. Assets increase $50,000; equity decreases $50,000
  4. Equity increases $50,000; liabilities decrease $50,000
Answer

B. Cash, an asset, goes up. Common Stock, part of equity, goes up by the same amount. Owners are not creditors, so liabilities do not change. Choice A is built on exactly that confusion.
