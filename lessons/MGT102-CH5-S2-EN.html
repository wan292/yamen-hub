Chapter 5 — Managers and the Effective Management of Diversity

MGT 102 · Chapter 5 · Section 2 of 4: Managers and the Effective Management of Diversity
Objective LO5-2: Explain the central role that managers play in the effective management of diversity.
Objective LO5-3: Explain why the effective management of diversity is both an ethical and a business imperative.

This section is “the manager either builds diversity or ruins it + why it is an ethical duty and a business duty at the same time”

In the previous lesson you learned that the workforce and the environment around the organization are becoming more and more diverse (12 sources of diversity and 8 laws). OK, so who is responsible for making this diversity actually work? The book says: the manager — in every one of the ten roles you memorized in Chapter 1 (LO5-2). Then it answers a second question: why should we bother at all? The answer is two reasons together: an ethical reason (ethical imperative) built on two principles — distributive justice and procedural justice — and a business reason (business imperative): competitive advantage, saving costs, and avoiding costly lawsuits (LO5-3).

Focus from now on four things that trip up students here: (1) the difference between distributive (what is shared out, and on what basis) and procedural (how we decided), (2) mixing up entrepreneur and resource allocator in Table 5.2, (3) the numbers of Table 5.3 — who has the highest percentage and who has the lowest, (4) the number “two times the salary” and the Coca-Cola story. And take the English definitions word for word from the EXAM BOX.

EXPLANATION — WHY IS THIS HARD FOR THE MANAGER?
  • The environment becomes more diverse → so the workforce becomes more diverse → so the challenges the manager faces in managing diversity grow.
  • Each kind of diversity from lesson 1 (age, gender, race, religion…) brings its own set of issues, and the manager must understand them before he can respond to them well. Understanding them is not always simple — even well-informed managers discovered this.
  • That is why research on how different groups are treated today and on unconscious biases that might adversely affect them is vital, because it makes the manager aware of the subtle ways in which groups of employees come to be treated unfairly over time.
  • And the manager can take many steps to: (1) become sensitive to the ongoing effects of diversity in his organization, (2) take advantage of all the contributions diverse employees can make, (3) prevent unfair treatment.

EXAM BOX

In each of their managerial roles (see Chapter 1), managers can either promote the effective management of diversity or derail such efforts; thus, they are critical to this process. For example, in their interpersonal roles, managers can convey that the effective management of diversity is a valued goal and objective (figurehead role), can serve as a role model and institute policies and procedures to ensure that all organizational members are treated fairly (leader role), and can enable diverse individuals and groups to coordinate their efforts and cooperate with each other both inside the organization and at the organization's boundaries (liaison role).

Both the workforce and the organizational environment are increasingly diverse, and effectively managing this diversity is an essential component of management. In each of their managerial roles, managers can encourage the effective management of diversity, which is both an ethical and a business imperative.

TRAP
  • Entrepreneur = commits resources to new ways (innovation). Resource allocator = allocates resources to support (sharing out). Both have the word “resources” — the difference is the words new ways. This is the most famous trap in the table.
  • The three informational roles differ in direction: Monitor evaluates fairness inside, Disseminator informs employees, Spokesperson speaks to the wider community and interests diverse groups in jobs.
  • Figurehead = symbolic (conveys that diversity is a valued goal). Leader = does the work (role model + policies and procedures). The words role model go to the leader, not the figurehead.
  • Disturbance handler = fast (quick action): it corrects an unfairness that happened and stops discrimination. Negotiator = works with suppliers and labor unions — not with employees inside the company.
  • The count is 10 = 3 + 3 + 4, and the category with 4 is decisional. And the book says the manager either promotes or derails — a choice that says “some roles have nothing to do with diversity” is wrong; it is every role.

Why Managers Are Central to Diversity
People copy those with status, and small unfairness grows over time if it is left alone
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The manager has formal authority, so his influence is bigger than an ordinary employee's — when he commits to diversity, the others commit. On top of that, two factors: underrepresented groups start at a slight disadvantage, and small differences accumulate until they become big gaps.
🧠
A plane one degree off course: you feel nothing in the first hour, but after ten hours you land in another city.
EXPLANATION — AUTHORITY AND SOCIAL INFLUENCE

Because of the formal authority the manager has, his influence is usually bigger than that of rank-and-file employees. So when the manager commits to supporting diversity — as the NFL did in A Manager's Challenge at the start of the chapter — his authority, position, power, and status influence other members to make the same commitment.

Research on social influence supports this link: people are influenced and persuaded by others who have high status.

EXPLANATION — TWO OTHER FACTORS THAT MAKE THE MANAGER CENTRAL
The factorWhat the book says
1. Starting at a slight disadvantage
البداية بعيب بسيط		 Women, African Americans, Hispanics, and other underrepresented groups often start at a slight disadvantage because of how others see them in the organization — especially when they are a numerical minority. The book quotes Virginia Valian (a psychologist at Hunter College who studies gender): “In most organizations women begin at a slight disadvantage. A woman does not walk into the room with the same status as an equivalent man, because she is less likely than a man to be viewed as a serious professional.” — in other words, a woman walks into the room less likely to be seen as a “serious professional” than an equivalent man.
2. Small differences accumulate
الفروق الصغيرة تتراكم		 Research says that slight differences in treatment accumulate and become major disparities over time. The book's example: a small bias in favor of men in promotions leads over time to a big difference in the number of male and female managers. And although women and minorities are sometimes advised “don't complain” when they are treated unfairly, research by Valian and others says: differences based on irrelevant distinctions such as race, gender, and ethnicity turn into major disparities in important outcomes like promotions — if they are ignored.

The bottom line: the manager has an obligation — from an ethical side and from a business side — to prevent any disparities in treatment and outcomes caused by irrelevant distinctions such as race or ethnicity. This is the bridge to the next two concepts.

EXPLANATION — the Manager as a Person box: Stryker CEO Promotes Diversity
  • Stryker is a global medical technology company based in Kalamazoo, Michigan. Its three businesses: medical/surgical equipment (including surgical power tools), orthopedics (knee and hip implants), and neurotechnology (products for treating strokes). Its stated mission: work with its customers — hospitals and doctors — to “make healthcare better”.
  • Chair and CEO Kevin Lobo sees diversity and inclusion as part of the strategy. His view: the company should reflect the diversity of its customers and patients for two reasons: because this “is the right way to treat people” and “leads to better business results” — notice: an ethical reason + a business reason, exactly the pair in LO5-3.
  • The numbers: 35% of the workforce are women, 26% a group inclusive of multiple races and genders, 2% people with disabilities.
  • His most visible action: he doubled the number of women on the board of directors from 2 to 4. When all the seats were full, he expanded the board from 9 to 10 to appoint one of them.
  • On hiring: “If you hire from the same area over and over, you are not getting the best talent.”
  • He is a role model: he takes part himself in employee resource groups and encourages his executives to do the same.
  • Policies: the company website describes diversity and inclusion as “a business imperative that aligns with our mission and values”. Top managers have measurable goals for diversity and inclusion, and progress counts in performance assessments. There are flexible work arrangements (part-time or remote work) for family caregiving, applied equally across genders. Employee groups include the African Ancestry Network, the Veterans Association, and Allies for Equality (which supports LGBTQ employees).

EXAM BOX

Given the formal authority that managers have in organizations, they typically have more influence than rank-and-file employees. When managers commit to supporting diversity, as is the case within the National Football League as discussed in “A Manager's Challenge,” their authority and positions of power and status influence other members of an organization to make a similar commitment. Research on social influence supports such a link: People are likely to be influenced and persuaded by others who have high status.

When managers commit to diversity, their commitment legitimizes the diversity management efforts of others. In addition, resources are devoted to such efforts, and all members of an organization believe that their diversity-related efforts are supported and valued. Consistent with this reasoning, top management commitment and rewards for the support of diversity are often cited as critical ingredients in the success of diversity management initiatives.

The first factor is that women, African Americans, Hispanics, and other underrepresented groups often start out at a slight disadvantage due to how they are perceived by others in organizations, particularly in work settings where they are a numerical minority. The second factor is that research suggests that slight differences in treatment can accumulate and result in major disparities over time.

TRAP
  • The source of the manager's influence = formal authority + positions of power and status. The research that supports it is called social influence: people are influenced by high status — not “by the majority” and not “by younger people”.
  • The results of commitment are three: legitimacy for others' efforts · resources + the feeling that efforts are supported and valued · confidence that reduces prejudice coming from ignorance or stereotypes.
  • The two critical ingredients: top management commitment and rewards for supporting diversity — both together.
  • The disadvantage at the start is slight, not major, and it shows more when the group is a numerical minority. The big thing is the result after it accumulates: major disparities over time.
  • The advice “don't complain” is what the research disagrees with. A choice that says “small differences disappear over time if we ignore them” is the opposite of the book.
  • Stryker: Lobo's two reasons (ethical right way to treat people + business better business results); women on the board 2 → 4, and the board 9 → 10; and the percentages 35% / 26% / 2%.

Distributive Justice
What is shared out and on what basis: by your contribution, not by something you cannot control
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Managing diversity is an ethical imperative guided by two principles: distributive justice and procedural justice. The first = pay, promotions, and organizational resources are shared out by contribution, not by personal characteristics you have no control over.

EXPLANATION — DOES IT REALLY EXIST IN AMERICAN COMPANIES?
  • The book's answer: things are getting better. 50 years ago, overt discrimination against diverse groups was common; today organizations are inching closer to the ideal of distributive justice.
  • But the statistics say most managers need a proactive approach to achieve it.
  • The book's example: across occupations, women consistently earn less than men (Table 5.3), according to U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data. And even in occupations dominated by women — such as sales and office — men earn more.

EXAM BOX

Effectively managing diversity not only makes good business sense (which is discussed in the next section) but also is an ethical imperative in U.S. society. Two moral principles guide managers in their efforts to meet this imperative: distributive justice and procedural justice.

The principle of distributive justice dictates fair distribution of pay, promotions, job titles, interesting job assignments, office space, and other organizational resources among members of an organization. These outcomes should be distributed according to the meaningful contributions that individuals have made to the organization (such as time, effort, education, skills, abilities, and performance levels) and not irrelevant personal characteristics over which individuals have no control (such as gender, race, or age). Managers have an obligation to ensure that distributive justice exists in their organizations. This does not mean that all members of an organization receive identical or similar outcomes; rather, it means that members who receive more favorable outcomes than others have made substantially higher or more significant contributions to the organization.

For example, across occupations, women consistently earn less than men (see Table 5.3), according to data collected by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Even in occupations dominated by women, such as sales and office occupations, men tend to earn more than women.

TRAP
  • Distributive justice is not absolute equality. A choice that says “all members receive identical or similar outcomes” is wrong; the right one: whoever gets more made a substantially higher or more significant contribution.
  • What is distributed is not only money: job titles, interesting job assignments, and office space are in it too.
  • The basis = contributions (time, effort, education, skills, abilities, performance). Gender, race, and age = characteristics the person cannot control. Notice that education is an accepted contribution, not a forbidden personal characteristic.
  • The situation today: things are getting better and inching closer — not “already achieved” and not “getting worse”; what is needed is a proactive approach.
  • The sales and office example: an occupation dominated by women where men still earn more. From the table: highest percentage Service 84, lowest Natural resources… 71.
  • Only two principles for the ethical imperative (distributive and procedural) — do not mix them with the four ethical rules of Chapter 4 (utilitarian, moral rights, justice, practical).

Procedural Justice
Not who got what — but how we decided who gets what
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Procedural justice = use fair procedures to decide how outcomes are distributed. Distributive asks “who got what?”, and procedural asks “how did we decide?”.

EXAM BOX

The principle of procedural justice requires that managers use fair procedures to determine how to distribute outcomes to organizational members. This principle applies to typical procedures such as appraising employees' performance, deciding who should receive a raise or a promotion, and deciding whom to lay off when an organization is forced to downsize.

Procedural justice exists, for example, when managers (1) carefully appraise an employee's performance; (2) take into account any environmental obstacles to high performance beyond the employee's control, such as lack of supplies, machine breakdowns, or dwindling customer demand for a product; and (3) ignore irrelevant personal characteristics such as the employee's age or ethnicity. Like distributive justice, procedural justice is necessary not only to ensure ethical conduct but also to avoid costly lawsuits.

TRAP
  • The word procedures, or “the way of appraising”, or “how we decided who is laid off” → procedural. The words distribution of pay/promotions, or “the outcome itself” → distributive.
  • The three examples of environmental obstacles (lack of supplies · machine breakdowns · dwindling customer demand) belong to procedural justice — not distributive.
  • The manager does three things in order: appraise carefully → count the obstacles beyond the employee's control → ignore irrelevant characteristics. A choice that says “gives everyone the same outcome” is not one of them.
  • In the third example the book names age and ethnicity; in distributive justice it names gender, race, and age.
  • Both are necessary for ethics and to avoid lawsuits — a choice that says “procedural justice is only ethical and has nothing to do with the law” is wrong.

Managing Diversity Makes Good Business Sense
Diversity is a source of competitive advantage, and managing it badly costs money and reputation
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Diverse employees can be a source of competitive advantage: better decisions, understanding diverse customers, keeping employees (and saving the cost of replacing them), suppliers who support diversity, and avoiding costly lawsuits.
🧠
Coca-Cola paid $192 million in one lawsuit, and twenty years later it found itself behind the point where it started.
EXPLANATION — THE BUSINESS REASONS (THE BOOK GIVES THEM AS PARAGRAPHS; WE PUT THEM IN SIX POINTS FOR YOU)

The main sentence: diverse employees can be a source of competitive advantage, helping the organization give customers better goods and services.

EXPLANATION — RETENTION AND TURNOVER
  • With the current legal environment, more organizations care about diversity in hiring. But after they are hired, if diverse employees feel they are treated unfairly, they will look for opportunities somewhere else. That is why: recruiting a diverse workforce has to be followed with ongoing effective management of diversity to keep valued members.
  • If diversity is not managed well and turnover goes up among the groups that are not treated fairly, profitability suffers in several ways: (1) their future contributions are lost when they quit, (2) the organization bears the costs of hiring replacements, and (3) this hiring may become a challenge if the organization gets a reputation that its management does not consider minorities and underrepresented groups important members.
  • The number: according to a recent report, hiring a replacement costs the employer on average two times the employee's salary if the employee leaves.
  • On top of that, large indirect costs (3): knowledge lost when an experienced worker leaves, time spent finding a replacement, and time the new employee needs to get up to speed.
EXPLANATION — THE COCA-COLA STORY (MEMORIZE THE ORDER)
  1. In 2000 the company settled a class-action suit brought by African American employees at a cost of $192 million.
  2. The damage is bigger than the money paid to the injured parties: such lawsuits can tarnish a company's image.
  3. One positive outcome: the company recognized it needs to commit additional resources to diversity management initiatives.
  4. More than 20 years later: the company redoubled its efforts because its progress had been thwarted by a “sense of complacency”. Only 8% of its executives are Black, and Black employees are about 15% of U.S. salaried staff — 5 percentage points lower than when the lawsuit was filed in 2000.
  5. The new steps: a 10-year internal and external recruiting program for people of color with an emphasis on Black executives, + training leaders to recognize bias, + a new HR position focused on diversity in executive recruitment, + a commitment to recruit talent not only for current job openings but also for future career paths.

End of the section: now it is clear that managing diversity is a necessity on both ethical and business grounds. And the next question: why does diversity give managers — and all of us — so many challenges? The answer is in lesson 3: perception.

EXAM BOX

Diverse organizational members can be a source of competitive advantage, helping an organization provide customers with better goods and services. The variety of points of view and approaches to problems and opportunities that diverse employees provide can improve managerial decision making.

Thus, recruiting a diverse workforce has to be followed with ongoing effective management of diversity to retain valued organizational members. According to a recent report, it costs employers an average of two times the amount of that employee's salary to hire a replacement if the employee leaves the organization.

More and more, managers and organizations concerned about diversity are insisting that their suppliers also support diversity. Finally, from both business and ethical perspectives, effective management of diversity is necessary to avoid costly lawsuits.

By now it should be clear that effectively managing diversity is a necessity on both ethical and business grounds.

TRAP
  • The cost of a replacement = two times the employee's salary — not half the salary and not the same salary. On top of it, indirect costs (lost knowledge · search time · the new employee's time).
  • Hiring is the start, not the end: recruiting … has to be followed with ongoing effective management of diversity. A choice that says “once you hire a diverse workforce, no more effort is needed” is wrong.
  • Link the example to the reason: IKEA = diverse customers (products by city), automakers = employees attuned to market segments (women in design), frozen food = better decisions and more ideas.
  • Coca-Cola: the year 2000, $192 million, a class-action suit by African American employees. The irony: after 20 years the percentage went down 5 points (about 15%), and the reason the company gave is a sense of complacency.
  • The damage of lawsuits is not only money — tarnish a company's image. And avoiding them is necessary for business and ethics together.
  • Remember from lesson 1: the book says having a diverse workforce alone may not guarantee higher performance — the advantage comes from managing it well.

  4. Liaison
Answer

B. The Entrepreneur example in Table 5.2 says, word for word: Commits resources to develop new ways to effectively manage diversity and eliminate biases and discrimination — the key word is new ways. The trap in A: resource allocator also has “resources”, but it allocates resources to support, it does not develop something new. C is a trap too: disturbance handler = quick action that corrects an unfairness and stops discrimination. And D: liaison = lets diverse people coordinate and cooperate, and its category is interpersonal, not decisional.

2. Research by Virginia Valian and others suggests that if slight differences in how people are treated based on irrelevant distinctions such as gender or race are ignored, they will most likely:
  1. disappear on their own over time
  2. accumulate and result in major disparities over time in outcomes such as promotions
  3. affect employees' feelings but not their promotions
  4. matter only in organizations where women are the majority
Answer

B. The book says: slight differences in treatment can accumulate and result in major disparities over time, and its example is a small bias in favor of men in promotions that becomes a big difference in the number of managers. The trap in A: this is exactly the “don't complain, it will go away” idea that the research disagrees with. C is a trap: the book names promotions as an outcome that is affected. And D is the opposite of the book: the slight disadvantage shows more when the group is a numerical minority.

3. When deciding whom to lay off during a downsizing, a manager carefully appraises each employee's performance, takes into account machine breakdowns that were beyond the employees' control, and ignores the employees' age and ethnicity. This manager is following the principle of:
  1. distributive justice
  2. procedural justice
  3. the utilitarian rule
  4. social influence
Answer

B. These are the three things the book lists for procedural justice, in order: appraise carefully, take into account environmental obstacles beyond the employee's control (machine breakdowns is one of the examples), and ignore age and ethnicity — and “deciding whom to lay off when downsizing” is one of the procedures the principle applies to. The trap in A: distributive is about the outcome itself (who got what, based on contribution), and this question describes the way of deciding. C is one of the four rules in Chapter 4. And D: social influence = people are influenced by those with high status; it has nothing to do with this.

4. According to Table 5.3, in which occupation do women earn the highest percentage of men's earnings?
  1. Management, professional, and related
  2. Service
  3. Sales and office
  4. Natural resources, construction, and maintenance
Answer

B. Service = 84%, the highest percentage in the table (the smallest gap). The trap in A: the highest pay in dollars ($1,726 and $1,284), but its percentage is only 74% — the question asks about the percentage, not the amount. C = 74%, the book's example of an occupation dominated by women where men still earn more. And D = 71%, the lowest percentage, which means the biggest gap — if the question said lowest, this would be the answer.

5. Which of the following is NOT a reason the chapter gives for why effectively managing diversity makes good business sense?
  1. The variety of points of view that diverse employees provide can improve managerial decision making.
  2. Increasing retention of valued employees decreases the costs of hiring replacements.
  3. Once a diverse workforce has been recruited, no further effort to manage diversity is needed.
  4. Effective management of diversity is necessary to avoid costly lawsuits.
Answer

C. The book says the exact opposite: recruiting a diverse workforce has to be followed with ongoing effective management of diversity to retain valued organizational members — because diverse employees who feel treated unfairly will leave, and a replacement costs two times the salary. A is a correct reason (the frozen food example), B is correct (retention), and D is correct (the Coca-Cola story). The question uses NOT, so do not rush and pick the first true sentence you see.
