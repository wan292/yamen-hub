Chapter 4 — Ethical Decision Rules and Why Behave Ethically

MGT 102 · Chapter 4 · Section 2 of 4: Ethical Decision Rules and Why Behave Ethically
Objective LO4-3: Describe four rules that can help companies and their managers act in ethical ways.
Objective LO4-4: Discuss why it is important for managers to behave ethically.

This section is “four rules to test your decision + why we should bother with ethics at all”

In the previous lesson you learned that a company has six groups of stakeholders, and each group wants something. The problem: a decision that pleases one group often hurts another. So how does a manager know that his decision is ethical? The book gives him four rules (LO4-3), and then it answers a bigger question: why should a manager be ethical in the first place (LO4-4)? The answer turns around three words: tragedy of the commons, trust, and reputation.

Focus from now: the thing that trips up students most here is mixing up the four rules — utilitarian = the greatest good for the greatest number, moral rights = protecting rights, justice = distributing fairly, practical = you can announce the decision to people and feel comfortable. The exam gives you a scenario and asks which rule it fits, so take the English definitions word for word from the EXAM BOX and memorize the key word in each one.

Rules for Ethical Decision Making
Why we need rules, and what a company gains when it is ethical
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
When a decision pleases one group and hurts another, the manager passes his decision through four rules: utilitarian, moral rights, justice, and practical — to know whether it is ethical or not.
🧠
The manager's office sits in the middle with four checkpoints around it; every decision leaving the office must pass all four.
EXPLANATION — WHY DO WE NEED RULES AT ALL?

When you look at the company through the eyes of its stakeholders (the stakeholder perspective), many ethical questions come up: what is the right way to manage the claims of all of them? Because a decision that favors one group often hurts the interests of the other groups.

The book's example: a company charges its customers high prices.

  • In the short run: high returns for shareholders and high salaries for managers.
  • In the long run: if customers move to companies that sell for less, the result is falling sales, laid-off employees, and the communities that support the high-priced company's business decline.
When the company acts ethicallyThe results of unethical behavior (four)
Stakeholders support it, for example: 1. Loss of reputation and resources
1. Banks are willing to give it new capital 2. Shareholders sell their shares
2. It attracts highly qualified job applicants 3. Skilled managers and employees leave the company
3. New customers are drawn to its products 4. Customers turn to the products of more reputable companies
The result: ethical companies grow and expand over time, and all their stakeholders benefit.

So when a manager makes his decisions, he must consider the claims of all stakeholders. He can use four ethical rules or principles to analyze the effects of his decisions on them. The book calls them useful guidelines — they help the manager decide on the right way to behave in situations where he must balance the company's self-interest and the interests of its stakeholders.

And the book's sentence that ties everything together: the right choices make resources go where they can create the most value, and if all companies choose right, all stakeholders benefit in the long run.

EXAM BOX

When companies act ethically, their stakeholders support them. For example, banks are willing to supply them with new capital, they attract highly qualified job applicants, and new customers are drawn to their products. Thus, ethical companies grow and expand over time, and all their stakeholders benefit. The results of unethical behavior are loss of reputation and resources, shareholders selling their shares, skilled managers and employees leaving the company, and customers turning to the products of more reputable companies.

When making business decisions, managers must consider the claims of all stakeholders. To help themselves and employees make ethical decisions and behave in ways that benefit their stakeholders, managers can use four ethical rules or principles to analyze the effects of their business decisions on stakeholders: the utilitarian, moral rights, justice, and practical rules (Figure 4.2). These rules are useful guidelines that help managers decide on the appropriate way to behave in situations where it is necessary to balance a company's self-interest and the interests of its stakeholders.

TRAP
  • The count is four rules. Six = the groups of stakeholders (lesson 1), four sources of ethics (lesson 3), and four approaches to social responsibility (lesson 4). The exam swaps the numbers between the lists on purpose.
  • The book's order: utilitarian, moral rights, justice, and practical. None of them is called societal, legal, or stakeholder rule — societal is a source of ethics, not a rule.
  • “Banks supply new capital”, “attracts qualified applicants”, and “new customers” are results of ethical behavior. A choice that puts them under unethical behavior is wrong. And the results of unethical behavior are four: reputation and resources · shareholders sell · skilled people leave · customers go to more reputable companies.
  • The rules are guidelines for balancing the company's self-interest and the interests of stakeholders — not laws, and not absolute rules (remember from lesson 1: no absolute or indisputable rules).

The Utilitarian and Moral Rights Rules

EXPLANATION — Utilitarian rule: how to apply it and where it gets hard

Two steps to apply it:

  1. The manager first thinks about how each possible course of action could benefit or harm each group of stakeholders.
  2. Then he chooses the course of action that gives the most benefits — or, conversely, the one that does the least harm to stakeholders.

The ethical dilemma for managers here is three questions:

  • How do you measure the benefit and harm that will happen to each group?
  • How do you evaluate the rights of different groups and the relative importance of each group when you decide?
  • Since shareholders own the company, shouldn't their claims be put above those of employees?

The book's example — global outsourcing: a manager must choose between moving production abroad (global outsourcing) to cut costs and lower prices, or continuing with high-cost production at home in the United States.

The choiceWho benefitsWho is harmed
Global outsourcingshareholders and customersemployees and their communities (major layoffs)
Production at home(not stated in the book — implied: employees keep their jobs)the company might collapse and go bankrupt → more harm to everyone

The book says: in a capitalist society like the United States, the interests of shareholders are usually put above those of employees, so production moves abroad. And this is usually regarded as the ethical choice — because the other choice might make the company go bankrupt, and then the harm would be greater for all stakeholders. That is exactly the “least harm” logic.

EXPLANATION — Moral rights rule: the six rights

Ethical decisions protect people's rights to (the book names them — six):

#The rightIn Arabic
1Freedomالحرية
2Life and safetyالحياة والسلامة
3Propertyالملكية
4Privacyالخصوصية
5Free speechحرية التعبير
6Freedom of conscienceحرية الضمير
  • The famous adage “Do unto others as you would have them do unto you” (treat people the way you want them to treat you) is what the book calls a moral rights principle — the manager uses it to decide which rights to uphold.
  • Customers must also respect the rights of the companies and the people who make the products they want to consume.
  • How to apply it: the manager compares courses of action by the effect of each one on the rights of the different stakeholders, and chooses the one that best protects and upholds the rights of everyone. The book's example: decisions that could seriously harm the safety or health of employees or customers are clearly unethical.

The ethical dilemma: a decision that protects the rights of some people often hurts the rights of others — so whom do you protect? The book's examples:

  • Is it ethical for a company to spy on employees or search them when they leave work to stop theft? In other words, is the employee's right to privacy more important than the organization's right to protect its property?
  • A coworker has personal problems, comes in late and leaves early, and you are doing his work. Do you tell your boss, knowing this will probably get him fired?
EXAM BOX

The utilitarian rule is that an ethical decision is a decision that produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people. To decide which is the most ethical course of business action, managers should first consider how different possible courses of business action would benefit or harm different stakeholders. They should then choose the course of action that provides the most benefits, or, conversely, the one that does the least harm, to stakeholders.

Under the moral rights rule, an ethical decision is one that best maintains and protects the fundamental or inalienable rights and privileges of the people affected by it. For example, ethical decisions protect people's rights to freedom, life and safety, property, privacy, free speech, and freedom of conscience. The adage “Do unto others as you would have them do unto you” is a moral rights principle that managers should use to decide which rights to uphold.

TRAP
  • Utilitarian = numbers and amounts. If the scenario has “the most people benefit” or “the least harm to stakeholders as a whole” → utilitarian.
  • Moral rights = rights. If the scenario has privacy, safety, or free speech → moral rights — even if the majority benefits from breaking the right.
  • The outsourcing example: the book says it is usually regarded as ethical — not because employees do not matter, but because the other choice might bankrupt the company and hurt everyone more. A choice that says “unethical under the utilitarian rule” is the opposite of the book.
  • “Do unto others…” = a moral rights principle — not justice and not practical.
  • The rights are six, and life and safety is one right on the list. A choice with “the right to high profits” or “the right to a bonus” is not one of them.

The Justice and Practical Rules
One shares things out fairly; the other tests the decision in front of people
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Justice rule = distribute benefits and harms fairly, with no bias. Practical rule = the ethical decision is one you have no hesitation about announcing to people outside the company, because the typical person in a society would see it as acceptable.
🧠
Justice: you cut the cake equally among people who worked equally. Practical: imagine your decision trending online while your family reads it — comfortable?
EXPLANATION — THE TWO DEFINITIONS
TermThe book's definition (memorize it word for word)What it means
Justice rule
قاعدة العدالة		 An ethical decision distributes benefits and harms among people and groups in a fair, equitable, or impartial way. the ethical decision distributes benefits and harms among people and groups in a way that is fair, equitable, or impartial.
Practical rule
القاعدة العملية		 An ethical decision is one that a manager has no reluctance about communicating to people outside the company because the typical person in a society would think it is acceptable. the ethical decision is one the manager has no reluctance at all about telling people outside the company, because the typical person in a society would see it as acceptable.
EXPLANATION — Justice rule
  • How to apply it: the manager compares the alternatives by how far each one distributes outcomes to stakeholders fairly or equitably.
  • The book's example: employees who are similar in their level of skill, performance, or responsibility should get similar pay, and the distribution of outcomes should not be based on differences such as gender, race, or religion.
  • The ethical dilemma: how do you decide on fair rules and procedures for distributing outcomes? A manager must not give bigger pay raises to people he likes than to people he does not like, and must not bend the rules to help his favorites.
  • The other side (they love it on the exam): if employees want their manager to be fair to them, they must also be fair to their company — by working hard and being loyal. The same goes for customers: they must be fair to the company if they expect it to be fair to them.
EXPLANATION — Practical rule: why was a fourth rule added?

The book says: each of the first three rules gives you a different and complementary way to judge a decision, and all three should be used to sort out the ethics of any course of action. But ethical issues are seldom clear-cut, because the rights, interests, goals, and incentives of stakeholders often conflict. That is why many experts on ethics add a fourth rule — the practical rule.

The three-question test: a business decision is probably ethically acceptable if the manager can answer “yes” to all three:

#The question in EnglishWhat it means
1Does my decision fall within the accepted values or standards that typically apply in business activity today? Is my decision inside the accepted values or standards of business today?
2Am I willing to see the decision communicated to all people and groups affected by it—for example, by having it reported on TV or via social media? Am I willing to have it told to everyone it affects — for example, shown on TV or social media?
3Would the people with whom I have a significant personal relationship, such as family members, friends, or even managers in other organizations, approve of the decision? Would the people I have an important personal relationship with — family, friends, or even managers in other organizations — approve?

Why it helps: applying the practical rule makes sure the manager takes the interests of all stakeholders into account. After he applies it, he can judge whether he chose an ethical or unethical action, and he must accept the consequences (they must abide by the consequences).

EXAM BOX

The justice rule is that an ethical decision distributes benefits and harms among people and groups in a fair, equitable, or impartial way. Managers should compare and contrast alternative courses of action based on the degree to which they will fairly or equitably distribute outcomes to stakeholders.

Each of these rules offers a different and complementary way of determining whether a decision or behavior is ethical, and all three rules should be used to sort out the ethics of a particular course of action. For this reason many experts on ethics add a fourth rule to determine whether a business decision is ethical: The practical rule is that an ethical decision is one that a manager has no hesitation or reluctance about communicating to people outside the company because the typical person in a society would think it is acceptable.

Applying the practical rule to analyze a business decision ensures that managers are taking into account the interests of all stakeholders.

TRAP
  • Justice = distribution (distributes benefits and harms). If the scenario has “same skill and performance = same pay”, “does not favor the people he likes”, or “no difference by gender, race, or religion” → justice, not moral rights.
  • Justice is a two-way street: employees must be fair to the company (hard work and loyalty), and customers too. A choice that says “fairness is the manager's duty only” is incomplete.
  • The yardstick of the practical rule is the typical person in a society — not the law, not the manager's own opinion, and not the shareholders.
  • The questions are three, and you need “yes” to all of them. The second one has TV or social media, and the third has family members, friends, or even managers in other organizations.
  • no hesitation (the figure) / no reluctance (the margin) / no hesitation or reluctance (the main text) — all three wordings are the same rule.
  • The sentence all three rules should be used means the first three rules, before the fourth is added. The total is four, and practical is the fourth one, added by ethics experts.

The Tragedy of the Commons and Trust
Why ethics is not a luxury: without it, a shared resource is ruined for everyone
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
When one person profits from cheating, the others copy him, and everyone chases his own interest without caring about others → a group disaster called the tragedy of the commons. And the other way round: trust raises efficiency and company performance, and ethical behavior also raises the country's standard of living (Figure 4.3).
🧠
A shared pasture: every shepherd adds more sheep “because it's free”, the grass dies, and the wind blows the soil away — everyone loses.
EXPLANATION — the direct answer: why should a manager be ethical?

Why is it important for managers — and people in general — to act ethically and to hold back their pursuit of self-interest by thinking about how their actions affect others? The book's answer: the relentless pursuit of self-interest can lead to a collective disaster when one or more people start to profit from being unethical, because this encourages others to do the same. As more and more people act for their own interests, soon everybody is trying to manipulate the situation for personal ends with no regard for how their actions affect others. This is called the “tragedy of the commons”.

The book's story (learn it in order, it comes as a scenario):

  1. A farming community has common land that everyone has an equal right to use.
  2. Each farmer chases his own interest and uses the free resource as much as he can — grazing his cattle and sheep on it.
  3. Together, all the farmers overgraze the land, and it is quickly worn out.
  4. Then a strong wind blows away the exposed topsoil, and the common land is destroyed.

The lesson: chasing personal interest with no regard for the interests of society leads to disaster for each person and for the whole society, because scarce resources are destroyed.

Trust = The willingness of one person or group to have faith or confidence in the goodwill of another person, even though this puts them at risk. In other words, a person or group is ready to believe in the goodwill of others even though this puts them at risk — and the book explains the risk: the other side might act in a deceitful way.

EXAM BOX

The answer is that the relentless pursuit of self-interest can lead to a collective disaster when one or more people start to profit from being unethical because this encourages other people to act in the same way. As more and more people act in their own interests, soon everybody is trying to manipulate the situation to serve their personal ends with no regard for the effects of their action on others. This is called the “tragedy of the commons.”

Trust is the willingness of one person or group to have faith or confidence in the goodwill of another person, even though this puts them at risk (because the other might act in a deceitful way). When trust exists, stakeholders are likely to signal their good intentions by cooperating and providing information that makes it easier to exchange and price goods and services.

Managers should behave ethically because this avoids the tragedy of the commons and results in a general increase in efficiency, effectiveness, and company performance.

TRAP
  • The tragedy of the commons is not about one company that cheated and got caught; it is about a shared resource that is destroyed because everyone chased his own interest. The engine is imitation: one person profits from cheating, so the others copy him.
  • Trust includes risk: even though this puts them at risk. A choice that says trust “without any risk” or “after full guarantees” is wrong.
  • The problem of the unethical society: time is lost in bargaining (an unproductive activity) → less efficiency and effectiveness → fewer goods and services → a lower standard of living. Not “prices rise” and not “taxes increase”.
  • The order of Figure 4.3: efficiency and effectiveness → company performance → national standard of living. If a choice puts company performance first, or adds trust or reputation as a box in the figure, it is wrong.
  • In an ethical society, ethical behavior becomes a valued social norm — a norm (from the values lesson in Chapter 3: norms are unwritten), not a law.

Reputation
The guard that stops cheating — and every stakeholder has a reputation to lose
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Reputation = the esteem or high repute that people and organizations gain when they behave ethically. And the chance of losing it is an important safeguard against unethical behavior.
🧠
A shawarma shop famous for being clean, people drive across town for it. It cheats once, the video spreads — years to win its name back.
EXPLANATION — wasting collective resources

As you learned in Chapter 1: one of a manager's biggest responsibilities is to protect and nurture the resources under his control. And any stakeholder — manager, worker, shareholder, supplier — who advances his own interest by behaving unethically toward others, in one of two ways:

  • either by taking resources,
  • or by denying resources to others,

is wasting collective resources. If others copy him, the rate of resource misuse goes up, and in the end there are not enough resources left to produce goods and services.

The important rule: unethical behavior that goes unpunished creates incentives for people to put their unbridled self-interest above the rights of others. When that happens, the benefits people gain from joining together in organizations disappear quickly.

EXPLANATION — reputation

The book says: an important safeguard against unethical behavior is the potential for loss of reputation. And reputation is an important asset. All stakeholders have valuable reputations they must protect, because their ability to earn a living and obtain resources in the long run depends on how they behave.

Consequences for a manager: if a manager misuses resources and people see his behavior as against accepted standards, his reputation suffers — and behaving unethically in the short run has serious long-term consequences:

  • A separate consequence: a manager with a bad reputation finds it hard to get a job with other companies.

The chain of damage (in order, four links): the book links it to shareholders:

  1. Shareholders who see managers as unethical may refuse to invest in their companies.
  2. This lowers the stock price.
  3. It undermines the companies' reputations.
  4. And in the end it puts the managers' own jobs at risk.

All stakeholders have reputations to lose — the book's examples:

StakeholderThe bad behaviorThe result
Suppliersprovide shoddy inputs over time organizations learn not to deal with them, and in the end they go out of business
Powerful customersdemand ridiculously low prices their suppliers become less willing to deal with them, and resources become hard to get
Workersshirk their responsibilities at work when they are fired, they find it hard to get new jobs
The manager or the companyknown for being unethical other stakeholders see them with suspicion and hostility → a bad reputation. The opposite: one known for ethical practices builds a good reputation
EXPLANATION — the book's conclusion for the section

In a complex and diverse society, stakeholders and people in general need to realize that they are part of a larger social group: the way they make decisions and act affects not only themselves but the lives of many people. Sadly, some people — because of the daily struggle to survive and succeed, or because they ignore the rights of others completely — lose this larger connection. We see our relationship with our family, friends, school, and place of worship (church in the book), but we must go further and remember how our actions affect people who will judge what we do and whom we might harm. The sentence the book ends with: “Our moral scruples are like those ‘other people’ but are inside our heads.” — in other words, your moral scruples (from lesson 1) are like “the other people”, but inside your head.

Reputation, the esteem or high repute that people or organizations gain when they behave ethically, is an important asset. TRAP
  • Reputation has two definitions in this chapter: here = the esteem or high repute… when they behave ethically, and in lesson 4 (Why Be Socially Responsible?) = the trust, goodwill, and confidence others have in a company…. If the question is about a safeguard against unethical behavior → this lesson's definition.
  • The danger is in unethical behavior that goes unpunished — that is what creates the incentives. A choice that says “any unethical behavior, even when punished, encourages copying” is wrong.
  • Reputation is not only for the company: all stakeholders have one. Link the pairs: suppliers → shoddy inputs · powerful customers → ridiculously low prices · workers → shirk responsibilities.
  • Wasting collective resources happens in two ways: taking resources or denying them to others. And imitation raises the rate.
  • The direction: unethical in the short run → serious consequences in the long run. Do not reverse it.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 1 or 2, go back to the table “The question you ask yourself” in the first concept; if you miss question 5, go back to the order of Figure 4.3.

1. A manager must choose between global outsourcing, which benefits shareholders and customers but causes major layoffs, and keeping high-cost production at home, which might cause the business to go bankrupt. She chooses the course of action that does the least harm to stakeholders as a whole. Which ethical rule is she applying?
  1. The moral rights rule
  2. The utilitarian rule
  3. The justice rule
  4. The practical rule
Answer

B. “The most benefits or the least harm to stakeholders” is exactly how you apply the utilitarian rule, and this is the book's own outsourcing example — which is usually regarded as the ethical choice because the other choice might bankrupt the company. The trap in A: moral rights asks about rights (privacy, safety, freedom…), not about the total harm. C is a trap too: justice is about distributing benefits and harms fairly, not about the least total harm. And D is a trap: practical asks whether you could announce the decision to people without hesitation.

2. Before announcing a decision, a manager asks himself: Does it fall within the accepted standards of business today? Would I be willing to see it reported on TV or via social media? Would my family and friends approve of it? He is applying the:
  1. Justice rule
  2. Moral rights rule
  3. Practical rule
  4. Utilitarian rule
Answer

C. These are the three questions of the practical rule in order — and the decision is acceptable if the answer is “yes” to all three, because the yardstick is the typical person in a society. The trap in B: moral rights has the adage “Do unto others…”, and you may feel that “my family would approve” is close to it, but the three-question test belongs to practical only. And A and D have no idea of “announcing the decision outside the company”.

3. According to Figure 4.2, which of the following is NOT one of the four rules for ethical decision making?
  1. The utilitarian rule
  2. The moral rights rule
  3. The societal rule
  4. The justice rule
Answer

C. Figure 4.2 has four boxes around Rules for Ethical Decision Making: Utilitarian (top), Moral Rights (left), Justice (right), Practical (bottom). There is no societal rule. The trap: societal ethics is one of the four sources of managerial ethics (lesson 3) — the exam mixes the list of rules with the list of sources because both have four. And the question uses NOT, so do not rush and pick the first rule you know.

4. Several fishing companies share a lake that no one owns. Each company catches as many fish as it can to maximize its own profit, other companies copy this behavior, and soon the fish are gone and every company suffers. This situation is best described as:
  1. An ethical dilemma
  2. The tragedy of the commons
  3. The justice rule
  4. A loss of reputation
Answer

B. It is exactly the story of the common pasture: a shared resource + each one chases his own interest + the others copy → the scarce resource is destroyed and everyone loses. The trap in A: an ethical dilemma (lesson 1) is the problem of one person choosing between his own interest and what is right, not a group disaster. C is a rule for making decisions, not a description of a result. And D is a trap: reputation is another topic in the same section, but in this scenario no one was caught and no reputation was damaged.

5. According to Figure 4.3, ethical behavior first increases ________, which then increases company performance and, finally, the national standard of living, well-being, and prosperity.
  1. Trust between stakeholders
  2. The company's reputation
  3. Efficiency and effectiveness of production and trade
  4. Shareholders' dividends
Answer

C. The first box after Ethical behavior in Figure 4.3 is Increases efficiency and effectiveness of production and trade, then company performance, then the national standard of living. The trap in A and B: trust and reputation are explained in the same section, but they are not written inside the figure. And D is a trap: dividends are not mentioned in the figure at all.

Utilitarian ruleAn ethical decision produces the greatest good for the greatest number of peoplenumbers and amounts; the outsourcing example is usually regarded as ethical under it
Moral rights ruleAn ethical decision best keeps and protects the fundamental or inalienable rights and privileges of the people affectedsix rights; the employee's privacy against the company's property
“Do unto others as you would have them do unto you”Treat people the way you want them to treat youa moral rights principle — not justice
Justice ruleAn ethical decision distributes benefits and harms in a fair, equitable, or impartial waydistributes; same skill = same pay; fairness runs both ways
Practical ruleAn ethical decision is one a manager has no hesitation or reluctance about telling people outside the companythe yardstick is the typical person in a society; three questions, all “yes”; it is the fourth rule
Tragedy of the commonsA collective disaster when everyone chases self-interest and a shared resource is destroyeda shared resource + people copy the one who profited from cheating
Unethical society / ethical societyA society where people cheat each other / a society where people trust each otherunethical = cheating and bargaining → lower standard of living; ethical = trust → higher efficiency and performance
TrustThe willingness to have faith in the goodwill of another person even though it puts you at riskit includes risk: even though this puts them at risk
Valued social normWhat ethical behavior becomes when people see the results of honestya norm, not a law
Collective resourcesThe resources everyone shares in an organization and a societywasted by taking them or denying them to others; imitation raises the rate
ReputationThe esteem or high repute people or organizations gain when they behave ethicallya safeguard against unethical behavior; its second definition is in lesson 4
