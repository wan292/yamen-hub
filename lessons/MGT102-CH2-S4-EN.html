Management Science Theory
Taylor's same goal, but with mathematics and technology
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Rigorous quantitative techniques that help the manager make maximum use of the organization's resources — and the book calls it a contemporary extension of scientific management.
🧠
Taylor with a stopwatch and paper. Today's manager, same spot, laptop and a math model — same question, newer tools.
EXPLANATION

The book describes management science theory as a contemporary approach to management that focuses on the use of rigorous quantitative techniques to help managers make maximum use of resources to produce goods and services.

And the most important sentence in this part: the book says it is a contemporary extension of scientific management, because Taylor also took a quantitative approach to measuring the worker–task mix to raise efficiency. So the two are from the same family — but management science is newer and its tools are sophisticated mathematics.

And technology has a large effect on all kinds of practices in management, and it continues to change the tools a manager uses to make decisions.

Management science has many branches, and each branch deals with a specific set of concerns. The book lists three branches — memorize the count and the names:

And quantitative management in particular uses mathematical techniques, and the book names five examples of them (it says such as) — learn them as they are (careful: the book counts linear and nonlinear programming as one item):

All these subfields, together with sophisticated technology, provide tools and techniques the manager uses to improve the quality of his decisions and increase efficiency and effectiveness.

The book's example — Toyota: it applied management science theory through the Toyota Production System (TPS). TPS emphasizes two things: continuous improvement in quality and the reduction of waste through learning. TPS was a major catalyst for the “lean revolution” in global manufacturing, and manufacturing companies around the world embraced this philosophy and adapted it for their own operations.

A cross-reference the book makes: Chapter 9 focuses on how to use operations management and the principle of total quality management (TQM) to improve quality, efficiency, and responsiveness to customers. So TQM is not one of the three branches of management science — it is the topic of another chapter.

EXAM BOX

Management science theory is a contemporary approach to management that focuses on the use of rigorous quantitative techniques to help managers make maximum use of organizational resources to produce goods and services. In essence, management science theory is a contemporary extension of scientific management, which, as developed by Taylor, also took a quantitative approach to measuring the worker–task mix to raise efficiency.

Quantitative management uses mathematical techniques—such as linear and nonlinear programming, modeling, simulation, queuing theory, and chaos theory—to help managers decide, for example, how much inventory to hold at different times of the year, where to locate a new factory, and how best to invest an organization's financial capital. Operations management gives managers a set of techniques they can use to analyze any aspect of an organization's production system to increase efficiency. Management information systems (MISs) give managers details about events occurring inside the organization as well as in its external environment—information that is vital for effective decision making.

TRAP
  • management science theory ≠ scientific management. The first is contemporary and its tools are advanced mathematics; the second is Taylor at the turn of the 20th century. The book says the first is an extension of the second — memorize the relationship, but never swap the two names.
  • The count is three branches: quantitative management · operations management · MISs. Any choice that adds total quality management, organizational learning, or behavioral management as a fourth branch is wrong.
  • An MIS gives information about the inside and the outside — not only the inside. A choice that says only inside the organization is wrong.
  • Do not mix the two jobs: operations management analyzes the production system, and quantitative management analyzes decisions with mathematics.
  • TPS is an example of management science theory, and it is linked to the lean revolution and TQM (Chapter 9). A choice that links TPS to behavioral management is wrong.

The Organizational Environment
The first time management thought steps outside the walls
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
All the forces and conditions that operate beyond the organization's boundaries, yet affect the manager's ability to acquire and utilize resources.
🧠
A small grocery store: your shelves are perfect, but the supplier raises prices and a competitor opens next door.
EXPLANATION

The book calls this moment an important milestone in the history of management thought: the moment researchers went beyond the study of how managers can influence behavior within organizations to consider how managers control the organization's relationship with its external environment.

What resources does the organizational environment include? The book lists them clearly:

  • raw materials and skilled people that the organization requires to produce goods and services.
  • the support of groups — including customers who buy these goods and services and provide the organization with big data that is critical to its overall financial success.

And there is a sentence the book puts as a way of measuring an organization's success: you look at how effective its managers are at obtaining scarce and valuable resources. So success is not only good internal order — it is also the ability to pull resources from outside.

When did the importance of studying the environment become clear? After the development of open-systems theory and contingency theory during the 1960s, and then more recent theories, including dynamic capabilities. Those three are exactly what comes next in this lesson.

EXAM BOX

An important milestone in the history of management thought occurred when researchers went beyond the study of how managers can influence behavior within organizations to consider how managers control the organization's relationship with its external environment, or organizational environment—the set of forces and conditions that operate beyond an organization's boundaries but affect a manager's ability to acquire and utilize resources.

One way of determining the relative success of an organization is to consider how effective its managers are at obtaining scarce and valuable resources. The importance of studying the environment became clear after the development of open-systems theory and contingency theory during the 1960s and other more recent theories, including dynamic capabilities.

TRAP
  • The definition says beyond an organization's boundaries — that means outside the boundaries. A choice that defines the organizational environment as the forces inside the organization is wrong.
  • The environment affects the manager's ability to acquire and utilize resources — not to “motivate employees”; that one is behavioral management.
  • The dates: open-systems and contingency = the 1960s. Management science theory = World War II. Dynamic capabilities = the late 1990s. The exam swaps them.

The Open-Systems View
Inputs → conversion → outputs, and the cycle starts again
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The organization takes in resources from its environment, transforms them into goods and services, and sends them back to the same environment where customers buy them — and the sales money pays for the next cycle.
🧠
A burger shop: it buys meat, bread and workers (inputs), cooks them (conversion), sells the meal (output), and with that money buys tomorrow's meat.
EXPLANATION — who developed it and what it says

One of the most influential views of how an organization is affected by its external environment was developed by Daniel Katz, Robert Kahn, and James Thompson in the 1960s — three names; memorize the count and the names together. They viewed the organization as an open system.

Why is it called “open”? Because the organization draws from and interacts with the external environment in order to survive — the organization is open to its environment, not closed on itself.

The three stages, in order:

  1. At the input stage — the organization acquires resources such as raw materials, money, and skilled workers to produce goods and services.
  2. At the conversion stage — the organization's workforce, using appropriate tools, techniques, and machinery, transforms the inputs into outputs of finished goods and services — such as cars, hamburgers, or flights to Hawaii. And this is exactly where value is added.
  3. At the output stage — the organization releases the finished goods and services to its external environment, where customers buy and use them to satisfy their needs.

And then? The money the organization gets from selling its outputs lets it acquire more resources, and the cycle begins again. That is why the figure is circular, not a straight line.

Figure 2.4 — The Organization as an Open System

This is Figure 2.4 in the book. First, notice the word at the top: ENVIRONMENT — all three boxes sit under it, so the environment surrounds the whole process instead of standing at one side. Then read the boxes from the left: Input stage (Raw materials · Money and capital · Human resources) → Conversion stage (Machinery · Computers · Human skills) → Output stage (Goods · Services). And under the line, the key sentence: Sales of outputs allow organization to obtain new supplies of inputs — the arrow goes back and closes the circle. What gets asked: “what are the elements of the input stage?” and “at which stage is value added?” — the answer is the conversion stage, because the box itself says adds value to them.

EXPLANATION — the closed system and entropy

The opposite of the open system is the closed system: a self-contained system that is not affected by changes in its external environment.

And the link the exam loves: organizations that operate as closed systems, that ignore the external environment, and that fail to acquire inputs → are likely to experience entropy. Entropy is the tendency of a closed system to lose its ability to control itself and thus to dissolve and disintegrate.

Using the model: management theorists can model the activities of most organizations with this view. The book's example: manufacturing companies like Ford and Caterpillar buy inputs such as component parts, skilled and semiskilled labor, and robots and computer-controlled manufacturing equipment; then at the conversion stage they use their manufacturing skills to assemble inputs into outputs of cars and heavy equipment.

And a warning from the book: competition between organizations for resources is one of several major challenges to managing the organizational environment.

EXPLANATION — synergy

Researchers using the open-systems view also care about another question: how the parts of the system work together to promote efficiency and effectiveness. And systems theorists like to argue that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts — they mean that an organization performs at a higher level when its departments work together rather than separately.

And synergy = the performance gains that result when individuals and departments coordinate their actions. One important point: synergy is possible only in an organized system.

Application: the strategy of using teams composed of people from different departments reflects systems theorists' interest in designing organizational systems that create synergy, and thus increase efficiency and effectiveness.

EXAM BOX

These theorists viewed the organization as an open system—a system that takes in resources from its external environment and converts or transforms them into goods and services that are sent back to that environment, where they are bought by customers. At the input stage, an organization acquires resources such as raw materials, money, and skilled workers. At the conversion stage, the organization's workforce, using appropriate tools, techniques, and machinery, transforms the inputs into outputs of finished goods and services. At the output stage, the organization releases finished goods and services to its external environment.

A closed system, in contrast, is a self-contained system that is not affected by changes in its external environment. Organizations that operate as closed systems, that ignore the external environment, and that fail to acquire inputs are likely to experience entropy, which is the tendency of a closed system to lose its ability to control itself and thus to dissolve and disintegrate.

Systems theorists like to argue that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts; they mean that an organization performs at a higher level when its departments work together rather than separately. Synergy, the performance gains that result from the combined actions of individuals and departments, is possible only in an organized system.

TRAP
  • open system ≠ closed system ≠ entropy. Entropy is not a type of system — it is a result that hits the closed system. A choice that says entropy is a type of open system is wrong.
  • The stages are three, in order: input → conversion → output. Any choice that adds a feedback stage or a planning stage as a fourth stage is wrong.
  • Value is added at the conversion stage, not at the output stage. And the elements of each stage in Figure 2.4 get swapped: Machinery · Computers · Human skills are conversion, and Raw materials · Money and capital · Human resources are input.
  • Synergy is defined as performance gains from coordinating individuals and departments — not “reducing costs” and not “increasing specialization”. And the sentence tied to it: the whole is greater than the sum of its parts.
  • The three names Katz, Kahn, Thompson = the open system. Burns & Stalker and Lawrence & Lorsch = contingency theory. The exam mixes them because all of them are from the 1960s.

Contingency Theory
There is no one best way — the environment is what decides
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The organizational structures and control systems a manager chooses depend on (are contingent on) the characteristics of the external environment the organization operates in.
🧠
Your clothes depend on the weather, not your taste: Jeddah summer, a light thobe; Tabuk winter, a jacket.
EXPLANATION — the theory and who developed it

Another milestone in management theory: the development of contingency theory in the 1960s by Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker in Britain, and Paul Lawrence and Jay Lorsch in the United States — four names in two pairs; memorize who is where.

And the theory's crucial message is one sentence — learn it in English word for word: there is no one best way to organize.

According to the theory, the characteristics of the environment affect the organization's ability to obtain resources. So to maximize the chance of getting resources, the manager must allow the organization's departments to organize and control their activities in the ways most likely to let them acquire resources, given the constraints of the environment they face.

In other words: how the manager designs the hierarchy, chooses the control system, and leads and motivates his employees — all of it is contingent on the characteristics of the organizational environment.

Figure 2.5 — Contingency Theory of Organizational Design

This is Figure 2.5. Read it from the left: a box reading Characteristics of the environment… goes to a box reading Determine the design of an organization's structure and control systems, and then it branches in two. The top branch: stable → mechanistic structure (centralized authority, vertical communication flows, control through strict rules and procedures) — in the figure's own words, Organizations in stable environments choose a mechanistic structure. The bottom branch: changing → organic structure (decentralized authority, horizontal communication flows, cross-departmental cooperation). And under the figure, the key sentence: There is no one best way to organize…. How to read it: the environment is the cause and the structure is the result — the arrow moves in one direction only. What gets asked: they give you a description of an environment and ask which structure, or they give you three characteristics and ask which structure — so memorize the three for each branch exactly as written in the brackets.

EXPLANATION — an important characteristic: the degree of change

The book says an important characteristic of the external environment that affects the organization's ability to obtain resources is the degree to which the environment is changing. The changes it names — three kinds:

  • changes in technology — they create new products (the book's example: Amazon Echo) and cause existing products to become obsolete (the book's example: Blu-ray players).
  • the entry of new competitors — such as foreign organizations that compete for the same available resources.
  • unstable economic conditions.

And the general rule it ends with: the faster the environment changes, the greater the problems of getting resources, and the greater the manager's need to coordinate the activities of people in different departments so they respond to the environment quickly and effectively.

EXPLANATION — mechanistic versus organic

Drawing on Weber's and Fayol's principles of organization and management (the ones you took in the two sections before this), Burns and Stalker proposed two basic ways in which managers can organize and control an organization's activities in response to its external environment.

And there is a link the book states openly, which is a very likely question: a mechanistic structure typically rests on Theory X assumptions, and an organic structure typically rests on Theory Y assumptions — go back to section 3 if you forgot them.

Why does the mechanistic structure work in a stable environment? Because it provides the most efficient way to operate: it lets the manager obtain inputs at the lowest cost, gives the organization the greatest control over its conversion processes, and enables the most efficient production of goods and services with the smallest expenditure of resources. And at McDonald's: supervisors make all the important decisions, and employees are closely supervised and follow well-defined rules and standard operating procedures.

And why the organic structure in a changing environment? Because getting resources becomes hard, so the manager needs to organize activities in a way that allows cooperation, quick movement to obtain scarce resources (such as new kinds of inputs for new products), and an effective response to surprises. And like Follett's model, the organization works in an organic way because authority rests with the individuals, departments, and teams best placed to control the current problems. The result: a manager in an organic structure reacts faster to a changing environment than a manager in a mechanistic structure. That is why, in an organic structure, tasks and roles are left ambiguous so people cooperate and respond to surprises.

But watch the drawback — the exam loves it: the organic structure is generally more expensive to operate because it requires more managerial time, money, and effort to be spent on coordination. So it is used only when it is needed — that is, when the environment is unstable and changing rapidly.

THE BOOK'S BOX — MANAGING GLOBALLY: Changing Up the Manager's Role

This is a side box on the same pages, and its idea is: perhaps more than ever, global forces are reshaping the manager's role. The forces it lists:

  • A pandemic that shook whole industries and forced companies to rethink where people can and should work.
  • pent-up consumer demand that exploded while some regions and companies were still keeping workers at home — which helped disrupt supply chains.
  • The Russian invasion of Ukraine and the global response to it added fuel to demand-driven inflation, and forced some companies to re-evaluate their global footprint.
  • The rapid adoption of digital technology — work once considered exclusively human is being automated.
  • A tight labor market because of slowing birth rates and the challenges of the pandemic.

The result for the manager's skills: today's manager spends less time on the routine tasks the computer does, and focuses more on retaining employees through meaningful work and professional development, including one-on-one coaching. And a turbulent environment can be frustrating, exhausting, and overwhelming — and this comes after years of company initiatives to get leaner by removing layers of management, so the manager must build new skills while the number of his direct reports grows at the same time.

The Telstra example (Australia) — memorize it: it split the manager's job into two roles: Leaders of people focus on human resource planning, selecting employees, and professional development; and Leaders of work handle project teams and are responsible for budgets, timelines, and project delivery. The result: managers can focus on their strengths, and employees see the benefit of one manager who cares about their development and another who is clear about succeeding at projects.

EXAM BOX

The crucial message of contingency theory is that there is no one best way to organize: The organizational structures and the control systems that managers choose depend on (are contingent on) characteristics of the external environment in which the organization operates. An important characteristic of the external environment that affects an organization's ability to obtain resources is the degree to which the environment is changing.

Drawing on Weber's and Fayol's principles of organization and management, Burns and Stalker proposed two basic ways in which managers can organize and control an organization's activities: They can use a mechanistic structure or an organic structure. A mechanistic structure typically rests on Theory X assumptions, and an organic structure typically rests on Theory Y assumptions. In a mechanistic structure, authority is centralized at the top of the managerial hierarchy; tasks and roles are clearly specified, employees are closely supervised, and the emphasis is on strict discipline and order. In an organic structure, authority is decentralized to middle and first-line managers to encourage them to take responsibility and act quickly to pursue scarce resources. However, an organic structure is generally more expensive to operate because it requires that more managerial time, money, and effort be spent on coordination.

TRAP
  • The easiest thing to reverse under pressure: stable → mechanistic, changing → organic. Remember: “changing = organic = alive = flexible”.
  • The organic one is more expensive because of coordination costs. A choice that says an organic structure is cheaper to operate is wrong, even though it sounds logical because there is less control in it.
  • mechanistic = Theory X · organic = Theory Y. If they swap them, it is wrong.
  • The examples: McDonald's = mechanistic · Google, Apple, 3M = organic (and notice: the same three companies are named as Theory Y examples — that is not a contradiction, it confirms the link).
  • The three characteristics of each structure in Figure 2.5 get swapped: vertical + centralized + strict rules = mechanistic. horizontal + decentralized + cross-departmental cooperation = organic.
  • The arrow in Figure 2.5 goes one way: the environment determines the structure. A choice that turns it around (“the structure determines the environment”) is wrong.
  • Do not mix the two names: organizational environment is the concept (the forces beyond the boundaries), and contingency theory is the theory that says the structure depends on it. And a question that asks for the “crucial message” is always answered with there is no one best way to organize.

Dynamic Capabilities
Stable enough to deliver, flexible enough to shift
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The organization's ability to build, integrate, and reconfigure processes so it can deal with internal and external environments that change fast.
🧠
A football player who covers two positions: steady in his main role, yet able to switch position in a minute when the plan changes.
EXPLANATION

The book says that over the past few decades other management theories with a large effect appeared, among them total quality management (Chapter 9), organizational learning (Chapter 7), and dynamic capabilities — and this last one is the one it explains here.

Whose theory it is: David Teece, a professor at UC–Berkeley's Haas School of Business, came up with it in the late 1990s.

What does it explain? Companies must be stable enough to deliver value to customers, and at the same time resilient and flexible enough to shift focus when situations demand a different approach.

An important exam difference — against best practices:

Best practicesDynamic capabilities
Where they come fromlearned capabilities and activities undertaken by one or two companies rooted in the organization's past experiences
What happens to themthey spread to an entire industry they are unique to each company — they do not travel

Teece's three managerial activities that make a process “dynamic” — sensing · seizing · transforming, in order:

#The activityThe book's definitionThe book's example (Apple)
1Sensing
الاستشعار		 identifying and assessing opportunities outside the company — finding and judging the opportunities that are outside the company. Steve Jobs realized that consumers wanted a smaller, nicer mp3 player than the ones in the market.
2Seizing
الاغتنام		 mobilizing company resources to capture value…from these opportunities — moving the company's resources to take the value from the opportunity. Apple — based on its assessment of the competition — seized the chance to design and produce the iPod, an elegant alternative to the bulky devices.
3Transforming
التحويل		 an organization's ability to continue making changes as needed to maintain success — the ability to keep changing in order to hold on to success. After the iPod, Apple shifted its focus from producing computers only to widening its product line into consumer electronics, and then into music and original content streaming.

A number the book gives: in 2022, Apple topped the annual Forbes list of the world's most valuable brands for the tenth year in a row, at a value of more than $482 billion.

And the role of technology: advances such as artificial intelligence and data analytics pushed managers to create processes and tools that take advantage of opportunities while staying ahead of the competition. Among them is big data — information gathered via social media and other digital platforms — which is analyzed and categorized into useful patterns and data sets that companies use to:

  1. change or refine internal processes.
  2. develop and introduce new products and services.
  3. create efficiencies in managing customers and suppliers.

And the conclusion: these technologies and dynamic capabilities can be tested, scaled, and adapted quickly, and that gives organizations a tremendous competitive advantage.

EXAM BOX

David Teece came up with the theory of dynamic capabilities in the late 1990s to explain how companies must be stable enough to deliver value to customers yet resilient and flexible enough to shift focus when situations demand a different approach. Unlike best practices, which typically are learned capabilities and activities undertaken by one or two companies that spread to an entire industry, dynamic capabilities are unique to each company and are more than likely rooted in the organization's past experiences.

According to Teece, three types of managerial activities can help make a process or activity dynamic: sensing, seizing, and transforming. Sensing refers to identifying and assessing opportunities outside the company. Seizing describes the action of mobilizing company resources to capture value for the organization from these opportunities. Transforming is an organization's ability to continue making changes as needed to maintain success. For example, big data, information gathered via social media and other digital platforms, can be analyzed and categorized into useful patterns and data sets.

TRAP
  • dynamic capabilities are unique to the company · best practices spread to a whole industry. A choice that reverses them is wrong, and it is the most expected choice here.
  • The order is sensing → seizing → transforming: first you see the opportunity outside, then you move the resources and take it, then you keep changing. A choice that says sensing means mobilizing the resources is wrong.
  • Sensing is outside the company — not inside it.
  • The date: the late 1990s and Teece. If they put the 1960s or Burns & Stalker, it is wrong — those belong to contingency theory.
  • TQM is Chapter 9 · organizational learning is Chapter 7 — the book only names them here and does not define them. Do not look for a definition of them in this chapter.
  • big data is defined as information gathered via social media and other digital platforms — not “any large amount of data” and not “a management information system”.

Test yourself

The questions are in English because the exam is in English. Answer first, then open the solution. If you miss question 3 or 4, go back to Figure 2.4 and Figure 2.5 and study them from the picture itself.

1. A manager at a food company uses linear programming and queuing theory to decide how much inventory to hold in each season and where to locate a new factory. This work is best described as an application of:
  1. Operations management
  2. Quantitative management
  3. Management information systems
  4. Behavioral management
Answer

B. Linear programming and queuing theory are two of the mathematical techniques the book names under quantitative management, and “how much inventory” and “where to locate a new factory” are word for word the book's own examples. The trap in A: operations management analyzes the production system; it does not compute decisions with mathematics. C is a trap: an MIS gives information about the inside and the outside; it does not solve equations. And D is not a branch of management science at all — it is a theory from section 3.

2. Which of the following is NOT one of the three branches of management science theory described in this chapter?
  1. Quantitative management
  2. Operations management
  3. Total quality management
  4. Management information systems
Answer

C. The branches are only three: quantitative management, operations management, and management information systems. And the trap: TQM is named in the same paragraph — but the book names it as a Chapter 9 topic together with operations management, not as a fourth branch. Watch the word NOT: they give you three correct answers so you rush and pick one of them.

3. According to Figure 2.4, at which stage does an organization transform inputs and add value to them?
  1. The input stage
  2. The conversion stage
  3. The output stage
  4. The feedback stage
Answer

B. The middle box in Figure 2.4 says word for word Organization transforms inputs and adds value to them, and its elements are Machinery · Computers · Human skills. The trap in A: the input stage acquires the resources (raw materials, money and capital, human resources) but adds no value. C is a trap: the output stage releases the goods and services to the environment. And D is a stage that does not exist — the stages are three, and the return arrow is not a stage; it is the sentence Sales of outputs allow organization to obtain new supplies of inputs.

4. A software firm faces rapid technological change and new competitors every year. According to Figure 2.5, this firm is most likely to choose:
  1. A mechanistic structure, with centralized authority and vertical communication flows
  2. An organic structure, with decentralized authority and horizontal communication flows
  3. A closed system, to protect itself from the environment
  4. A mechanistic structure, because it is cheaper to operate
Answer

B. The bottom branch of Figure 2.5 says word for word Organizations in changing environments choose an organic structure (decentralized authority, horizontal communication flows, cross-departmental cooperation). The trap in A and D: the mechanistic structure really is cheaper, but it is for a stable environment — and the question says rapid change. And C is a nice trap: no organization “chooses” to be a closed system; the one that works that way and ignores its environment ends in entropy.

5. A company ignores its external environment, acts as a self-contained unit, and fails to acquire the inputs it needs. According to the chapter, it is most likely to experience:
  1. Synergy
  2. Entropy
  3. Seizing
  4. Best practices
Answer

B. Entropy = the tendency of a closed system to lose its ability to control itself and thus to dissolve and disintegrate — and the sentence in the question repeats the book's own three conditions: operates as a closed system, ignores the environment, fails to acquire inputs. The trap in A: synergy is the exact opposite — performance gains from coordinating individuals and departments. C is a trap: seizing is an activity of dynamic capabilities — mobilizing resources to capture an opportunity. And D: best practices are learned capabilities that spread to an entire industry; they have nothing to do with disintegration.

Toyota Production System (TPS)Toyota's application of management science theorycontinuous quality improvement + waste reduction; a catalyst of the lean revolution
Organizational environmentThe forces and conditions beyond the organization's boundaries that affect the managerbeyond the boundaries; it affects acquire and utilize resources
Open systemA system that takes in resources, converts them, and sends them back to the environment3 stages: input → conversion → output; Katz, Kahn, Thompson — the 1960s
Input stageThe organization acquires raw materials, money, and skilled workersRaw materials · Money and capital · Human resources — adds no value
Conversion stageThe workforce transforms the inputs into finished goods and servicesthis is where value is added; Machinery · Computers · Human skills
Output stageThe organization releases the finished goods and services to the environmentGoods · Services; the sales come back to pay for new inputs
Closed systemA self-contained system not affected by its external environmentits end is entropy
EntropyThe tendency of a closed system to lose control of itself and dissolve and disintegratea result that hits the closed system — not a type of system
SynergyPerformance gains that result when individuals and departments coordinate their actionsthe whole is greater than the sum of its parts; only in an organized system
Contingency theoryThe structure and control systems depend on the characteristics of the external environmentthere is no one best way to organize; Burns & Stalker + Lawrence & Lorsch — the 1960s
Mechanistic structureCentralized authority, clearly specified tasks, strict discipline and orderstable environment · vertical · strict rules · Theory X · McDonald's
Organic structureDecentralized authority, ambiguous roles, cooperation across departmentschanging environment · horizontal · Theory Y · more expensive
Dynamic capabilitiesBeing stable enough to deliver value yet flexible enough to shift focusTeece — the late 1990s; unique to each company
Best practicesLearned capabilities of one or two companiesthey spread to an entire industry — the opposite of dynamic capabilities
SensingIdentifying and assessing opportunities outside the companyoutside the company — the Steve Jobs / mp3 example
SeizingMobilizing company resources to capture value from the opportunitymoving the resources — the iPod example
TransformingThe ability to continue making changes as needed to maintain successkeep changing — Apple moving into music and streaming
Big dataInformation gathered via social media and other digital platformsnot “any large data” and not an MIS
