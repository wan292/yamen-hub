Chapter 2 — Scientific Management Theory

MGT 102 · Chapter 2 · Section 1 of 4: Scientific Management Theory
Objective LO2-1: Describe how the need to increase organizational efficiency and effectiveness has guided the evolution of management theory.
Objective LO2-2: Explain the principle of job specialization and division of labor, and tell why the study of person–task relationships is central to the pursuit of increased efficiency.

The whole of Chapter 2 answers one question

The question is: how do we raise the efficiency and effectiveness of an organization? (Both words come from lesson 1 of Chapter 1.) Every theory in this chapter came out as an answer to that question, and each one focused on something different: first the task, then the structure, then the people, then the numbers, and last the external environment.

This is the first section: Scientific Management Theory — the oldest theory and the first bar on the timeline. It has only three names in it, but the exam loves to swap them: Adam Smith, Frederick W. Taylor, and the Gilbreths. If you can tell them apart, you own the whole section.

And because this first section also carries LO2-1, it holds the map of the whole chapter (Figure 2.1) — learn the order of the five theories from here, because that question comes word for word.

The Evolution of Management Theory
Five theories in date order — learn the order before anything else
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Management theory did not arrive all at once — it came in five waves in date order, and each wave came to answer a weakness in the one before it.
🧠

EXPLANATION — the chapter's order, as the book announces it

The book walks through the chapter in this order:

  1. classical management theories — they emerged around the turn of the 20th century, and there are two of them:
    • scientific management — it focuses on matching people and tasks to maximize efficiency (this is our lesson today).
    • administrative management — it focuses on identifying the principles that lead to the most efficient system of organization and management (section 2).
  2. behavioral management theories — developed both before and after World War II, and they focus on how managers should lead and control their workforces to increase performance (section 3).
  3. management science theory — developed during World War II, and it became more and more important as researchers built rigorous analytical and quantitative techniques that help managers measure and control performance (section 4).
  4. the changes in management practices from the middle to the late 20th century, and the theories that explain how the external environment affects the organization and the manager (section 4 as well).

At the end of the chapter you will also understand how economic, political, and cultural forces (three forces — learn the count) affected the development of these theories, and how managers and their organizations changed their behavior as a result.

THE OPENING CASE — A Manager's Challenge: Google Searches for the Best Way to Work

The book opens the chapter with a story about Google and its parent company Alphabet. Read it, because the exam may give you a scenario taken from it:

  • Google expanded during the COVID-19 pandemic and hired about one-third of its 165,000 employees in that period. Most people were working remotely, so managers had to find ways to direct work, inspire innovation, and ensure collaboration.
  • Then the situation flipped: tech companies faced headwinds and talk of recession, and the chief executive Sundar Pichai urged employees to work with "more hunger" and to be "more mission-focused, more focused on our products, more customer focused".
  • The manager's ever-present objective, which his message carried: to serve customers well while keeping costs under control. Companies that reach this kind of efficiency are well placed to sustain profitable growth.
  • But words are not enough: managers traditionally reach efficiency and quality by structuring work and keeping a close eye on workers — and that is hard in a high-tech organization like Google. The manager also needs to know what kinds of people are likely to be high performers and what conditions will let them excel.
  • Fiona Cicconi — Google's chief people officer, and one of the pandemic-era hires. She had been working from home, managing human resources for the pharmaceutical company AstraZeneca, and she negotiated for the Google job by video conference; it was months before travel restrictions lifted so she could relocate.
  • Her challenge: to lead Google's transition away from remote work. According to managers, it was hard to mentor and acclimate new employees remotely, and employees in separate locations had trouble resolving conflicts. But employees had also grown used to remote work — before the pandemic Google was known for free food, on-site massages and other perks, and during the pandemic they enjoyed a different kind of comfort.
  • The solution: make "hybrid" working the norm — three days in the office + two days remote, with the in-office days designated according to the employee's product area so that collaboration works. And because not every job needs the same amount of interaction, the company lets an employee apply for permission to work only remotely.
  • And the data did not make it easy: in surveys, employee answers varied widely; Google adjusts pay rates to the local cost of living, so someone who moved to a cheaper place would have to take a pay cut; and some managers objected to being put in the position of deciding who may keep working remotely, an issue where employees are highly attuned to fairness.
  • Positive signs: productivity dipped at first and then improved — which suggests employees are adaptable; and employees were far less likely than expected to ask for full-time remote work.

How the book ties it to the chapter: when a company like Google faces a hard problem, many factors are in play — does the solution come from people · processes · technology · reporting relationships? (Four possibilities — learn the count.) And the advantage Google's managers have over a manager a century ago is that they can apply the knowledge gathered by the researchers, consultants and businesspeople who studied management and built and tested theories about "what gets results".

EXAM BOX

First we examine the so-called classical management theories that emerged around the turn of the 20th century. These include scientific management, which focuses on matching people and tasks to maximize efficiency, and administrative management, which focuses on identifying the principles that will lead to the creation of the most efficient system of organization and management. Next we consider behavioral management theories, developed both before and after World War II; these focus on how managers should lead and control their workforces to increase performance. Then we discuss management science theory, which developed during World War II and has become increasingly important as researchers have developed rigorous analytical and quantitative techniques to help managers measure and control organizational performance.

You will also understand how economic, political, and cultural forces have affected the development of these theories and how managers and their organizations have changed their behavior as a result.

TRAP
  • classical theories = scientific + administrative only. A choice that puts behavioral management inside the classical group is wrong.
  • management science theory came out during World War II — not in the 1960s. What came in the 1960s is open-systems and contingency theory (section 4).
  • efficiency ≠ effectiveness. The whole chapter is about the two together; do not mix them.
  • Any question that asks "which theory came first?" is answered by scientific management, and "the newest" is organizational environment theory. The choice that puts behavioral first is wrong.
  • The counts in this part: 5 theories in the figure · 3 forces (economic, political, cultural) · 4 possible sources of a solution (people, processes, technology, reporting relationships).

Job Specialization and the Division of Labor
Adam Smith's pin study — and its numbers are asked word for word
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
When each worker specializes in one or a few tasks instead of doing all of them, he becomes more skilled and faster — and the whole group produces far more.
🧠
A burger shop: one person toasts the bun, one fries the patty, one wraps it — much faster than one person making the whole order alone.
EXPLANATION — the background: why did the theory appear at all?

Modern management began to develop in the closing decades of the 19th century, after the Industrial Revolution had swept through Europe and America. In the new economic climate, managers of all types of organizations — political, educational, and economic — were trying to find better ways to satisfy customers' needs.

What exactly changed? The introduction of steam power and the development of sophisticated machinery and equipment changed how goods were produced, particularly in the weaving and clothing industries.

The result: crafts production — small workshops run by skilled workers who made hand-manufactured products — began to be replaced by large factories with sophisticated machines controlled by hundreds or even thousands of unskilled or semiskilled workers.

The book's example: raw cotton and wool, which families or whole villages used to spin into yarn by hand, were now shipped to factories where workers operated machines that spun and wove large quantities of yarn into cloth.

And the problem: the owners and managers of the new factories found themselves unprepared for the challenges. Moreover, many managers and supervisors were engineers who had only a technical orientation — so they were not ready for the social problems that occur when many people work together in a factory. So they started looking for new techniques, and soon they focused on raising the efficiency of the worker–task mix.

EXPLANATION — Adam Smith and the pin study

At the start, management theorists cared about one question: why were the new factories more efficient and more productive than the older crafts-style operations?

Adam Smith — a famous economist, and one of the first writers to investigate the advantages of producing goods and services in factories. He journeyed around England in the 1700s studying the effects of the Industrial Revolution — that is nearly 200 years before the management theorists. Learn this, because the trap turns it around.

In a study of factories that produced various pins or nails, Smith identified two manufacturing methods:

  1. The first was similar to crafts production: each worker was responsible for all 18 tasks involved in producing a pin.
  2. The second had each worker performing only one or a few of those 18 tasks.

The result — numbers that are asked word for word:

MethodWhat the worker doesOutput
Specializedone or a few of the 18 tasks 10 workers → 48,000 pins a day
Crafts-styleall 18 tasks only a few thousand

Smith's explanation: the workers who specialized became much more skilled at their specific tasks, and as a group they could produce faster. His conclusion: raising the level of job specialization improves efficiency and leads to higher organizational performance.

DEFINITION — learn it in English exactly as it is

job specialization — The process by which a division of labor occurs as different workers specialize in different tasks over time.

After Smith's observations, other managers and researchers began to investigate how to improve job specialization to increase performance, and they focused on how the manager should organize and control the work process to maximize the advantages of specialization and the division of labor. This is exactly where Taylor's work starts.

EXAM BOX

Nearly 200 years before, Adam Smith had been one of the first writers to investigate the advantages associated with producing goods and services in factories. In a study of factories that produced various pins or nails, Smith identified two different manufacturing methods. The first was similar to crafts-style production, in which each worker was responsible for all the 18 tasks involved in producing a pin. Smith concluded that increasing the level of job specialization—the process by which a division of labor occurs as different workers specialize in tasks—improves efficiency and leads to higher organizational performance.

TRAP
  • Adam Smith ≠ Taylor. Smith was an economist who observed the advantage of specialization in the 18th century. Taylor was a manufacturing manager who built a systematic scientific method at the turn of the 20th century. A choice that gives the pin study to Taylor is wrong.
  • The numbers get flipped: 18 tasks · 10 workers · 48,000 pins a day. Any choice that says 18 workers, or 10 tasks, or 48,000 a week, is wrong.
  • job specialization is the process, and division of labor is the splitting of the work itself — and the book ties them in one definition: "the process by which a division of labor occurs".
  • crafts production = small workshops + skilled workers + a hand-made product. A choice that describes it as large factories with unskilled workers describes the opposite.

F. W. Taylor and Scientific Management
Four principles in order — and the count itself is a question
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Taylor says: the best way to divide the work is decided by science — by timing, measuring and testing — not by a gut feeling or general experience.
🧠
A coach films a player, times every step, writes the perfect method on a sheet, then pays a bonus for beating it.
EXPLANATION — who Taylor was and what his logic is

Frederick W. Taylor (1856–1915) is best known for defining the techniques of scientific management. He was a manufacturing manager, and he later became a consultant who taught other managers how to apply his techniques.

DEFINITION — learn it in English exactly as it is

scientific management — The systematic study of relationships between people and tasks for the purpose of redesigning the work process to increase efficiency.

EXPLANATION — Taylor's logic and his main point

His logic: if the amount of time and effort each worker expends to produce a unit of output (a finished good or service) can be reduced by increasing specialization and the division of labor, the production process becomes more efficient.

And his main point — a sentence the exam loves: the way to create the most efficient division of labor could best be determined by scientific management techniques rather than by intuitive or informal, rule-of-thumb knowledge.

Based on his experiments and observations as a manufacturing manager in a variety of settings, he developed four principles to increase efficiency in the workplace.

EXPLANATION — the four principles in detail
#The principle, in the book's own wordsWhat it means, in short
1 Study the way workers perform their tasks, gather all the informal job knowledge that workers possess, and experiment with ways of improving how tasks are performed. Study and test. Watch how workers do their tasks, gather all the informal knowledge they hold, and experiment with ways of improving it.
2 Codify the new methods of performing tasks into written rules and standard operating procedures. Codify and write it down. Turn the new methods into written rules and standard operating procedures.
3 Carefully select workers who possess skills and abilities that match the needs of the task, and train them to perform the task according to the established rules and procedures. Select and train. Carefully pick the workers whose skills and abilities match what the task needs, and train them according to the established rules and procedures.
4 Establish a fair or acceptable level of performance for a task, and then develop a pay system that rewards performance above the acceptable level. Set a level and reward above it. Set a fair or acceptable level of performance, then build a pay system that rewards performance above that level.
DEFINITION — learn it in English exactly as it is

And here, inside Principle 1, comes the most famous tool in the whole section:

time-and-motion study — the careful timing and recording of the actions taken to perform a particular task.

EXPLANATION — the detail of each principle

Detail of Principle 1:

  • Once Taylor understood the existing method, he experimented to increase specialization and tried different ways of dividing and coordinating the tasks.
  • Usually this meant simplifying jobs and having each worker perform fewer, more routine tasks — as at a pin factory or on a car assembly line.
  • He also looked for ways to improve each worker's ability at the task — the book's examples: reducing the number of motions · changing the layout of the work area or the type of tools · experimenting with tools of different sizes.

Detail of Principle 2: once the best method for a task was determined, it had to be recorded so that the procedure could be taught to all workers performing the same task. This further standardized and simplified jobs — made them even more routine — and so efficiency could be increased throughout the whole organization.

Detail of Principle 3 — careful, there is a trap here: the worker had to understand the tasks and be thoroughly trained to perform them at the required level. A worker who could not be trained to this level was to be transferred to another job where he could reach the minimum required level of proficiency. Transferred, not fired.

Detail of Principle 4: Taylor wanted to give the worker an incentive to reveal the most efficient techniques for performing a task. He also wanted to encourage the worker to perform at a high level of efficiency. So he advocated that workers benefit from any gains in performance: they should be paid a bonus and receive some percentage of the performance gains achieved through the more efficient work process.

TRAP
  • The count is four principles — not five and not three. And five is Weber's principles of bureaucracy (section 2), while 14 is Fayol's. This is the most common mix-up in the whole chapter.
  • Principle 3: the worker who cannot be trained is transferred. A choice that says fired or dismissed is wrong.
  • time-and-motion study is a tool, not a theory — and it belongs to Principle 1. A choice that puts it under Principle 2 or 4 is wrong.
  • Written rules and SOPs = Principle 2. And the bonus and the percentage of the gains = Principle 4. Do not swap them.
  • The book says the best division of labor is determined by science and not by rule-of-thumb knowledge. A choice that says Taylor relied on intuition and general experience is the exact opposite of what he said.

Scientific Management in Practice
Selective use is the root of every problem — and the Franklin numbers get asked
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
Managers applied Taylor's principles selectively: they took the higher output and left out sharing the gains — so the result worked against the worker.
🧠
A diet done on half the recipe: you kept the "eat less" part and dropped the "eat protein" part — the result is exhaustion, not health.
EXPLANATION — the spread, then the problems

By 1910, Taylor's system had become nationally known, and in many cases it was practiced faithfully and fully. However, managers in many organizations chose to implement the principles selectively — and this is what caused the problems:

  • Some managers obtained increases in performance, but rather than sharing the performance gains with workers through bonuses as Taylor had advocated, they simply increased the amount of work each worker was expected to do.
  • Workers found that as their performance increased, the manager required them to do more work for the same pay.
  • They also learned that performance increases often meant fewer jobs and a greater threat of layoffs, because fewer workers were needed.
  • And the specialized, simplified jobs were often monotonous and repetitive, so many workers became dissatisfied with their jobs.

The result in the book's words: scientific management brought many workers more hardship than gain and a distrust of managers who did not seem to care about workers' well-being.

And the workers' reaction: they resisted the new techniques, and at times they even withheld their job knowledge from managers to protect their jobs and pay. It is not hard for workers to conceal the true potential efficiency of a work system. The book's example: experienced machine operators can slow their machines in undetectable ways — by adjusting the tension in the belts or by misaligning the gears.

EXPLANATION — how organizations answered: mechanization

Unable to inspire workers to accept the new techniques, some organizations increased the mechanization of the work process:

  • Henry Ford introduced moving conveyor belts in his factory, because he realized that when a conveyor belt controls the pace of work (instead of workers setting their own pace), workers can be pushed to perform at higher levels — levels they may have thought were beyond their reach.
  • Charlie Chaplin captured this in one of the opening scenes of his famous movie Modern Times (1936): a new factory employee fighting to keep up with the machine-imposed pace — and losing the battle.
  • Ford also used the principles of scientific management to identify the tasks each worker should perform on the production line, and so to determine the most effective division of labor for the needs of a mechanized production system.

From a performance point of view, the book says the combination of two management practices makes sense:

  1. achieving the right worker–task specialization;
  2. linking people and tasks by the speed of the production line.

Together they produce huge cost savings and dramatic output increases in large, organized work settings.

The book's number example — Franklin Motor Company, the year 1908: managers applied the principles of scientific management and redesigned the work process, and the output of cars increased from 100 cars a month to 45 cars a day; workers' wages, however, increased by only 90%.
Notice the gap: output jumped from monthly to daily, and pay went up only 90% — this is exactly the book's example of gains that are not shared fairly.

  • The movie Modern Times is from 1936 and is by Charlie Chaplin — it is about the speed of the machine. Do not mix it with Cheaper by the Dozen (2003), which is about the Gilbreths.

The Gilbreths
Three aims and four causes of fatigue — and the counts get asked
THE IDEA IN ONE LINE
The Gilbreths broke every task into small movements on film, frame by frame, and then put it back together in a more efficient way.
🧠
Like replaying a goal in slow motion to see every step — except here you do it to delete the steps you do not need.
EXPLANATION — who they were and what their aims were

Frank Gilbreth (1868–1924) and Lillian Gilbreth (1878–1972) were two prominent followers of Taylor. They refined Taylor's analysis of work movements and made many contributions to time-and-motion study.

Their three aims — learn the count and the order:

  1. Analyze every individual action necessary to perform a particular task and break it into each of its component actions.
  2. Find better ways to perform each component action.
  3. Reorganize each of the component actions so that the action as a whole could be performed more efficiently — at less cost in time and effort.

Their method: they often filmed a worker performing a task and then separated the task actions frame by frame into their component movements. The goal was to maximize the efficiency of each task so that the gains add up across tasks into enormous savings of time and effort.

The light side: their attempts were captured — at times quite humorously — in the movie Cheaper by the Dozen, and a new version of it appeared in 2003. It depicts how the Gilbreths — with their 12 children — tried to live their own lives according to these efficiency principles and apply them to daily actions such as shaving, cooking, and even raising a family. The book's photo caption on the movie shows how "efficient families" like the Gilbreths use formal family courts to solve problems, such as assigning chores to different family members.

EXPLANATION — the study of fatigue, and the effect of the theory on practice

Eventually the Gilbreths became increasingly interested in the study of fatigue. They studied how the physical characteristics of the workplace contribute to job stress that often leads to fatigue and thus to poor performance.

They isolated four factors that result in worker fatigue — learn them as they are:

Their pioneering studies paved the way for new advances in management theory.

The summary of how scientific management affected practice — the book closes the section with it:

  • In workshops and factories, the work of the Gilbreths, Taylor and many others had a major effect on the practice of management.
  • In comparison with the old crafts system, jobs in the new system became more repetitive, boring, and monotonous, and workers became increasingly dissatisfied.
  • And often the management of work settings became a game between workers and managers: the manager tried to initiate work practices that raise performance, and the worker tried to hide the true potential efficiency of the work setting to protect his own interest.
  • And three lines from the book's Summary and Review — the book's own summary of these two objectives: the search for efficiency started with the study of how managers could improve person–task relationships; the concept of job specialization and division of labor remains the basis for the design of work settings in modern organizations; and new developments such as lean production and total quality management are often viewed as advances on the early scientific management principles developed by Taylor and the Gilbreths.

  • They had 12 children — not 10 and not 6. And the movie is Cheaper by the Dozen — and dozen = 12, which helps you remember.

  • The study of fatigue here is a study of physical conditions — it is still inside scientific management. Do not mix it with the Hawthorne studies (section 3), which are about behavior and human relations.

1. A researcher visits two pin factories. In the first, each worker performs all 18 tasks needed to make a pin. In the second, each worker performs only one or a few of those tasks, and 10 workers together produce 48,000 pins a day. The principle this study demonstrates is best described as:
  1. Job specialization and the division of labor
  2. Bureaucracy
  3. Time-and-motion study
  4. Administrative management
Answer

A. This is Adam Smith's study word for word, and it is the book's example of job specialization — "the process by which a division of labor occurs". The trap is in C: the word "study" pulls you to time-and-motion study, but that is Taylor's tool (Principle 1) and it measures time and movement, not a comparison between two manufacturing methods. And B and D are order traps: bureaucracy and administrative management are in section 2 of the chapter and have nothing to do with the pin factory.

2. Which of the following is NOT one of Taylor's four principles of scientific management?
  1. Codify the new methods of performing tasks into written rules and standard operating procedures
  2. Establish a fair or acceptable level of performance, and develop a pay system that rewards performance above that level
  3. Arrange positions hierarchically so employees know whom to report to and who reports to them
  4. Study the way workers perform their tasks and experiment with ways of improving how tasks are performed
Answer

C. Arranging positions in a hierarchy is Weber's Principle 4 from the five principles of bureaucracy — from section 2, not from Taylor's principles. The trap here is double: the question uses NOT, and A, B and D are all correct Taylor principles (2, 4 and 1 in order) so that you rush and pick one of them. Remember the counts: Taylor 4 · Weber 5 · Fayol 14.

3. According to Figure 2.1, "The Evolution of Management Theory," which statement is correct?
  1. Behavioral management theory begins earliest on the timeline
  2. Scientific management theory begins earliest, and organizational environment theory begins last
  3. All five theories begin in the same decade
  4. Management science theory is the only one shown before 1900
Answer

B. The figure is a timeline from 1890 to 2030, and the bars are ordered from the bottom up by their starting date: scientific management (the oldest, around 1890) → administrative → behavioral → management science → organizational environment (the newest, around the 1960s, and the only bar that reaches the end of the timeline at 2030). 4. A factory manager carefully selects a worker whose skills match a task and trains her according to the established rules. After full training, she still cannot reach the required level. According to Taylor's third principle, she should be:
  1. Dismissed from the organization
  2. Paid a bonus to stay motivated
  3. Transferred to a job where she can reach the minimum required level of proficiency
  4. Filmed frame by frame to analyze her component actions
Answer

C. The book is explicit: "Workers who could not be trained to this level were to be transferred to a job where they were able to reach the minimum required level of proficiency" — transferred, not fired. The trap in A is the most common one on this principle.   4. Henri Fayol
Answer

C. The first part is the Gilbreths' three aims word for word (break it down → find a better way → reorganize), and the second part is the four causes of fatigue they isolated. The trap in A: Smith is in the same section, but his work was an economic observation on a pin factory — he filmed nothing and studied no fatigue. And B and D are name traps from section 2 — Weber for bureaucracy and Fayol for the 14 principles, and neither of them studies movement. And careful: the Gilbreths refined the time-and-motion study; they did not invent it.
